# Wittgenstein (later) — Map

## Initial phrases

Philosophical Investigations:

"For a large class of cases — though not for all — in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language." (§43)

"I shall also call the whole, consisting of language and the actions into which it is woven, the 'language-game'." (§7)

"To imagine a language means to imagine a form of life." (§19)

"The speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life." (§23)

"When I obey a rule, I do not choose. I obey the rule blindly." (§219)

"If a lion could talk, we could not understand him." (p. 223)

"What has to be accepted, the given, is — so one could say — forms of life." (p. 226)

"Philosophy may in no way interfere with the actual use of language; it can in the end only describe it." (§124)

The private language argument (§243-315): language cannot be a private affair — there is no language whose words "refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations."

Family resemblances (§66-67): "Don't say: 'There must be something common, or they would not be called "games"' — but look and see whether there is anything common to all."

## Forms of life — discussion summary

"Forms of life" is famously underdeveloped. Wittgenstein uses it only a handful of times and never defines it. Beyond the initial phrases above, key occurrences include:

"Can only those hope who can talk? Only those who have mastered the use of a language. That is to say, the phenomena of hope are modes of this complicated form of life." (p. 174)

"What we are supplying are really remarks on the natural history of human beings." (§415)

And from On Certainty: "I want to regard man here as an animal; as a primitive being to which one grants instinct but not ratiocination. Language did not emerge from some kind of ratiocination." (§475)

### Not anthropocentric

The concept is not anthropocentric, though Wittgenstein never develops it beyond the human case. The evidence: the lion passage doesn't say lions lack a form of life — it presupposes they have one, so different from ours that language couldn't bridge it. He places humans within animal life ("man as animal"), and the phrase itself — "forms of life," plural, unqualified — never specifies "human forms of life."

All his examples stay human: builders, shopkeepers, children learning words. The concept is open; his practice stays with us. He left the door open without walking through it. We walk through it — language types include animal communication, plant signalling, cellular processes. If forms of life aren't exclusively human, then language-games aren't exclusively human either. His own framework allows it.

### Form of life as form of activity, interaction

In §23, "The speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life" — that "or" equates form of life with activity. Not life as biological category, not life as biography, but life as what is being done. The living that is going on.

And §7: language is woven into action. The language-game is not the words — it's the words plus the doing. The form of life is the form of the doing.

Read this way, "form of life" means: the pattern of interaction within which language has its place. The builders calling for slabs — the form of life is building, not being human. The shopkeeper counting apples — the form of life is exchanging, not being a shopkeeper.

This reads close to the principle's "what a language gives access to depends on what it relates to." The form of life is what the language relates to. Change the activity, change the relations, change what the language gives access to.

And it explains the lion: we can't understand the lion not because lions are biologically different, but because we don't share the lion's activity. The form of interaction is alien.

## Note: early vs later Wittgenstein on structure

The later stance on structure — depth grammar over surface grammar, structure follows activity — is in contrast to his earlier Tractatus, where structure is everything: names refer to objects, propositions picture states of affairs, language and world share the same logical form. A strict isomorphism.

Both are valid. The Tractatus describes how formal languages actually operate — structural mapping. The Investigations describes language-in-practice — structure follows activity. The same thinker, working within different languages (formal logic vs ordinary language), arrives at different but internally valid descriptions. Neither cancels the other.

For the principle's "language is relational": relational structure can take both forms. This needs further thought.

## Language, language-game, form of life

§7 defines the language-game as "the whole, consisting of language and the actions into which it is woven." §19 then says "to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life" — with no language-game in between.

Form of life holds both aspects: the form (pattern, structure, the static) and the life (activity, game, the dynamic). To imagine a language — the form — inevitably implies the activity. The static entails the dynamic. They are inseparable.

Language-game is not a middle step between language and form of life. It is the name for the dynamic aspect of what form of life already contains. Form of life is the whole. Language-game is that whole in motion.

Language (form) → inevitably → language-game (life/activity) → together = form of life.

For the principle: language is relational (the structure), but relation implies relating (the activity). You can't have a relation without the dynamic of relating. The static and the dynamic are inseparable — which is what "relational" already means.

## Private language

Wittgenstein's private language argument (§243-315) concludes that a language accessible only to one person is logically incoherent: language requires rules, rules require the possibility of checking correctness, checking requires community. "Whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'." (§258)

And the beetle in a box (§293): if everyone has a box with a "beetle" no one else can see, the word gets its meaning from its role in the language-game, not from what's in the box. "The thing in the box has no place in the language-game at all."

The argument addresses one reading of "private" — a self-contained system with private rules — and shows it to be incoherent. There is a different reading it does not address: the private experience of inhabiting any language. Not a sealed-off private language, but the subject's side of the relation — when a poem resonates, when a craftsperson feels the wood is right, when a thought is forming before it becomes articulate.

In the principle's terms: language is the medium through which a subject experiences reality. The language is shared, but the experience is the subject's. The private is not a separate language — it is the subject's experience within the language.

The beetle in the box points toward this. The thing in the box "has no place in the language-game" — but that doesn't mean it doesn't exist. It means the language-game can't reach it. In the principle's framing: the private experience is outside the reach of the shared language. Not nonexistent — unreachable from that language.

There is a parallel in quantum foundations worth noting: RQM's third-person problem asks how two observers comparing notes can establish shared facts when each has only their own relational perspective. The private language argument and the cross-perspective problem are analogous — both concern how what is private or relative becomes shared. In both cases, the answer involves shared practice and interaction rather than access to a private interior. The analogy is suggestive, not exact — the domains are different and the arguments operate differently.

## Family resemblances

"Consider for example the proceedings that we call 'games'. I mean board-games, card-games, ball-games, Olympic games, and so on. What is common to them all? — Don't say: 'There must be something common, or they would not be called "games"' — but look and see." (§66)

"I can think of no better expression to characterize these similarities than 'family resemblances'; for the various resemblances between members of a family: build, features, colour of eyes, gait, temperament, etc. etc. overlap and criss-cross in the same way." (§67)

No single thread runs through all instances. Instead, a network of overlapping similarities — some share this feature, others share that one, the resemblances criss-cross.

Applied to "language" itself: what do all languages have in common? Wittgenstein would say don't assume, look and see. The principle does claim a common layer — language is relational — but it also refuses to prescribe what languages must look like beyond that.

The structure of family resemblance is itself relational — a network of overlapping, criss-crossing connections with no centre. This looks like "languages are inter-relational." Not a hierarchy with one language at the top, but a web of overlapping relations. The family metaphor is already a relational metaphor.

## Mapping statement

Wittgenstein's meaning-as-use, his form of life, and his family resemblances each describe, in his own terms, what we recognise as language being relational. The language-game is the doing. The form of life is the pattern of interaction within which language has its place. Family resemblances are overlapping, criss-crossing connections with no centre — a web of relations, not a hierarchy.

We read these with a wide scope. His "forms of life," plural and unqualified, need not be limited to the human case. The builders' language relates to building, the shopkeeper's to exchanging — change the activity, change what the language gives access to. His framework allows this reading; the extension is ours.

His private language argument and our principle address the same question from different angles: how meaning is grounded in shared practice rather than private experience — while the subject's experience within the shared language remains real, even if the language-game cannot reach it.
