# Relational

Principle statement: **Language is relational**

110 positions pre-categorised under this concept, across 16 categories.

---

## 1. Ordinary language / pragmatics / pragmatism

1.1. Wittgenstein (later) | Language is a collection of "language-games" embedded in "forms of life"; the meaning of a word is its use; there is no single essence of language, only family resemblances; a private language is incoherent.

1.2. J.L. Austin | Language is not primarily a vehicle for making true or false statements; many utterances are performatives that do things; all utterances have locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary dimensions; language is fundamentally a form of action.

1.3. John Searle | Speech acts are the basic units of linguistic communication, governed by constitutive rules; language is the fundamental social institution that makes all other institutions possible, resting on collective intentionality and the ability to create "status functions."

1.4. H.P. Grice | Meaning is fundamentally a matter of speaker intention: a speaker means something by intending to produce an effect in the hearer by means of the hearer's recognition of that intention; communication is governed by a Cooperative Principle and conversational maxims.

1.5. William James | The meaning of a concept consists in its practical consequences -- what difference it would make to experience; language is instrumental: ideas and words are tools for navigating experience, not mirrors of reality.

1.6. John Dewey | Language is the "tool of tools" -- the instrument that makes all other instruments possible; it is fundamentally social, arising from cooperative activity; language transforms biological impulses into intelligent, reflective inquiry.

1.7. Richard Rorty | Language is not a medium of representation; there is no "way the world is" independent of our descriptions; languages are contingent tools to be evaluated by usefulness, not accuracy in mirroring nature.

1.8. Robert Brandom | Language is fundamentally a practice of giving and asking for reasons; meaning is determined by inferential role -- what follows from an expression, what it follows from, and what is incompatible with it; representation and reference are derivative achievements.

1.9. Dell Hymes | What speakers know is not just grammatical competence but communicative competence -- knowledge of when, how, and to whom to speak; language is fundamentally an activity embedded in social life; the proper unit of analysis is the speech event, not the decontextualized sentence.

## 2. Phenomenology / hermeneutics

2.1. Martin Heidegger | Language is "the house of Being" -- the medium in which Being reveals and conceals itself; it is not we who speak language but language that speaks through us; poetry is the primordial form of language in which Being comes to presence.

2.2. Hans-Georg Gadamer | Language is the universal medium of understanding; all understanding is linguistic; understanding occurs as a "fusion of horizons" (Horizontverschmelzung) that is always linguistically mediated; "Being that can be understood is language."

2.3. Maurice Merleau-Ponty | Language is a bodily gesture that expresses meaning; it is a creative, corporeal activity through which thought comes into being; speaking is a "motor intentionality" rooted in the lived body's expressive relation to the world.

2.4. Paul Ricoeur | The fundamental unit of meaning is the sentence, not the word; a living metaphor is a semantic innovation that creates new meaning by bringing together previously unrelated domains; narrative is the linguistic form through which human temporal experience becomes intelligible.

2.5. Emmanuel Levinas | Language is fundamentally ethical: it originates in the face-to-face encounter with the Other; the primordial linguistic act is the Saying (le Dire) -- the exposure and vulnerability of the subject before the Other, prior to any thematization.

---

## 3. Externalism / holism

3.1. W.V.O. Quine | There is no sharp boundary between analytic and synthetic truths; meaning is not a determinate property of individual sentences (holism); translation between languages is radically indeterminate; language is a social art acquired on publicly available behavioral evidence.

3.2. Donald Davidson | To know the meaning of a sentence is to know its truth-conditions; there is no such thing as a language if a language is a set of conventions shared by a community; interpretation requires triangulation between two speakers and a shared world.

3.3. Saul Kripke | Names are rigid designators fixed by an initial "baptism" and transmitted through causal-historical chains; meaning is partly determined by the world, not solely by what is in the head.

3.4. Hilary Putnam | "Meanings ain't in the head"; the meaning of a natural kind term is determined partly by the external environment and partly by the social division of linguistic labor.

3.5. David Lewis | A language is an abstract function from sentences to meanings; what makes a particular language the language of a community is a convention of truthfulness and trust -- meaning is grounded in rational coordination.

## 4. Formal / mathematical

4.1. Gottlob Frege | Natural language is logically defective; a logically perfect language (Begriffsschrift) would make inference transparent. The meaning of an expression has two dimensions: its reference (Bedeutung) and its sense (Sinn, the mode of presentation); senses are objective, mind-independent abstract entities.

4.2. Bertrand Russell | Ordinary language is systematically misleading; its grammatical form diverges from its logical form; philosophy's task is to reveal the true logical form underlying surface grammar.

4.3. Ludwig Wittgenstein (early) | Language is a picture of reality; a proposition is a logical picture of a state of affairs; language and world share a common logical form, and what lies beyond the limits of language must be passed over in silence.

4.4. Rudolf Carnap | Language is a formal calculus; the choice of a linguistic framework is a practical matter (the principle of tolerance); meaning is determined by the syntactic and semantic rules of the framework; metaphysical statements are literally meaningless.

4.5. Panini | Language is a rule-governed generative system; all valid linguistic expressions can be derived from a finite set of roots, suffixes, and approximately 4,000 rules; language is a structured totality amenable to complete formal description.

4.6. Al-Farabi | Language is conventional, but logic is universal; grammar is particular to each language and culturally specific, while logic is the "grammar of reason" applicable to all peoples; language has a social-political dimension: different levels of discourse are appropriate for different audiences.

4.7. The Modistae (Thomas of Erfurt et al.) | Grammar is universal and mirrors the structure of reality: modes of signifying in language correspond to modes of understanding in the mind, which correspond to modes of being in reality.

4.8. Alan Turing | A language, at its most reduced, is a set of symbols plus rules for their transformation; a sufficiently powerful symbol system can simulate any other (universality); language is not fundamentally about specific symbols but about the structural relationships and transformation rules those symbols participate in.

4.9. Alonzo Church | Language is fundamentally about binding and substitution (function abstraction and application); the Church-Turing thesis implies that radically different surface structures can express identical underlying capacities; language is, in some sense, independent of its specific notation.

4.10. Alfred Tarski | Defining truth for a language requires a metalanguage; no sufficiently powerful language can fully describe its own semantics within itself; language is inherently stratified, hierarchical, and self-referential but with limits.

4.11. Kurt Godel | Any consistent formal system powerful enough to express basic arithmetic contains true statements that cannot be proved within it; meaning exceeds any formalization of it; no single formal language can be both consistent and complete for a sufficiently rich domain.

4.12. David Hilbert | Mathematics is a formal game played with symbols according to rules; mathematical language does not refer to anything outside itself; meaning is constituted by the rules of the formal system; language is pure syntax.

4.13. L.E.J. Brouwer | Mathematics is a languageless activity of the mind; language is a secondary, imperfect record of mental construction; language can never fully capture mathematical intuition.

## 5. Language and reality

5.1. Bhartrhari | Brahman (ultimate reality) is essentially linguistic (Shabda-Brahman); all cognition is permeated by language; the sentence, not the word, is the primary unit of meaning, grasped in a flash of intuition (pratibha); speech has three levels: Pashyanti (visionary/undifferentiated), Madhyama (mental/internal), and Vaikhari (external/articulated).

5.2. Kukai | The sounds and letters of Sanskrit are the body of the Dharmakaya (the cosmic Buddha); the universe is the "sermon" that the Dharmakaya is always preaching; language, properly understood and ritually enacted, is a direct means of realizing enlightenment.

5.3. Dogen | Language is not opposed to reality -- it is itself a form of practice and realization; his concept of dotoku ("expression") holds that authentic expression and authentic realization are inseparable; he transforms language rather than rejecting it, enacting the dissolution of ordinary categories through his writing.

5.4. Ibn Arabi | The cosmos is the self-disclosure (tajalli) of God through His Names; everything that exists is a "word" (kalima) of God; language is a barzakh (isthmus) between meaning and sound, between the intelligible and the sensible; poetic and symbolic expressions convey truths that discursive prose cannot.

5.5. Advaita Vedanta (Shankara) | Language belongs to the realm of maya/avidya and cannot describe Brahman, the ultimate non-dual reality (neti neti); yet Vedic language has an indispensable indicative function, pointing toward Brahman by progressively negating false attributions; language is simultaneously indispensable and ultimately inadequate.

5.6. Early Buddhism (Pali Canon) | Language is conventional (sammuti), useful for communication but unable to capture reality as it truly is; language is rooted in papanca (conceptual proliferation/reification), carving the flux of experience into apparently stable entities, leading to attachment; the Buddha used language skillfully (upaya) to guide beings toward liberation.

5.7. Nagarjuna | Language and conceptual thought (prapanca) are structured by binary oppositions that do not correspond to how things are; all phenomena are shunya (empty) of inherent existence; the ultimate truth is the cessation of all conceptual proliferation; yet "without relying on convention, the ultimate cannot be taught."

5.8. Jain Tradition (Kundakunda, Siddhasena, et al.) | Reality is infinitely complex (anekanta); language can only capture it partially, from one perspective (naya) at a time; the syadvada (sevenfold predication) acknowledges that any statement is conditionally true and that there is an aspect of reality (avaktavya) that language cannot capture at all.

5.9. Laozi | "The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao; the name that can be named is not the constant name"; naming is the beginning of differentiation, which takes us away from the primordial unity of the Dao; yet the text uses language paradoxically to point beyond language.

5.10. Zhuangzi | Language is a trap that ensnares us in fixed perspectives and arbitrary distinctions; all language-based judgments are perspectival and relative; "words exist because of meaning -- once you have gotten the meaning, you can forget the words"; his "goblet words" (zhiyan) model language that empties and refills, never staying fixed.

5.11. Chinese Buddhist Adaptations (Sengzhao, Jizang, Chan/Zen) | Language cannot capture reality because reality transcends all dualistic categories, yet language has a skillful, provisional function; Chan's founding principle: "not relying on words and letters" yet vast literary production.

5.12. Wang Yangming | Genuine understanding cannot be adequately expressed in words alone -- it must be realized in practice; the locus of truth is liangzhi (innate moral knowing), which is pre-linguistic and directly intuitive; words without action are empty.

5.13. Nishida Kitaro | Reality in its most fundamental form is "pure experience" (junsui keiken) -- experience prior to the subject-object division and prior to linguistic conceptualization; language cannot objectify "the place of absolute nothingness" (zettai mu no basho) because objectification would make it into a being rather than the nothingness grounding all beings.

5.14. Rumi | "Silence is the language of God; everything else is a poor translation"; language is inadequate to express mystical experience yet the Sufi tradition constantly uses indirect, allusive, poetic language to approach what direct language cannot.

## 6. Systems / information

6.1. Claude Shannon | Language can be modeled as a stochastic process transmitting information through a noisy channel; the fundamental problem is reproducing a message at one point that was selected at another; this framework is deliberately agnostic about meaning: "the semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem."

6.2. Gregory Bateson | Information is "a difference which makes a difference"; communication occurs at every level of physical organization; "mind" is an immanent feature of any system with sufficient complexity of feedback and self-correction, including ecosystems and societies.

## 7. Cross-boundary / foundational

7.1. Plato | Language is an instrument (organon) for distinguishing and communicating the natures of things, but it is an imperfect tool, and knowledge of the Forms must ultimately go beyond language.

7.2. Aristotle | Spoken words are symbols (symbola) of affections in the soul, and the word-thing relation is established by convention; language is essentially connected to rationality and political life -- man is a zoon logon echon.

7.3. Thomas Aquinas | Words signify things through the mediation of concepts; crucially, certain terms can be predicated of radically different domains analogically -- with a meaning that is proportionally related rather than univocal or equivocal.

7.4. John Locke | Words are sensible marks of ideas in the mind of the speaker; language is the great instrument and common tie of society, but words signify only the speaker's own ideas, making communication always imperfect.

7.5. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Language is not merely a vehicle for communicating pre-existing thoughts but an instrument of reason itself; languages embody the collective intellectual labor of peoples and contain more wisdom than any individual speaker possesses.

7.6. Friedrich Nietzsche | Language is a mobile army of metaphors, metonymies, and anthropomorphisms; what we call "truth" is a worn-out metaphor that has forgotten it is a metaphor; grammar creates the illusion of substances and agents.

7.7. Jacques Derrida | Language is a system of differance -- meaning is never fully present but is constituted by differences and perpetually deferred; there is no "transcendental signified" outside the play of language; the properties traditionally attributed to writing (absence, iterability, displacement) belong to all language.

7.8. Karl Buhler | Language has three simultaneous functions: expression (speaker's inner state), appeal (directing the hearer), and representation (referring to states of affairs); the linguistic sign is a triadic Organon connecting speaker, hearer, and world.

7.9. Jurgen Habermas | Language is fundamentally oriented toward reaching understanding (Verstandigung); every speech act raises validity claims (truth, rightness, sincerity, comprehensibility) and anticipates a situation of ideal discourse; communicative action is the fundamental form of social interaction.

7.10. Wilfrid Sellars | Language is the medium of the "space of reasons" -- the normative domain of justification and inference; the "myth of the given" is rejected: all awareness is a linguistic affair; to be a sentient being in the epistemic sense is to be a player in the language game of giving and asking for reasons.

7.11. Zhu Xi | Language is adequate to express li (principle/pattern) that structures all things; the investigation of things (gewu) is carried out through linguistic activities (reading, discussion, commentary); there is a basic confidence in language's capacity to convey truth.

## 8. Sign / semiotic / structuralist

8.1. The Stoics (Zeno, Chrysippus) | Language is a three-part structure: the signifier (semainon, physical sound), the signified (semainomenon/lekton, an incorporeal "sayable"), and the external object (tynchanon); the lekton subsists as an objective incorporeal entity.

8.2. Charles Sanders Peirce | All thought is in signs; a sign stands for something (its object) to someone (the interpretant) in some respect; meaning is an ongoing process of semiosis -- each interpretant is itself a sign, creating an infinite chain; language is irreducibly social, processual, and triadic.

8.3. Ferdinand de Saussure | Language (langue) is a system of differences without positive terms; a sign unites a signifier and signified arbitrarily; meaning arises from oppositions within the system, not from any sign in isolation.

8.4. Roman Jakobson | Language has six functions (referential, emotive, conative, phatic, metalingual, poetic) corresponding to six elements of communication; the poetic function foregrounds the message itself.

8.5. Patanjali | A word (shabda) is something grasped through sounds but not identical with them -- the sphota is an intelligible, invariant linguistic unit manifested by sounds but not constituted by them; the word as universal type is eternal and natural.

8.6. Dignaga and Dharmakirti | Language operates exclusively through apoha (exclusion); a word denotes its object by excluding everything that is not that object; language never touches reality as it is in itself -- it operates in the realm of conceptual construction (vikalpa).

8.7. M.A.K. Halliday | Language is a social semiotic -- a resource for making meaning in social contexts; it simultaneously performs three metafunctions: ideational (construing experience), interpersonal (enacting social relationships), and textual (organizing discourse); grammar is functional, shaped by what language is used to do.

8.8. Charles W. Morris | Semiotics is divided into syntactics, semantics, and pragmatics; language is a special case of a sign system; a sign directs behavior toward a goal in a manner similar to the response the object itself would produce; language is a system of behavioral dispositions organized into three dimensions.

8.9. Umberto Eco | Semiotics is the study of everything that can be used to lie; a sign is anything that can stand for something else; meaning is culturally produced and negotiated through an open "encyclopedia" of accumulated knowledge, not a fixed code.

8.10. Jesper Hoffmeyer | Semiosis is a fundamental property of all living systems, not limited to human language; every living cell engages in sign processes; human language is a species-specific elaboration of a universal biological semiosis.

8.11. Kalevi Kull | Semiosis is coextensive with life; the sign relation is the minimal defining feature of living processes; each organism inhabits its own species-specific sign-world (Umwelt).

8.12. Terrence Deacon | Human language is unique because it is symbolic in the Peircean sense -- based on symbols whose reference is determined by their relations to other symbols; the transition to symbolic reference was the critical threshold in language evolution, requiring co-evolution of brain, language, and social behavior.

8.13. Claude Levi-Strauss | Language is the paradigm case of a cultural system analyzable by structural methods; cultural phenomena are organized like languages -- as systems of differences and binary oppositions; the unconscious structures of language provide the model for understanding the unconscious structures of the mind.

8.14. Thomas Sebeok | Semiosis (sign use) is coextensive with life; wherever there is life, there is semiosis; "language" is the specifically human form of a much more general phenomenon.

8.15. Marcello Barbieri | The organic code concept captures what is genuinely novel about biological information; codes involve arbitrary mappings between two independent worlds; the genetic code is the first true "code," and language is built upon this more general coding capacity.

## 9. Symbol systems / aesthetics

9.1. Ernst Cassirer | Language is one of several "symbolic forms" (alongside myth, art, science) through which humans constitute their world; humans are "animal symbolicum"; language is a formative activity that shapes experience into an intelligible world.

9.2. Nelson Goodman | "Languages" of art are genuine symbol systems varying along multiple structural dimensions (density vs. differentiation, notationality, repleteness); language is not a single kind of thing but a family of symbol systems.

9.3. Susanne Langer | There are two fundamentally different kinds of symbolic expression: discursive symbolism (linear, propositional, translatable) and presentational symbolism (presenting wholes that cannot be broken into independently meaningful units); any adequate theory of language must account for non-discursive meaning.

---

## 10. Inner speech / mental language

10.1. Augustine of Hippo | Language is a system of signs (signa) -- a sign causes something beyond its sensory impression to come to mind -- but signs cannot teach; the "inner word" (verbum interius) is a pre-linguistic mental content clothed in a particular language.

10.2. William of Ockham | General terms are merely signs that stand for many individual things; there is a natural "mental language" (oratio mentalis) composed of concepts that are natural signs of things, and conventional spoken language is a kind of translation of it.

10.3. Ibn Sina / Avicenna | Language is a conventional sign-system that serves as the vehicle for thought; thought has a structure (logic) independent of language; the distinction between essence (mahiyya) and existence (wujud) has linguistic implications -- language expresses essences, but existence is grasped through immediate pre-linguistic awareness.

10.4. Ash'ari Kalam (Kalam al-Nafsi) | God's speech exists on two levels: eternal inner speech (kalam nafsi), an uncreated attribute co-eternal with the divine essence, and temporal expressed speech (kalam lafzi), the actual Arabic words; there is a level of "speech" or "meaning" that is pre-linguistic and exists prior to articulation.

---

## 11. Convention vs nature

11.1. Democritus | Language is conventional (thesei), not natural; names are assigned to things by human imposition, not because of any intrinsic connection between word and thing.

11.2. Epicureans (Epicurus) | Language arises naturally from involuntary responses to things and feelings and is then refined by convention; meaning derives from sensory experience (prolepsis).

11.3. Mimamsa (Jaimini, Kumarila Bhatta, Prabhakara) | Language is eternal and uncreated (nitya); the relation between word and meaning is natural and inherent (autpattika); shabda (verbal testimony) is an independent, irreducible means of valid knowledge (pramana); the primary function of Vedic language is injunctive -- to command action.

11.4. Nyaya (Gautama, Vatsyayana, Udayana) | The word-meaning relation is conventional (samketa); shabda is a valid pramana only when from a reliable person (apta); language works through the mechanisms of shakti (expressive capacity), yogyata (semantic compatibility), akanksha (syntactic expectancy), and sannidhi (contiguity).

11.5. Confucius (Kongzi) | The rectification of names (zhengming) is the foundation of good governance; when names do not correspond to actualities, social and political chaos follows; a name carries a normative standard -- language is constitutive of social reality and moral obligation.

11.6. Xunzi | Names are conventional (yue), established by agreement, not by nature; but once conventions are established, there are objective standards for correct use; the purpose of names is to distinguish similarities and differences.

11.7. Mozi and the Later Mohists | Language should be evaluated by clear, pragmatic criteria of correctness and utility; the Later Mohists analyzed types of names, conditions for correct predication, and language paradoxes; the "three standards" (san biao) evaluate claims by precedent, evidence, and practical consequences.

## 12. Language-thought constitution

12.1. Condillac | Language originates from natural cries and gestures and develops into articulate speech; crucially, the development of arbitrary signs transforms cognition itself -- language is constitutive of higher thought, not merely expressive of it.

12.2. Johann Gottfried Herder | Language arises naturally from human reflective awareness (Besonnenheit); it is inseparable from thought, and each language constitutes a unique worldview.

12.3. Wilhelm von Humboldt | Language is not a work (ergon) but an activity (energeia) -- the continually repeated labor of the mind to make articulated sound serve thought; each language embodies a particular worldview (Weltansicht) and is the outer appearance of the spirit of a people.

12.4. Johann Georg Hamann | Reason is language; there is no "pure" reason apart from language; language is a constitutive and irreducibly historical, embodied, and sensuous dimension of all human understanding.

12.5. Edward Sapir | Language is a "purely human and non-instinctive method of communicating ideas, emotions, and desires by means of a system of voluntarily produced symbols"; it is both a window into the mind and a cultural institution with its own historical momentum that shapes thought and perception.

12.6. Benjamin Lee Whorf | The categories and structures of a language influence (or determine) the cognitive categories available to its speakers; grammar organizes experience into fundamentally different patterns in different languages; language is a world-organizing system.

## 13. Cognitive/embodied

13.1. George Lakoff | Language is embodied cognition; meaning arises from embodied human experience -- bodily interaction with the physical and social environment; abstract concepts are systematically understood through conceptual metaphors grounded in bodily experience; there is no sharp boundary between language and general cognition.

13.2. Ronald Langacker | Language is a structured inventory of conventional symbolic units pairing phonological form with semantic structure (conceptualization); grammar is itself meaningful, fully reducible to assemblies of symbolic units; meaning is identified with conceptualization -- dynamic cognitive processing.

13.3. Michael Tomasello | Language is a set of conventionalized communicative practices learned through joint attention, intention-reading, and pattern-finding; there is no innate Universal Grammar; language is fundamentally a social-cooperative activity arising from shared intentionality.

13.4. Joan Bybee | Language is a complex adaptive system whose structure emerges from use; grammar is a cognitive organization of experience with language shaped by frequency, entrenchment, and categorization; language structure is emergent, not given.

13.5. Embodied/Grounded Cognition (Barsalou, Glenberg, Pulvermuller) | Language comprehension involves the reactivation of sensorimotor neural circuits; meaning is grounded in modal (perceptual, motor, affective) simulations; language is deeply integrated with the body's sensorimotor systems.

13.6. Predictive Processing Accounts (Friston, Clark, Kuperberg) | Language processing is a special case of the brain's general predictive inference; comprehension involves generating predictions and updating on prediction errors; language is a domain of hierarchical Bayesian inference.

---

## 14. Developmental/behavioral

14.1. Lev Vygotsky | Language is a psychological tool -- the most important mediating tool -- that transforms cognitive processes; thinking becomes verbal and speech becomes intellectual; language is first interpsychological (between people) and then intrapsychological (internalized for thought); higher mental functions are mediated by linguistic signs.

14.2. Jean Piaget | Language is dependent on and derivative of more general cognitive development; cognitive structures develop through sensorimotor interaction prior to language; language is an expression of the broader symbolic function; thought creates language, not vice versa.

14.3. B.F. Skinner | Language (verbal behavior) is operant behavior controlled by its consequences and discriminative stimuli; verbal behavior is classified by its functional relations to controlling variables (mands, tacts, echoics, intraverbals, autoclitics); language is a repertoire of stimulus-response relations shaped by reinforcement.

---

## 15. Nativist/computational

15.1. Noam Chomsky | Language is a biological organ -- a species-specific computational faculty of the mind/brain (I-language); all humans share a Universal Grammar (UG); in Minimalist work, language is characterized as a recursive computational procedure (Merge); language is primarily a system of thought, not communication.

15.2. Jerry Fodor | Thought occurs in a language of thought (Mentalese) -- an innate mental representational system; natural language is a code for expressing thoughts formulated in Mentalese; it is not the medium of thought.

15.3. Steven Pinker | Language is a human instinct -- a biological adaptation shaped by natural selection for communication of propositional structures; it is a complex, specialized skill that develops spontaneously; thought is largely independent of language.

15.4. Language as Complex Adaptive System (Beckner et al.) | Language is a complex adaptive system that emerges from the interactions of multiple agents across multiple timescales; language structure is emergent -- not specified by genes or Universal Grammar but arising from self-organization of communicative interactions.

---

## 16. Arts/defamiliarization

16.1. Maxine Sheets-Johnstone | Movement is the most primordial form of meaning-making, prior to language; kinesthetic experience is a primary source of concepts; if anything, language is derivative of movement, not the other way around.

16.2. Adorno | Musical materials carry historical and social sediment; tonality is not a neutral system but an ideology; language (including musical language) is never innocent of its social context; a form can "say" something about reality by enacting or refusing structural possibilities.

16.3. Viktor Shklovsky | The purpose of literary art is defamiliarization (ostranenie) -- making the familiar strange; everyday language renders the world invisible through automatization; art uses language against its own grain to renew perception.
