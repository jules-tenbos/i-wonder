# Ricoeur — Map

## Initial phrases

"The fundamental unit of meaning is the sentence, not the word." — meaning lives in the relation between terms, not in terms alone.

The living metaphor — a semantic innovation that creates new meaning by bringing together previously unrelated domains.

Narrative — the linguistic form through which human temporal experience becomes intelligible.

Distanciation — a text takes on a life beyond the author's intention. Once expressed, meaning is no longer owned by the one who expressed it.

The world of the text — a text projects a world the reader can inhabit.

Oneself as Another (Soi-même comme un autre) — identity is constituted through narrative, and always involves the other.

---

## Mapping statement

Ricoeur insists that the sentence, not the word, is the fundamental unit of meaning. A word alone doesn't mean; meaning arises from the relating of terms. This is a relational observation in his own terms — meaning is constituted by the relation, not by the parts in isolation.

His living metaphor — bringing together previously unrelated domains to create new meaning — describes a mechanism for inter-relational within language. A metaphor connects two domains that had no prior link, and something new emerges from the connection. This parallels Lakoff's conceptual metaphor (bodily experience mapped onto abstract domains) but from a hermeneutic rather than cognitive angle. Two descriptions of the same mechanism.

Expression, once made, is cast off as an entity of its own — a text, a work of art, a theory. It is no longer the author's; the author is only accessible through it as intermediary. What was expressed now has its own relational life, available for interaction independently. Dewey arrives at the same observation from the pragmatist side: "Once we have words and essences, their meanings can become independent of their original objects." Two traditions, same structural observation — expression gains independence from its origin.

His "world of the text" — a text projects a world the reader can inhabit — describes how an expression, once cast off, creates its own reach. The reader enters a world constituted by the text's relational structure. Different readers, different interactions with the same text, different worlds inhabited.

In his oneself as another, no self without the relation to another. Identity is constituted through narrative, and narrative always involves the other. The relating constitutes the related — from within his hermeneutic framework, in his own terms.
