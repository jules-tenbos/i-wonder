# Experience

Principle statement: **Language is the medium through which a subject experiences reality**

20 positions pre-categorised under this concept, across 4 categories.

---

## 1. Language constitutes the subject's experience

1.1. Martin Heidegger | Language is "the house of Being" -- the medium in which Being reveals and conceals itself; it is not we who speak language but language that speaks through us; poetry is the primordial form of language in which Being comes to presence.

1.2. Hans-Georg Gadamer | Language is the universal medium of understanding; all understanding is linguistic; understanding occurs as a "fusion of horizons" (Horizontverschmelzung) that is always linguistically mediated; "Being that can be understood is language."

1.3. Ernst Cassirer | Language is one of several "symbolic forms" (alongside myth, art, science) through which humans constitute their world; humans are "animal symbolicum"; language is a formative activity that shapes experience into an intelligible world.

1.4. Edward Sapir | Language is a "purely human and non-instinctive method of communicating ideas, emotions, and desires by means of a system of voluntarily produced symbols"; it is both a window into the mind and a cultural institution with its own historical momentum that shapes thought and perception.

1.5. Benjamin Lee Whorf | The categories and structures of a language influence (or determine) the cognitive categories available to its speakers; grammar organizes experience into fundamentally different patterns in different languages; language is a world-organizing system.

1.6. Bhartrhari | Brahman (ultimate reality) is essentially linguistic (Shabda-Brahman); all cognition is permeated by language; the sentence, not the word, is the primary unit of meaning, grasped in a flash of intuition (pratibha); speech has three levels: Pashyanti (visionary/undifferentiated), Madhyama (mental/internal), and Vaikhari (external/articulated).

## 2. The embodied/cognitive subject

2.1. Maurice Merleau-Ponty | Language is a bodily gesture that expresses meaning; it is a creative, corporeal activity through which thought comes into being; speaking is a "motor intentionality" rooted in the lived body's expressive relation to the world.

2.2. George Lakoff | Language is embodied cognition; meaning arises from embodied human experience -- bodily interaction with the physical and social environment; abstract concepts are systematically understood through conceptual metaphors grounded in bodily experience; there is no sharp boundary between language and general cognition.

2.3. Embodied/Grounded Cognition (Barsalou, Glenberg, Pulvermuller) | Language comprehension involves the reactivation of sensorimotor neural circuits; meaning is grounded in modal (perceptual, motor, affective) simulations; language is deeply integrated with the body's sensorimotor systems.

2.4. Predictive Processing Accounts (Friston, Clark, Kuperberg) | Language processing is a special case of the brain's general predictive inference; comprehension involves generating predictions and updating on prediction errors; language is a domain of hierarchical Bayesian inference.

2.5. Lev Vygotsky | Language is a psychological tool -- the most important mediating tool -- that transforms cognitive processes; thinking becomes verbal and speech becomes intellectual; language is first interpsychological (between people) and then intrapsychological (internalized for thought); higher mental functions are mediated by linguistic signs.

2.6. Jean Piaget | Language is dependent on and derivative of more general cognitive development; cognitive structures develop through sensorimotor interaction prior to language; language is an expression of the broader symbolic function; thought creates language, not vice versa.

---

## 3. Experience exceeds language

3.1. Early Buddhism (Pali Canon) | Language is conventional (sammuti), useful for communication but unable to capture reality as it truly is; language is rooted in papanca (conceptual proliferation/reification), carving the flux of experience into apparently stable entities, leading to attachment; the Buddha used language skillfully (upaya) to guide beings toward liberation.

3.2. Chinese Buddhist Adaptations (Sengzhao, Jizang, Chan/Zen) | Language cannot capture reality because reality transcends all dualistic categories, yet language has a skillful, provisional function; Chan's founding principle: "not relying on words and letters" yet vast literary production.

3.3. Nishida Kitaro | Reality in its most fundamental form is "pure experience" (junsui keiken) -- experience prior to the subject-object division and prior to linguistic conceptualization; language cannot objectify "the place of absolute nothingness" (zettai mu no basho) because objectification would make it into a being rather than the nothingness grounding all beings.

3.4. Rumi | "Silence is the language of God; everything else is a poor translation"; language is inadequate to express mystical experience yet the Sufi tradition constantly uses indirect, allusive, poetic language to approach what direct language cannot.

---

## 4. Language as lived practice/realization

4.1. Kukai | The sounds and letters of Sanskrit are the body of the Dharmakaya (the cosmic Buddha); the universe is the "sermon" that the Dharmakaya is always preaching; language, properly understood and ritually enacted, is a direct means of realizing enlightenment.

4.2. Dogen | Language is not opposed to reality -- it is itself a form of practice and realization; his concept of dotoku ("expression") holds that authentic expression and authentic realization are inseparable; he transforms language rather than rejecting it, enacting the dissolution of ordinary categories through his writing.

4.3. William James | The meaning of a concept consists in its practical consequences -- what difference it would make to experience; language is instrumental: ideas and words are tools for navigating experience, not mirrors of reality.

4.4. Paul Ricoeur | The fundamental unit of meaning is the sentence, not the word; a living metaphor is a semantic innovation that creates new meaning by bringing together previously unrelated domains; narrative is the linguistic form through which human temporal experience becomes intelligible.
