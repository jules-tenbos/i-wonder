# Knowledge

Principle statement: **Language is where subjects share knowledge about reality**

Knowledge = shared experience. Two blocks: how language enables sharing of experience between subjects (knowledge), and how language relates to what is real (reality).

75 positions pre-categorised under this concept, across 8 categories.

---

## 1. Language as instrument of reason

1.1. Gottlob Frege | Natural language is logically defective; a logically perfect language (Begriffsschrift) would make inference transparent. The meaning of an expression has two dimensions: its reference (Bedeutung) and its sense (Sinn, the mode of presentation); senses are objective, mind-independent abstract entities.

1.2. Bertrand Russell | Ordinary language is systematically misleading; its grammatical form diverges from its logical form; philosophy's task is to reveal the true logical form underlying surface grammar.

1.3. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Language is not merely a vehicle for communicating pre-existing thoughts but an instrument of reason itself; languages embody the collective intellectual labor of peoples and contain more wisdom than any individual speaker possesses.

1.4. Al-Farabi | Language is conventional, but logic is universal; grammar is particular to each language and culturally specific, while logic is the "grammar of reason" applicable to all peoples; language has a social-political dimension: different levels of discourse are appropriate for different audiences.

1.5. Ibn Sina / Avicenna | Language is a conventional sign-system that serves as the vehicle for thought; thought has a structure (logic) independent of language; the distinction between essence (mahiyya) and existence (wujud) has linguistic implications -- language expresses essences, but existence is grasped through immediate pre-linguistic awareness.

1.6. Kurt Godel | Any consistent formal system powerful enough to express basic arithmetic contains true statements that cannot be proved within it; meaning exceeds any formalization of it; no single formal language can be both consistent and complete for a sufficiently rich domain.

## 2. Language as social/communicative practice

2.1. Aristotle | Spoken words are symbols (symbola) of affections in the soul, and the word-thing relation is established by convention; language is essentially connected to rationality and political life -- man is a zoon logon echon.

2.2. John Searle | Speech acts are the basic units of linguistic communication, governed by constitutive rules; language is the fundamental social institution that makes all other institutions possible, resting on collective intentionality and the ability to create "status functions."

2.3. H.P. Grice | Meaning is fundamentally a matter of speaker intention: a speaker means something by intending to produce an effect in the hearer by means of the hearer's recognition of that intention; communication is governed by a Cooperative Principle and conversational maxims.

2.4. Jurgen Habermas | Language is fundamentally oriented toward reaching understanding (Verstandigung); every speech act raises validity claims (truth, rightness, sincerity, comprehensibility) and anticipates a situation of ideal discourse; communicative action is the fundamental form of social interaction.

2.5. Dell Hymes | What speakers know is not just grammatical competence but communicative competence -- knowledge of when, how, and to whom to speak; language is fundamentally an activity embedded in social life; the proper unit of analysis is the speech event, not the decontextualized sentence.

2.6. Michael Tomasello | Language is a set of conventionalized communicative practices learned through joint attention, intention-reading, and pattern-finding; there is no innate Universal Grammar; language is fundamentally a social-cooperative activity arising from shared intentionality.

2.7. David Lewis | A language is an abstract function from sentences to meanings; what makes a particular language the language of a community is a convention of truthfulness and trust -- meaning is grounded in rational coordination.

2.8. John Dewey | Language is the "tool of tools" -- the instrument that makes all other instruments possible; it is fundamentally social, arising from cooperative activity; language transforms biological impulses into intelligent, reflective inquiry.

2.9. Karl Buhler | Language has three simultaneous functions: expression (speaker's inner state), appeal (directing the hearer), and representation (referring to states of affairs); the linguistic sign is a triadic Organon connecting speaker, hearer, and world.

2.10. Confucius (Kongzi) | The rectification of names (zhengming) is the foundation of good governance; when names do not correspond to actualities, social and political chaos follows; a name carries a normative standard -- language is constitutive of social reality and moral obligation.

2.11. Xunzi | Names are conventional (yue), established by agreement, not by nature; but once conventions are established, there are objective standards for correct use; the purpose of names is to distinguish similarities and differences.

2.12. Mozi and the Later Mohists | Language should be evaluated by clear, pragmatic criteria of correctness and utility; the Later Mohists analyzed types of names, conditions for correct predication, and language paradoxes; the "three standards" (san biao) evaluate claims by precedent, evidence, and practical consequences.

## 3. Language as sign system

3.1. Charles Sanders Peirce | All thought is in signs; a sign stands for something (its object) to someone (the interpretant) in some respect; meaning is an ongoing process of semiosis -- each interpretant is itself a sign, creating an infinite chain; language is irreducibly social, processual, and triadic.

3.2. Augustine of Hippo | Language is a system of signs (signa) -- a sign causes something beyond its sensory impression to come to mind -- but signs cannot teach; the "inner word" (verbum interius) is a pre-linguistic mental content clothed in a particular language.

3.3. Thomas Aquinas | Words signify things through the mediation of concepts; crucially, certain terms can be predicated of radically different domains analogically -- with a meaning that is proportionally related rather than univocal or equivocal.

3.4. William of Ockham | General terms are merely signs that stand for many individual things; there is a natural "mental language" (oratio mentalis) composed of concepts that are natural signs of things, and conventional spoken language is a kind of translation of it.

3.5. John Locke | Words are sensible marks of ideas in the mind of the speaker; language is the great instrument and common tie of society, but words signify only the speaker's own ideas, making communication always imperfect.

3.6. Umberto Eco | Semiotics is the study of everything that can be used to lie; a sign is anything that can stand for something else; meaning is culturally produced and negotiated through an open "encyclopedia" of accumulated knowledge, not a fixed code.

3.7. M.A.K. Halliday | Language is a social semiotic -- a resource for making meaning in social contexts; it simultaneously performs three metafunctions: ideational (construing experience), interpersonal (enacting social relationships), and textual (organizing discourse); grammar is functional, shaped by what language is used to do.

3.8. Claude Levi-Strauss | Language is the paradigm case of a cultural system analyzable by structural methods; cultural phenomena are organized like languages -- as systems of differences and binary oppositions; the unconscious structures of language provide the model for understanding the unconscious structures of the mind.

3.9. Claude Shannon | Language can be modeled as a stochastic process transmitting information through a noisy channel; the fundamental problem is reproducing a message at one point that was selected at another; this framework is deliberately agnostic about meaning: "the semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem."

3.10. Ronald Langacker | Language is a structured inventory of conventional symbolic units pairing phonological form with semantic structure (conceptualization); grammar is itself meaningful, fully reducible to assemblies of symbolic units; meaning is identified with conceptualization -- dynamic cognitive processing.

## 4. Language and the constitution of knowledge

4.1. Ludwig Wittgenstein (later) | Language is a collection of "language-games" embedded in "forms of life"; the meaning of a word is its use; there is no single essence of language, only family resemblances; a private language is incoherent.

4.2. W.V.O. Quine | There is no sharp boundary between analytic and synthetic truths; meaning is not a determinate property of individual sentences (holism); translation between languages is radically indeterminate; language is a social art acquired on publicly available behavioral evidence.

4.3. Donald Davidson | To know the meaning of a sentence is to know its truth-conditions; there is no such thing as a language if a language is a set of conventions shared by a community; interpretation requires triangulation between two speakers and a shared world.

4.4. Saul Kripke | Names are rigid designators fixed by an initial "baptism" and transmitted through causal-historical chains; meaning is partly determined by the world, not solely by what is in the head.

4.5. Hilary Putnam | "Meanings ain't in the head"; the meaning of a natural kind term is determined partly by the external environment and partly by the social division of linguistic labor.

4.6. Edward Sapir | Language is a "purely human and non-instinctive method of communicating ideas, emotions, and desires by means of a system of voluntarily produced symbols"; it is both a window into the mind and a cultural institution with its own historical momentum that shapes thought and perception.

4.7. Benjamin Lee Whorf | The categories and structures of a language influence (or determine) the cognitive categories available to its speakers; grammar organizes experience into fundamentally different patterns in different languages; language is a world-organizing system.

4.8. Wilfrid Sellars | Language is the medium of the "space of reasons" -- the normative domain of justification and inference; the "myth of the given" is rejected: all awareness is a linguistic affair; to be a sentient being in the epistemic sense is to be a player in the language game of giving and asking for reasons.

4.9. Ernst Cassirer | Language is one of several "symbolic forms" (alongside myth, art, science) through which humans constitute their world; humans are "animal symbolicum"; language is a formative activity that shapes experience into an intelligible world.

4.10. Martin Heidegger | Language is "the house of Being" -- the medium in which Being reveals and conceals itself; it is not we who speak language but language that speaks through us; poetry is the primordial form of language in which Being comes to presence.

4.11. Hans-Georg Gadamer | Language is the universal medium of understanding; all understanding is linguistic; understanding occurs as a "fusion of horizons" (Horizontverschmelzung) that is always linguistically mediated; "Being that can be understood is language."

4.12. Jacques Derrida | Language is a system of differance -- meaning is never fully present but is constituted by differences and perpetually deferred; there is no "transcendental signified" outside the play of language; the properties traditionally attributed to writing (absence, iterability, displacement) belong to all language.

4.13. Maurice Merleau-Ponty | Language is a bodily gesture that expresses meaning; it is a creative, corporeal activity through which thought comes into being; speaking is a "motor intentionality" rooted in the lived body's expressive relation to the world.

4.14. Paul Ricoeur | The fundamental unit of meaning is the sentence, not the word; a living metaphor is a semantic innovation that creates new meaning by bringing together previously unrelated domains; narrative is the linguistic form through which human temporal experience becomes intelligible.

4.15. Robert Brandom | Language is fundamentally a practice of giving and asking for reasons; meaning is determined by inferential role -- what follows from an expression, what it follows from, and what is incompatible with it; representation and reference are derivative achievements.

4.16. Richard Rorty | Language is not a medium of representation; there is no "way the world is" independent of our descriptions; languages are contingent tools to be evaluated by usefulness, not accuracy in mirroring nature.

4.17. Viktor Shklovsky | The purpose of literary art is defamiliarization (ostranenie) -- making the familiar strange; everyday language renders the world invisible through automatization; art uses language against its own grain to renew perception.

4.18. Epicureans (Epicurus) | Language arises naturally from involuntary responses to things and feelings and is then refined by convention; meaning derives from sensory experience (prolepsis).

4.19. Lev Vygotsky | Language is a psychological tool -- the most important mediating tool -- that transforms cognitive processes; thinking becomes verbal and speech becomes intellectual; language is first interpsychological (between people) and then intrapsychological (internalized for thought); higher mental functions are mediated by linguistic signs.

4.20. Jean Piaget | Language is dependent on and derivative of more general cognitive development; cognitive structures develop through sensorimotor interaction prior to language; language is an expression of the broader symbolic function; thought creates language, not vice versa.

4.21. Steven Pinker | Language is a human instinct -- a biological adaptation shaped by natural selection for communication of propositional structures; it is a complex, specialized skill that develops spontaneously; thought is largely independent of language.

## 5. Language as epistemic authority

5.1. Mimamsa (Jaimini, Kumarila Bhatta, Prabhakara) | Language is eternal and uncreated (nitya); the relation between word and meaning is natural and inherent (autpattika); shabda (verbal testimony) is an independent, irreducible means of valid knowledge (pramana); the primary function of Vedic language is injunctive -- to command action.

5.2. Nyaya (Gautama, Vatsyayana, Udayana) | The word-meaning relation is conventional (samketa); shabda is a valid pramana only when from a reliable person (apta); language works through the mechanisms of shakti (expressive capacity), yogyata (semantic compatibility), akanksha (syntactic expectancy), and sannidhi (contiguity).

5.3. Ash'ari Kalam (Kalam al-Nafsi) | God's speech exists on two levels: eternal inner speech (kalam nafsi), an uncreated attribute co-eternal with the divine essence, and temporal expressed speech (kalam lafzi), the actual Arabic words; there is a level of "speech" or "meaning" that is pre-linguistic and exists prior to articulation.

5.4. Ibn Arabi | The cosmos is the self-disclosure (tajalli) of God through His Names; everything that exists is a "word" (kalima) of God; language is a barzakh (isthmus) between meaning and sound, between the intelligible and the sensible; poetic and symbolic expressions convey truths that discursive prose cannot.

## 6. Language mirrors reality

6.1. Heraclitus | Logos is not merely human speech but is identical with the rational structure of the cosmos itself; human language participates in this cosmic reason but most people fail to understand it.

6.2. Parmenides | Language and thought are constrained by Being; one cannot speak or think what is not; genuine language tracks the structure of what exists.

6.3. The Modistae (Thomas of Erfurt et al.) | Grammar is universal and mirrors the structure of reality: modes of signifying in language correspond to modes of understanding in the mind, which correspond to modes of being in reality.

6.4. Ludwig Wittgenstein (early) | Language is a picture of reality; a proposition is a logical picture of a state of affairs; language and world share a common logical form, and what lies beyond the limits of language must be passed over in silence.

6.5. Zhu Xi | Language is adequate to express li (principle/pattern) that structures all things; the investigation of things (gewu) is carried out through linguistic activities (reading, discussion, commentary); there is a basic confidence in language's capacity to convey truth.

6.6. Bhartrhari | Brahman (ultimate reality) is essentially linguistic (Shabda-Brahman); all cognition is permeated by language; the sentence, not the word, is the primary unit of meaning, grasped in a flash of intuition (pratibha); speech has three levels: Pashyanti (visionary/undifferentiated), Madhyama (mental/internal), and Vaikhari (external/articulated).

6.7. Kukai | The sounds and letters of Sanskrit are the body of the Dharmakaya (the cosmic Buddha); the universe is the "sermon" that the Dharmakaya is always preaching; language, properly understood and ritually enacted, is a direct means of realizing enlightenment.

6.8. Dogen | Language is not opposed to reality -- it is itself a form of practice and realization; his concept of dotoku ("expression") holds that authentic expression and authentic realization are inseparable; he transforms language rather than rejecting it, enacting the dissolution of ordinary categories through his writing.

## 7. Reality exceeds language

7.1. Plato | Language is an instrument (organon) for distinguishing and communicating the natures of things, but it is an imperfect tool, and knowledge of the Forms must ultimately go beyond language.

7.2. Nagarjuna | Language and conceptual thought (prapanca) are structured by binary oppositions that do not correspond to how things are; all phenomena are shunya (empty) of inherent existence; the ultimate truth is the cessation of all conceptual proliferation; yet "without relying on convention, the ultimate cannot be taught."

7.3. Advaita Vedanta (Shankara) | Language belongs to the realm of maya/avidya and cannot describe Brahman, the ultimate non-dual reality (neti neti); yet Vedic language has an indispensable indicative function, pointing toward Brahman by progressively negating false attributions; language is simultaneously indispensable and ultimately inadequate.

7.4. Early Buddhism (Pali Canon) | Language is conventional (sammuti), useful for communication but unable to capture reality as it truly is; language is rooted in papanca (conceptual proliferation/reification), carving the flux of experience into apparently stable entities, leading to attachment; the Buddha used language skillfully (upaya) to guide beings toward liberation.

7.5. Dignaga and Dharmakirti | Language operates exclusively through apoha (exclusion); a word denotes its object by excluding everything that is not that object; language never touches reality as it is in itself -- it operates in the realm of conceptual construction (vikalpa).

7.6. Jain Tradition (Kundakunda, Siddhasena, et al.) | Reality is infinitely complex (anekanta); language can only capture it partially, from one perspective (naya) at a time; the syadvada (sevenfold predication) acknowledges that any statement is conditionally true and that there is an aspect of reality (avaktavya) that language cannot capture at all.

7.7. Laozi | "The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao; the name that can be named is not the constant name"; naming is the beginning of differentiation, which takes us away from the primordial unity of the Dao; yet the text uses language paradoxically to point beyond language.

7.8. Zhuangzi | Language is a trap that ensnares us in fixed perspectives and arbitrary distinctions; all language-based judgments are perspectival and relative; "words exist because of meaning -- once you have gotten the meaning, you can forget the words"; his "goblet words" (zhiyan) model language that empties and refills, never staying fixed.

7.9. Chinese Buddhist Adaptations (Sengzhao, Jizang, Chan/Zen) | Language cannot capture reality because reality transcends all dualistic categories, yet language has a skillful, provisional function; Chan's founding principle: "not relying on words and letters" yet vast literary production.

7.10. Wang Yangming | Genuine understanding cannot be adequately expressed in words alone -- it must be realized in practice; the locus of truth is liangzhi (innate moral knowing), which is pre-linguistic and directly intuitive; words without action are empty.

7.11. Nishida Kitaro | Reality in its most fundamental form is "pure experience" (junsui keiken) -- experience prior to the subject-object division and prior to linguistic conceptualization; language cannot objectify "the place of absolute nothingness" (zettai mu no basho) because objectification would make it into a being rather than the nothingness grounding all beings.

7.12. Rumi | "Silence is the language of God; everything else is a poor translation"; language is inadequate to express mystical experience yet the Sufi tradition constantly uses indirect, allusive, poetic language to approach what direct language cannot.

---

## 8. Subject encounters Other

8.1. Emmanuel Levinas | Language is fundamentally ethical: it originates in the face-to-face encounter with the Other; the primordial linguistic act is the Saying (le Dire) -- the exposure and vulnerability of the subject before the Other, prior to any thematization.

8.2. John Locke | Words are sensible marks of ideas in the mind of the speaker; language is the great instrument and common tie of society, but words signify only the speaker's own ideas, making communication always imperfect.
