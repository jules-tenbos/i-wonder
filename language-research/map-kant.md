# Kant — Map

## Initial phrases

"Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind." (Critique of Pure Reason)

The Copernican turn: objects conform to the mind's conditions of experience, not the mind to objects. We do not passively receive reality — we actively structure it through categories of understanding (causality, substance, unity, etc.) and forms of intuition (space and time).

"The thing in itself" (Ding an sich / noumenon) — reality as it is independent of the mind's structuring — is real but unknowable. We only ever encounter phenomena: reality as structured by our cognitive apparatus.

"All our knowledge begins with experience, but it does not follow that it all arises from experience." (Critique of Pure Reason, Introduction)

---

## Note on scope

Kant is not primarily a thinker about language. His concern is the conditions of knowledge and experience. He enters our map not as a position on language but as the turn that changed what the question is about: from how knowledge mirrors an independent order to how knowledge is structured by the knower.

---

## Mapping statement

Kant shifts the question: rather than asking how knowledge mirrors reality, he asks how the knower structures what counts as reality. Objects conform to the mind's conditions of experience, not the other way around. This is the turn that makes our kind of question possible — asking how language structures experience rather than how language mirrors what is already there.

His "thing in itself" — reality independent of the mind's structuring, real but unknowable — is consistent with our view that each language's reach is only accessible through that language. What lies beyond the reach is not denied; it is simply not accessible from within. From within our thinking, his thing in itself is reality in potential: what sits beyond all reach, postulated but structurally unknowable, because knowing always happens through a language.

Where we differ: Kant's categories are fixed and universal — the same for all rational beings. For us, the structuring happens through language, and languages vary. His single set of categories becomes, in our thinking, many languages, each structuring differently, each with its own reach. His framework is one language's account of what he takes to be the only possible structuring. Ours recognises many.
