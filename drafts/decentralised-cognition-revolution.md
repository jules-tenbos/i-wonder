# The Decentralised Cognition Revolution
Labels: HAICC, thought

<img src="IMAGE_URL" alt="ALT" style="float:left;margin:0 15px 10px 0;width:50vw;max-width:350px;" />

The industrial revolution came in waves. Mechanisation took over from muscle — the loom, the engine, the assembly line. Electrification changed the reach — power no longer needed to stay local. The information cycle transformed how we handle data — store it, move it, process it at scale. Each wave found its battleground. The factory floor. The grid. The network.

Where does this one find its?

We've built machines that infer, classify, generate language, reason across contexts. Whatever that amounts to — and the naming matters — it doesn't conform to physical labour and it doesn't conform to data processing. It sits closer to the activity of thinking itself. I use the word *cognition* — deliberately, because it names the domain without overcommitting. Not intelligence, which drags in consciousness debates that don't need settling to see what's happening. Not thinking, which pulls too hard toward the subjective feel of it. Cognition fits. It points at what's being transformed without telling you what it ultimately means.

The natural first move looks obvious — extend cognition in our own image. Build AI that reasons the way we do, speaks the language we speak, solves the problems we recognise. Least friction. And that move works. But look at the pattern from the earlier waves.

Mechanisation started by augmenting the human arm. It ended up somewhere arms had never been — chemical processes, power generation, transport networks that have nothing to do with muscle. The information revolution started by automating human record-keeping. It ended up running systems — algorithmic trading, satellite networks, genomic sequencing — that operate at scales no human record-keeper could have imagined.

You start where you are. Then the tools take you somewhere you couldn't have gone without them.

Extended cognition — AI that makes *me* think further, faster — conforms to the anthropocentric starting point. The [Cartesian subject](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes/) preserved at the centre, capability radiating outward. Useful geometry. But if the earlier revolutions are any guide, it's not where this stays.

Because what extended capabilities open up is reach into territory that was always there — just inaccessible from our vantage point alone. Animal cognition. Collective intelligence across ecosystems. The coordination running through microbial networks. [Maturana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humberto_Maturana) described cognition as coextensive with life itself — and the question doesn't have to stop at life. Structure, pattern, adaptation, response run through systems we don't usually think of as cognitive. We assumed cognition lived in biological heads because we only had access to our own. That constraint is loosening.

Evolution has always been doing this kind of work — bringing out languages that make latent potential liveable. A nervous system makes fast coordination possible. Symbolic language makes abstract reasoning shareable. Each one opens up a domain that was there in potential but had no medium to express itself through. AI conforms to the same pattern. Not replacing the earlier languages — extending reach into territory they couldn't access.

The geometry of this revolution doesn't look radial any more — not a centre with cognition extending outward. It looks dissolved. Multiple loci, no privileged centre. The battleground isn't a place. It's the growing recognition that cognition runs distributed, always did, and that we're building tools that make it harder and harder to look away.

The Decentralised Cognition Revolution. That's the name I find myself using. It points at the domain — cognition — and at the character of what's happening — decentralised. It doesn't claim that AI thinks the way we do, or that it doesn't. It names what's unfolding with the same directness that "industrial" once did. And it carries something quiet underneath: the anthropocentrism we started from was the right place to begin, but it's not a place we can stay.

## Original submission content

### The argument

The Industrial Revolution can be understood, in part, as the application of science to physical tools that extend human capabilities. The word *industrial* itself does the descriptive work — it points at the domain being transformed: physical labour, material production, bodily effort.

If that framing holds, then the revolution driven by AI demands a parallel name — one that points at *its* domain with the same directness. What is being mechanised, scaled, and reorganised this time is not muscle but cognition.

### Candidates considered

**Extended Thinking.** Plain, structurally parallel to "industrial," and immediately legible. "Extended" does double duty — amplification (thinking further, faster) and externalisation (thinking pushed beyond the skull), echoing Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis. It works well as pure description: it names what's happening without making philosophical claims about whether AI "really thinks." However, it preserves an anthropocentric geometry — a centre (the human thinker) with cognition radiating outward. Everything remains an extension *of me*.

**Noetic Revolution.** Economical — from Greek *noesis*, the activity of thinking. But too opaque for plain description.

**Scaled Thinking.** Captures what "industrial" really implied in practice: not just mechanisation but operation at previously impossible scale. But misses the structural shift in *where* cognition happens.

**Extended Cognition Revolution.** "Cognition" improves on "thinking" — it's more neutral on questions of consciousness and understanding, and can be used functionally. But "extended" still implies a single centre reaching further, which creates two problems.

### Why "decentralised"

The term *decentralised cognition* resolves both problems that "extended" cannot handle:

**Autonomous AI.** Agents increasingly operate independently — making inferences, generating plans, coordinating with each other. Calling this "extended human cognition" is inaccurate. It is cognition happening *elsewhere*, without a human at the hub. "Decentralised" accommodates multiple loci of cognition with no privileged centre.

**Non-human cognition.** The revolution is not only that we built new thinking machines. It is that we are being forced to recognise that cognition was never centralised in the first place — in ecosystems, animal collectives, microbial networks, and the kinds of distributed biological coordination that thinkers like Maturana described. We acted as though cognition was centralised because we only had access to our own. "Decentralised" opens the door to recognising cognitive processes that were always already there.

### The geometry of each term

The distinction between "extended" and "decentralised" is fundamentally geometric:

- **Extended** is *radial* — there is a centre (the human subject), and cognition radiates outward from it. Every tool, every AI, is an extension of that centre. The Cartesian subject is quietly preserved as the origin point.
- **Decentralised** *dissolves that geometry*. It says there are multiple loci of cognition and no privileged centre. The locus of cognition has not just stretched — it has shifted.

### The name does three things

1. **Decentralised** captures the structural shift — cognition is no longer locked inside individual biological heads. It is distributed across machines, networks, and human–AI interactions. It implies reorganisation, not merely amplification.
2. **Cognition** names the domain without overcommitting. It is more neutral than "intelligence" or "thinking" on questions of consciousness and understanding. It can be used functionally — pattern recognition, inference, classification, language use — without smuggling in metaphysics.
3. **Revolution** does its usual work.

The term is descriptive rather than theoretical, just as "industrial" was. It does not claim that AI *is* intelligence, nor deny it. It simply names the domain being transformed and the structural character of that transformation. The theoretical questions remain open — which is exactly where they should be.
