I stumbled across the word anti-foundationalism and something in me said — that's me too.

Don't be taken aback by the "anti." It is always a clumsy word for what it means, and has no real place in my dictionary. The point isn't to reject foundations. Foundations are important. It is the *single* foundation that is the problem — the idea that one layer underwrites everything else, and that whoever doesn't stand on it has to be explained away.

I came across the term listening to [Carlo Rovelli](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-relational/) on Curt Jaimungal's Theory of Everything podcast — particularly [around the one-hour-fifteen mark](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r_fUPbBNmBw). Rovelli has a way of naming physics without leaving philosophy, and a way of doing philosophy that doesn't leave the physics. He was talking about relational quantum mechanics, and somewhere in the middle he said *anti-foundationalist* the way you might say *water is wet* — as if it were obvious. I paused the video. I didn't yet have the vocabulary for what I wanted to say about the world, but I knew I was on the side of whatever this was.

A quick search gave me the standard definition:

> Anti-foundationalism is a philosophical position that rejects the idea that there is a single, absolute foundation for knowledge or philosophy.

I subscribed to that right away, though not in a denying sense. I wasn't saying no foundation exists anywhere. I was saying that because we experience the world from the inside, as situated subjects, what we call knowledge is always a shared view among agents who overlap. There is no position from which to check in against an absolute. That didn't feel like a defeat. It felt like an end to a long, nervous habit — the habit of needing certainty before being allowed to think.

What anti-foundationalism rejects, the same sources tell me, is the idea that knowledge is certain; that it rests on pure reason or experience; that there are infallible basic beliefs; that some fundamental principle grounds everything else. I am happy with all that, though I prefer staying away from the word *beliefs* altogether. Beliefs slide easily into the territory of settled conviction, and I carry my positions as working hypotheses instead — good for the next exchange, not much more.

The move I care about isn't the rejection. It's the consequence. Once single foundations are off the table, what replaces them? I found an answer I liked in a handbook piece by Mark Bevir — written for *The Oxford Handbook of British Politics*, though the politics side of it wasn't what drew me. This paragraph was:

> The most obvious implications of anti-foundationalism are perhaps meaning holism and anti-representationalism. Given that we cannot have pure experiences, our concepts and propositions cannot refer to the world in splendid isolation. Concepts cannot directly represent objects in the world since our experiences of those objects must in part be ones that we construct using our prior theories. Hence anti-foundationalists conclude that concepts, meanings, and beliefs do not have a one to one correspondence with objects in the world, but rather form webs.

Webs. Not foundations, not pyramids, not a chain of reasons going back to something inarguable — webs. Threads crossing each other. Meaning held in the connections. You can tug on one part and feel the rest shift. You can also cut one strand and find the neighbouring ones still carrying the load.

That matched how I had always experienced understanding, without knowing how to name it. A thought lives in its relation to other thoughts. A word lives in its difference from other words. A person lives among other people, in a place, with a history — meaning constituted in the crossings, not built up from a single point of certainty. I found out later that some of this had been worked out already — by [Wittgenstein](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/) on language games, by [Saussure](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/saussure/) on differential signs, by [Rorty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rorty/) on philosophy as conversation. I just recognised the shape.

Anti-foundationalism was the word I took away. For what it opened up, I was still to find out.
