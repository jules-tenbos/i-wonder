The previous post explored how software languages form a tower — binary at the base, assembly above it, higher languages on top. Each level absorbs complexity into its own vocabulary. A compiler maps one language into another, transforming representation without losing what matters. The body has been doing this long before we built computers.

Consider vision. Light hits the retina and is transduced into electrical impulses — a first compilation. The retinal ganglion cells don't pass the image forward; they transform it, detecting edges, contrasts, motion. The signal reaches the visual cortex in layers — V1 extracts orientations, V2 handles contours, V4 processes colour, further areas recognise objects and faces. Each stage is a transformation: raw signal into pattern, pattern into structure, structure into meaning. By the time you see a face, the original photons are many compilations behind you.

The reverse direction works the same way. You decide to pick up a cup. That intention — expressed in the language of goals and plans — gets compiled into motor cortex activation, then into signals to specific muscle groups, then into the precise timing of muscle fibre contractions. From concept to movement: a decompilation, unpacking high-level meaning into the raw instruction set of the body.

Speech is where both directions meet. Speaking: a thought compiles through Broca's area into motor plans for the vocal tract — tongue, lips, larynx, breath — producing sound waves. Listening: sound waves hit the cochlea, are transduced into nerve impulses, compiled through Wernicke's area into phonemes, words, sentences, meaning. Two compilations, running in opposite directions, meeting in conversation. And the body shapes the expression — gesture, rhythm, posture participate in the meaning, just as Merleau-Ponty described.

The parallel with software compilation is structural, not metaphorical. In both cases: multiple languages, each suited to its level. In both: complexity absorbed at each stage, making the next level simpler to work with. In both: no level is more real than another — the binary is not more real than Python, the nerve impulse is not more real than the perception. And in both: the full power sits at the base, with higher levels adding expression, not capability. P5 applies.

But there is a difference. A software compiler is designed — its mappings are explicit, written down, deterministic. The body's compilations are evolved, plastic, context-dependent. The visual cortex reorganises after injury. A blind person's visual areas start processing touch and sound — cross-modal plasticity, the brain rewriting its own compiler. And mirror neurons add another layer: when you watch someone else act, your motor system runs a partial compilation of their movement. You understand the action by compiling it through your own body. Merleau-Ponty's intercorporeality, described decades before the neurons were found.

Assemblers and compilers aren't unique to software. The body was compiling long before we gave the process a name. Every sensation transduced, every movement planned, every word spoken — languages mapped into languages, complexity absorbed, meaning carried through. Languages all the way down, and all the way up.
