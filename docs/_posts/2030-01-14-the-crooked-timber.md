Petrograd, February 1917. In the first days of the Revolution, a seven-year-old boy saw a man dragged away by a mob, pale and struggling, to what the boy understood was his death. Isaiah Berlin said later that the sight left him with a lifelong horror of political violence. The family — Jewish, in the Riga timber trade — went back to Latvia and then, in 1921, to England. Most philosophies are argued into; Berlin's was witnessed first and argued afterwards. The whole of it is already in that street scene: what happens to actual human beings when somebody is certain enough.

England turned the refugee boy into the most establishment figure imaginable: St Paul's, two Oxford firsts, and at twenty-three a fellowship at All Souls — the first unconverted Jew ever elected. He became the great talker of his age, a man who published few books and spoke in torrents; much of his work was delivered as lectures and rescued into volumes by other hands. During the war his weekly dispatches from Washington, reporting American opinion to London, were read at the top of Whitehall and admired for their sharpness. He knew everyone, and everyone kept his letters.

Then, in the autumn of 1945, a posting to the British Embassy in Moscow put him briefly in Leningrad, and he climbed the stairs to Anna Akhmatova's bare apartment. They talked until morning — one night of conversation between the Oxford don and the great poet Stalin had silenced. Berlin called it the most consequential personal encounter of his life. It was consequential for her too, and terribly: he believed the visit contributed to Stalin's renewed persecution of her the following year. He had seen, at the closest possible range, what a single-truth society does to its poets — and what a night of free conversation costs inside one.

The philosophy he is remembered for reads like that memory made structural. Value pluralism: the great human goods — liberty, equality, justice, mercy, spontaneity, order — are genuinely good and genuinely in conflict. They cannot be ranked on one scale or converted into a common currency; no formula resolves their collisions. And this, he insisted, is not relativism. Values are objective — a member of one culture can understand, even feel the pull of, what another culture holds dear. There are simply many of them, and they do not fit together. What Berlin denied was the Enlightenment's triple confidence: that every genuine question has one true answer, that the true answers are all compatible, and that a method exists for finding them. Grant those three and a perfect society is attainable in principle — and then any price is worth paying to get there. The mob in the street is what the price looks like when it is being paid.

His most famous lecture, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958), carries the same warning into the word freedom itself. Negative liberty is the space where nobody interferes with you; positive liberty is being master of your own life. Both are real goods — and the second turns lethal the moment someone claims to know your true self better than you do, and liberates you from your own wishes. The party, the state, the philosopher-king: Berlin had watched that liberation from a Petrograd window.

Where does he stop? At the question he is always asked: if goods genuinely conflict and no formula ranks them, how do we ever choose? Judgment, he answered — practical wisdom, weighing without a scale — and declined to systematise further, a refusal his critics have pressed on ever since. But the refusal is the man. He worked in portraits, not systems, and he kept returning to one crooked sentence of Kant's: "Out of the crooked timber of humanity, no straight thing was ever made." The utopias of his century all promised to plane the timber straight, and he had seen up close what the planing costs. Permanent trade-offs, tragic choices, judgment without a formula — it is a graver, less consoling philosophy than the one true answer. It is also the only one in the room that never handed anyone to the mob.
