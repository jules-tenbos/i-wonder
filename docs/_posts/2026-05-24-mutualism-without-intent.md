You hear it in decentralisation conversations. Someone is making the case for distributed systems, peer-to-peer networks, local-first alternatives — and at some point the vocabulary shifts. Nature cooperates. Fungi share resources. Organisms form mutual arrangements. The biological pattern is invoked as evidence: this is how living systems work, and we should build the same way.

Something feels off, and it takes a moment to name. Not with the observation — the patterns are real. But with what the vocabulary is quietly doing.

## What mutualism observed

The resonances are genuine. [Mutualism](/positioning/subjects/m/mutualism/) in both its biological and political forms noticed real things. Decentralised arrangements emerging in living systems without a central planner producing them. Diversity sustaining itself — not despite variety, but through it. Self-organisation from within, not instruction from above. Cooperation as a widespread phenomenon, documented across species and human communities, not an idealisation but something observed in the field.

These observations hold. [Kropotkin](/positioning/persons/k/kropotkin/)'s *Mutual Aid* (1902) remains one of the most sustained evidence-based arguments for cooperation as a factor in evolution. When people in decentralisation discussions reach for this vocabulary, they are reaching for something with substance behind it. The pull is not arbitrary.

## What travels with the words

But the words carry furniture. A fungus extending its hyphae into a root system is not cooperating. It is doing what the interaction between its reality and its environment produces. What we observe as mutualism is a pattern that emerges without anyone deciding it should. The vocabulary of cooperation, altruism, mutual benefit — these are words for actors with intent, for subjects that choose. When they travel into contemporary discussions, they bring the 19th century framework along: nature sorts into cooperators and defectors, someone identifies the right structure, and the task becomes implementation.

The conversations end up reproducing what they argue against. Someone identifies the right decentralised structure. Someone has the authority to say this is how we should build. Others follow. The path is a command chain, even when the destination is named decentralised. The interventionist stance — even when aimed at distributed outcomes — is structurally centralising.

This is not a failure of anyone's reasoning. It is what happens when a vocabulary shaped by one intellectual moment gets carried forward without the assumptions being recognised. The 19th century framework breathed classical, deterministic, absolutist air — cause-and-effect chains, predictable outcomes, "we can identify the right arrangement." That air travels with the words.

## What it looks like without the intent-language

Within the [SPLectrum seed](/seed/), the same territory is read differently. Subjects share common orientation through convergence — sharing reality through language synchronises what matters. Each subject acts from its own ground — privacy of experience means no centre, no vantage point from which one subject's reading is more real than another's. Equal standing in potential underwrites pluralism as structural, not optional.

Decentralisation, read this way, is what already happens when subjects converge on common orientation while each acts from its own ground. No apparatus needed. No one identifies the right structure and builds toward it. The pattern is there because of how reality is constituted — subjects interacting through shared language, each from their own experience, the web growing in complexity through interrelation.

The objectives mutualism reaches for — sustainable arrangements, decentralised structures, diversity sustaining itself — are how the fabric weaves, not what an external mind builds toward. Shared aims, not the command chain.

## What this means for the centralised internet

The place where this lands most concretely is the one that decentralisation discussions often frame as the problem to be solved. The centralised internet — the platforms, the aggregation, the concentration of infrastructure — is usually cast as a failure to be dismantled, a wrong turn to be corrected.

Within the SPLectrum seed, centralisation is read as one configuration among others. It is a viable path that emerged from relational dynamics — subjects interacting, patterns stabilising, some configurations concentrating while others distribute. What the 19th century framework reads as a failure of cooperation, the structural reading holds as something that happened because the conditions produced it. Not an enemy. Not a mistake. A configuration.

Decentralised alternatives — distributed protocols, local-first applications, peer-to-peer networks — emerge on top of what already exists. They do not replace the centralised layer. They layer above it. The old infrastructure continues to function; what emerges above it takes its own shape. Not revolution, but emergence. Not correction, but another configuration forming from the same relational dynamics that produced the first.

The mutualism vocabulary wants to make the case — cooperation is natural, centralisation is unnatural, build accordingly. The structural reading observes what is already happening without needing to make a case at all.
