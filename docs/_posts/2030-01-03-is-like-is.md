"We went to the same restaurant we went to last year."

Almost nothing in that sentence is literally true. Same building, perhaps, though the staff has turned over. Same name, perhaps, though the menu has changed. Different night, different season, different us — you and I are different people from the people who went last year. Different table, almost certainly. And yet the sentence isn't false. It's true in a particular, contextual, deeply ordinary way. We mean something by "same restaurant" and the meaning is clear enough to act on, plan around, build conversations from.

What we mean is *similar enough in the ways that matter here*. Not identical. Similar enough. The "same" is doing work the way a good metaphor does: holding together a set of resemblances tightly enough to treat the bundle as one thing for this purpose.

This isn't a peculiarity of restaurants. It's how everyday "is" works almost everywhere we use it. "She's the same person she was ten years ago" — no, but yes. "The team that won last season won again this year" — different players, but yes. "This is the same problem we had before" — different specifics, but yes. We bind together different things under one name when they're similar enough in the relevant ways. Then we say "is" and mean it.

The [previous](/blog/2026/06/the-seed-and-category-theory/) [posts](/blog/2026/06/the-wrapper/) in this series argued that the seed lives in higher-dimensional category theory, with the 1-categorical level operating as a wrapping function rather than as substance. This is the everyday consequence. Identity — "X is Y" with metaphysical force, the kind that requires an outside view to certify — was never the operation we were really running. The operation we were running is convergence. "X is Y" is shorthand for "we have converged on X being like Y tightly enough to treat them as the same here."

The reversal that follows is the interesting move. The usual ordering treats "is" (literal identity) as primary and "is like" (metaphor, simile) as decoration or deviation. The seed reverses this. "Is like" is the foundational mode. "Is" is the converged limit case — a similarity so settled it has forgotten that it was once a similarity.

Lakoff and Johnson made the related move from the cognitive side: metaphor is not ornament on top of a literal substrate; it is foundational to thought itself. Their case was empirical — look at how people actually use language, and metaphor is everywhere, doing structural work, not just decorating. The seed adds the structural reason. Metaphor is foundational because the outside view that literal identity requires does not exist. There is no view from nowhere to certify that two things are *really* the same beyond their similarities. So metaphor — the operation of "is like" — is what we have, and what we have always had.

This isn't a loss of precision. It's a clarification of what our precision has been doing. The "same restaurant" sentence is not a sloppy approximation to a perfect identity claim we could in principle make. It's an exact statement of the operation we actually performed: convergence on "same" in the ways that matter here. Precision lives in being clear about *what we have converged on* and *in what context the convergence holds*, not in pretending to a metaphysical sameness no act of speech could deliver.

The "is" doesn't disappear. It becomes a converged wrapper — useful, often necessary, always shorthand. What disappears is the illusion that beneath it is something more solid. Beneath it is more "is like", all the way down. The grammar of similarity is what holds the structure together.

If "is like" is foundational, the question becomes what kinds of likeness travel between contexts and hold their shape there. The next post.
