The two-body problem in physics: two entities, exerting mutual influence, evolving together. Newton solved it analytically — given the positions and velocities of two gravitating masses, the future and past trajectories can be computed exactly. The problem has a name because it is a specific shape — a structural pattern, not a particular pair of planets.

What's striking is how often the same shape shows up elsewhere. In atomic physics, the hydrogen atom is a two-body problem (electron and proton, electromagnetic instead of gravitational). In negotiation: two parties, each adjusting in response to the other. In partnership: two people, each shaping how the other lives. In software architecture: two components, each consuming and producing for the other. In animal behaviour: predator and prey, locked into reciprocal adaptation.

Same pattern, different domains. None of these is reducible to celestial mechanics. The pattern travels not by analogy in the loose sense but by *structural identity* — the relational shape of mutual influence between two entities holds regardless of what the entities are and what they exchange. When the pattern enters a new context, it brings the same structural moves to the table: the way attractor states form, the way disturbances propagate, the way one body's change ripples to the other and back.

These travelling patterns are everywhere. The three-body problem (introducing instability and chaos). The prisoner's dilemma (the structure of mutual choice under uncertainty). Predator-prey cycles. Mentor-student dynamics. Buyer-seller equilibria. Each is a relational shape that has been recognised, named, and is now available for use whenever the shape appears in a new domain.

This is what the seed means by *meaning patterns*. Relational shapes that travel across contexts because they have structural identity. The carrier — the specific language used to express the pattern at a given site — is exchangeable. The meaning pattern itself, the shape, is the load-bearing identity. Physicists, negotiators, and software architects all use the two-body shape; they describe it in their own languages, with their own variables, in their own conceptual style. But it's the same pattern, recognisable by anyone who has seen it elsewhere.

[Category theory](/positioning/subjects/c/ct/) is the mathematical apparatus best suited to articulate this formally. It captures the structural skeleton — the formal shape that lets us identify sameness-of-pattern across contexts. The 1940s development by Eilenberg and Mac Lane was originally about relationships between mathematical structures; what their tool gave us, beyond the original target, was a precise language for *structure-as-relational-shape*. That language is what lets us speak rigorously about pattern travel.

The seed is structurally relational throughout. Meaning patterns are not exceptions to that — they are the seed at work, demonstrating how relational structure survives transport across different carriers. The two-body problem appearing in negotiation isn't a metaphor borrowed from physics. It's the same shape, recognised again, in a context where it can do work it couldn't do in the celestial mechanics that first revealed it.

The world is full of these patterns. Once we learn to see them, the question stops being "is this like that?" and starts being "what shape are they both?" The shape is what travels.
