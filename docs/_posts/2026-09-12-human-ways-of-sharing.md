Watch a child come into language and it is plainly not sitting still. It is doing something — reaching for what a word points at, trying a phrase and watching what it does, catching a pattern in what everyone around keeps saying and pressing it into a shape of its own. Nobody hands the child language whole; the child goes and gets it, from the middle of a world already talking.

The fields that study this closely — developmental psychology, the science of how children acquire language — have named the ways it happens, and there turn out to be several. [Vygotsky](/positioning/persons/v/vygotsky/) saw the child take inward what it first did out loud with others: a thing done *between* people, later done *within* the person. [Tomasello](/positioning/persons/t/tomasello/) found language learned from use rather than switched on by an innate grammar — the child reading what others mean and finding the patterns in what they say, building grammar up out of concrete instances. [Piaget](/positioning/persons/p/piaget/) had the child construct its own understanding, fitting new experience to what it already has and reworking the structure when it fails. And [Di Paolo](/positioning/persons/d/di-paolo/) put meaning in the encounter itself, made in the coordination between two people rather than passed from one to the other.

These are not rival theories to pick between. A child runs all of them at once, and they describe different faces of one thing. What unites them is a picture of the subject: **active, engaged, building from the reality it is coming into.** Not a passive recipient and not a bystander — a doer, at work on the world it finds itself in the middle of. It is a warm and, once you see it, fairly obvious picture. It is worth dwelling on because for a long stretch of the last century the two schools that dominated the study of the mind did not draw it at all.

The first was behaviourism, and its subject was an automaton. Stimulus in, response out; reinforce the response and it repeats. Between the input and the output there was, by strict doctrine, nothing worth talking about — the inside was a black box, and the whole point was that you did not need to open it. Watson and Skinner made a virtue of the closure. But the active subject the developmental picture describes is *precisely what lives in that box*. Everything the child does — the reaching, the trying, the building — is the middle term behaviourism threw out on principle. The two pictures do not disagree about details; one of them simply declines to look where the other one looks.

The second was psychoanalysis, and its subject had an inside — a vivid one — but a strange geography. Drives, fantasy, buried history, a whole inner theatre running its own drama largely out of reach, and often at odds with the plain world outside. Reality came in two, an inner and an outer, and the inner one held the reins. The developmental subject has no such split. It is turned toward the one world it is coming into, and its inner life is part of how it meets that world, not a separate country hidden behind it.

This is the one place worth saying where SPLectrum stands, because it is easy to mishear. The point is not that the inner life is unreal — the drives and the fantasy and the private weather are all real enough. It is that they are not a *second* reality. They belong to the subject's one reality, the same one it construes and builds and shares in. SPLectrum keeps everything psychoanalysis noticed and refuses only the split that files it away in a sealed inner room. Nothing is denied a place; it is all placed in the open.

So the developmental family, seen against the two it isn't, comes into focus. Not the automaton with no inside, and not the divided self captive to its depths — but a subject at work: absorbing what the common holds, building its own from it, and feeding its own back into the shared as it goes. That is what it looks like to come into language. It is also, if you widen the frame past childhood, what it looks like to go on living in one — never finished, always at work, always in the middle of the talking.
