Watch a child come into language and it is plainly not sitting still. It is doing something — reaching for what a word points at, trying a phrase and watching what it does, catching a pattern in what everyone around keeps saying and pressing it into a shape of its own. Nobody hands the child language whole; the child goes and gets it, from the middle of a world already talking.

The fields that study this closely — developmental psychology, the science of how children acquire language — have named the ways it happens, and there turn out to be several. [Vygotsky](/positioning/persons/v/vygotsky/) saw the child take inward what it first did out loud with others: a thing done *between* people, later done *within* the person. [Tomasello](/positioning/persons/t/tomasello/) found language learned from use rather than switched on by an innate grammar — the child reading what others mean and finding the patterns in what they say, building grammar up out of concrete instances. [Piaget](/positioning/persons/p/piaget/) had the child construct its own understanding, fitting new experience to what it already has and reworking the structure when it fails. And [Di Paolo](/positioning/persons/d/di-paolo/) put meaning in the encounter itself, made in the coordination between two people rather than passed from one to the other.

Underneath those, quieter and more minimal, runs something barer still. Long before a baby can read anyone's intentions, it is already counting — not consciously, but tracking which sounds tend to follow which, and how often. Eight-month-olds pull words out of a continuous stream of speech on the strength of those regularities alone, with nobody meaning anything at them in particular. It is the most stripped-down mechanism in the family, and it makes the point better than the grand ones do: even here, at the bottom, the child is *extracting*. The stream does not stamp itself on a blank surface. Something is going out to meet it.

And running the other way there is teaching, which turns out to be less one-sided than it looks. Csibra and Gergely noticed that a great deal of what adults do around infants is *marked* — eye contact, the child's name, that exaggerated sing-song register everyone falls into without deciding to. The marking says: *this one is for you, and it is not just about this thing here — it is how such things are.* What is striking is that infants are tuned for the marking from the start. The teaching only works because something on the other side is already listening for it.

These are not rival theories to pick between. A child runs all of them at once, and they describe different faces of one thing. What unites them is a picture of the subject: **active, engaged, building from the reality it is coming into.** Not a passive recipient and not a bystander — a doer, at work on the world it finds itself in the middle of.

There is one more thing worth noticing about the shape of the family. Most of these mechanisms carry the common inward: the child takes what is already held out there and makes it its own. But not all of them run only that way. Scaffolding — a more capable other supporting a learner through what they can't yet manage alone, then withdrawing as they take it over — works in both directions, because a subject who holds something can hold it out to someone else. Coupling runs both ways by its nature: in a real encounter, meaning is made *between*, and what the child brings feeds into what arises there just as much as what the adult brings. The traffic is not one-way, and it never was.

So: absorbing what the common holds, building its own from it, and feeding its own back into the shared as it goes. That is what it looks like to come into language. It is also, if you widen the frame past childhood, what it looks like to go on living in one — never finished, always at work, always in the middle of the talking.
