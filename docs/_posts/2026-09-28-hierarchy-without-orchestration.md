The brain organises itself out of its parts — three process frameworks describe how, from three angles: Edelman's selection among neuronal groups, the global workspace's igniting coalitions, Friston's error-minimising models. A [companion post](/blog/2026/09/process-models-of-the-brain/) walks them. What none of that settles is the question underneath: the brain is ranked, layered, nested through and through — so where is the conductor? The answer is that there isn't one, and the brain is the case that shows a hierarchy can run without a seat at the top.

Start with the principle the fabric is built on. If languages have equal standing in potential — P4 — then nothing is ranked in advance: no privilege in principle. That is not the claim that no rankings ever form. Rankings form all the time; what P4 rules out is a permanent one, and a controller above the rest to enforce it. Orchestration is exactly that — something with a vantage on the whole, directing what's below. The brain has been avoiding it all along: ranked, and with no one in charge.

Take the rhythms first, because they look most like a clock. Gamma, around 40Hz, is the pure decentralised case: generated locally, falling straight out of the excitation-inhibition loop between fast-spiking interneurons, with nothing outside setting it. A beat that emerges from the network.

Theta, around 6Hz, does not — and it is the sharper case for it. Hippocampal theta depends on the medial septum: silence the septum and the rhythm vanishes, a subcortical structure pacing a cortical one. The closest thing in the brain to a metronome. But look at what it does and does not do. It supplies a beat and no content — *when* firing is possible, never *what* fires — and it does not sit above the hippocampus in any command sense: the connection runs both ways, the hippocampus projects back, and the frequency is set jointly. It paces without instructing. Something genuinely keeps time, and still no one is in charge.

The predictive stack tells the same story in anatomy. Predictive coding runs on a deep hierarchy of cortical levels, but no level directs another — each talks only to its neighbours, and what descends is a prediction, not an instruction. Errors ascend, predictions descend; the highest levels are not in charge, they are simply the slowest and most abstract. A commanding top would need a vantage on the entire stack, and there is no such seat. And the two substrates rhyme: theta-gamma coupling nests fast local gamma inside the slow paced theta — the same shape in time that the cortical stack has in anatomy. Ranked, layered, nested — and conductorless.

Which turns the hardest case, the binding problem, from a puzzle into a confirmation. You see, hear, feel a single integrated moment, but the underlying neural activity is scattered across cortex. There is no place where the unified experience lives — and yet there it is, coherent. How the binding happens is genuinely open: synchrony was the leading candidate from the late 1980s and has been contested ever since. But the mechanism is not what the point needs. What is settled is that no one has ever found the arena. Edelman and Tononi put it directly: their dynamic core (1998) locates the unified moment not in a place but in a shifting cluster of reentrantly linked groups — a coalition that forms, binds, and dissolves. The binding is real; the binder is a moving arrangement, not an address.

The brain doesn't orchestrate. Neither does the fabric.
