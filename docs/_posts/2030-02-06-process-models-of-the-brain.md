A process model of the brain asks not what the brain is made of but how it keeps going — what a system does, moment to moment and over a lifetime, to organise itself out of the activity of its parts. The landscape is wide. Some of it is after a different quarry: integrated information theory, recurrent processing theory and attention schema theory are asking what makes a state conscious. Some of it is formal rather than mechanistic — reservoir computing and attractor dynamics describe the shape of the dynamics without committing to the machinery. Some of it is about timing, the oscillatory framings. Three frameworks isolate a mechanism cleanly and carry evidence for its core, and it is those three this post takes: evolution, competition, error correction. Each names a process with standing outside neuroscience, and each answers for one facet of how a brain carries on.

Edelman's Neural Darwinism (1987) describes how capability gets embedded, and its shape is borrowed on purpose. Edelman had already won a Nobel for showing that the immune system meets a novel antigen not by templating an antibody to fit it but by selecting from a pre-existing repertoire of variants — selection, not instruction. He ported the argument to the brain. It stands on three legs: development throws up a vast variety of neuronal *groups* — the unit is the group, not the synapse — the primary repertoire; experience selects among them, strengthening some and weakening others, the secondary repertoire; and reentry — recursive, bidirectional signalling that correlates activity across mapped groups — ties the selected groups into coherent function. Edelman was explicit that without the third leg the first two give only a heap of independently tuned groups. This is competition in the setup: selection settles the standing repertoire, and what wins is retained. New groups keep forming and entering the running, and nothing gates their entry — but the competition leaves a residue, a capability that persists.

Global Workspace Theory (Baars, 1988; Dehaene's neural mechanisms) describes how one process becomes dominant, and ignition can be watched happening. Many processes run in parallel, unconsciously; when a coalition reaches sufficient activation strength it ignites — broadcasts widely, becomes the dominant content. Masking experiments, which hold a stimulus at the edge of visibility, separate the seen from the unseen cleanly: an unseen stimulus produces only early, local activation, while a seen one triggers a late, sudden, widespread activation around 300ms. A threshold a stimulus can be watched crossing. This is competition in the play: coalitions contest for dominance now, and what wins holds the position briefly and loses it. Whatever reaches threshold broadcasts, indifferent to where the coalition came from — an old one and a newly formed one judged at the same bar — and the win leaves no residue, only the next round.

The units differ — groups over a lifetime, coalitions in the moment — and both are process centres; the post fixes neither as the unit. What holds across both is that entry is open. A centre enters the standing repertoire by being selected and enters the current contest by reaching threshold, and neither door has a keeper.

Friston's free energy principle (2006) describes how fit is determined, and the reading it sits on is measurable. The brain maintains generative models that predict incoming signals; predictions descend and prediction errors ascend, and the system works to minimise the errors. Repetition suppression shows responses falling as a stimulus becomes predictable; the mismatch negativity shows the reverse, a signal thrown up precisely when a prediction fails; laminar recordings tell the descending predictions from the ascending errors by cortical layer. A brain forever anticipating and correcting, its errors and predictions running in opposite directions through the sheet.

Evolution, competition, error correction: capability retained by selection, dominance settled by contest, fit maintained by prediction. Three processes, each with its own mechanism and its own evidence, each answering for a facet of how a brain keeps going. They operate at different reaches — one across a lifetime, one in the passing moment, one continuously against every incoming signal — and a brain is running all three at once. But they are three answers to one question, not three parts of one answer, and this post leaves them standing alongside each other rather than folding them into a single account.
