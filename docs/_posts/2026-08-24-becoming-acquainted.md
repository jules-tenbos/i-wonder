For a while now the reading here has circled process — models of how a brain keeps going, and alongside them the machine-learning systems that have lately made those models interesting again. Two literatures, little traffic between them, and yet they keep asking versions of one question: something engages a medium, something is made of what arrives. I kept needing a word for that making-sense, one word that could stretch across both shelves. There is an obvious candidate. The trouble is what the dictionary has done to it.

Cognition, the entry says: *the mental action or process of acquiring knowledge and understanding through thought, experience, and the senses.* It reads cleanly, settled, nothing to argue with. It carries two restrictions all the same, and both are easy to miss precisely because they are doing their work so quietly.

Start with *mental*. One word in, cognition has been given an address: it happens inside minds. And the entry never says what a mind is — that word arrives undefined and stays that way. So the boundary work is done before anything has been said. Whatever has a mind can cognise; whatever lacks one cannot; and the criterion for having one is nowhere on the page. In practice a boundary drawn that way lands where such boundaries always land — on us, and on whatever resembles us closely enough to be granted honorary entry. Not anthropocentric by argument. Anthropocentric by default, which is harder to notice and harder to contest.

Then *acquiring*. Acquisition is a possession word — somewhere a stock exists, and it changes hands; knowledge as goods, transferred in. But the older word underneath points elsewhere: *cognoscere*, to get to know, to become acquainted. Becoming acquainted with a city is not taking delivery of it. Nothing is handed over; a familiarity grows where there was none. And if knowledge is not a stock waiting somewhere behind experience but something a subject comes to recognise within it, then nothing is being transferred at all, and *acquiring* loses its sting on its own. The etymology was wider than the entry all along.

The entry's other channels — thought and the senses — are experience by other names, interaction with the medium running inward or outward, so experience can stand for the three. Lift the two restrictions, and what remains is still recognisably the dictionary's own sentence: *the action or process of acquiring knowledge and understanding through experience.* Nothing exotic has been added. Two quiet narrowings have been undone.

Why insist on the width? Because of what the narrow word cannot say. Consider a processor. Its instruction set is a complete language — every instruction well defined, every execution exact, billions of them a second, flawlessly. And it goes nowhere. Nothing is made of what passes through; nothing is recognised; nothing new is deposited; the language ends the day exactly as it began it. There is no failure anywhere in that description — the medium is not defective, it is *frozen*. The narrow word has nothing to say here: no mind, question closed. The wide word says the interesting thing instead: what this language is missing is precisely the making-sense, and now the missing thing has a name.

Held this wide, cognition names an activity, not a faculty — the making-sense position, occupied wherever there is a subject to occupy it: a child in a stream of talk, an animal reading its terrain, and whatever else turns out to hold the position. How far that reaches is not a question the word has to settle in advance, which is exactly what is wrong with definitions that try — legislate the boundary first and every borderline case becomes a dispute about the definition. Getting to know, becoming acquainted: the old sense was the right one all along. It just needed the two restrictions taken off so it could do its work.
