In the winter of his late twenties, William James could barely get out of bed. He had trained as a doctor, drifted, sunk into a depression heavy enough that he questioned whether a human life was anything more than a machine running out its causes. What pulled him clear was not an argument but a decision. Reading the French philosopher [Charles Renouvier](/positioning/persons/r/renouvier/) on free will, he resolved to *believe* his choices were real — to act as if the future were open — and to treat that belief itself as his first free act. He came back to life through a wager. The philosophy he spent the next forty years building has the shape of that wager: a universe that is not finished, and a self entitled, where the evidence runs out and a decision cannot wait, to lean.

He is the most readable of the great American philosophers, and it cost him some standing among the rigorous. Where Peirce was technical and largely ignored, James was charismatic, and he made [pragmatism](/positioning/subjects/p/pragmatism/) a public movement — truth is what works, what proves itself in the living, "the expedient in our way of thinking." Critics heard vulgar relativism; James heard honesty about how beliefs actually function. He held the chair of philosophy at Harvard, wrote a *Principles of Psychology* that founded a field, and gave the world the phrase "stream of consciousness" — experience as a river with no joints, shading and flowing where earlier psychology had seen discrete states.

Underneath the pragmatism ran something deeper, and it is why he is remembered as the first self-avowed pluralist — the first to take the word as his own banner: [radical empiricism](/positioning/persons/j/james/), the insistence that relations are as real as the things they relate. The and, the with, the between — as real as the terms they connect. From that seed grows a picture of reality not as one block but as a patchwork of genuinely distinct parts, really joined and never wholly summed. He called it a *pluralistic universe*, and set it squarely against the monism of Hegel and the British idealists, for whom the many were finally moments of one Absolute. James refused the One. Reality, he insisted, is many, loosely connected, and always still being made. His phrase for it was "ever not quite": the whole never closes, there is always more coming, the account is never done.

That refusal is the through-line from the wager to the metaphysics. A finished universe has no room for a genuine free act — everything is already settled, the future only unrolling what the past contained. James wanted the future open because he had needed it open to live, and he built a philosophy in which openness is not a mood but a structural fact about the world. To say reality is a pluriverse is to say it is unfinished, and to say it is unfinished is to leave room for the leaning that saved him.

Where does he stop? He kept pragmatism personal — truth is what works *for me*, what helps *me* live — and left to others the question of how a community converges on a shared truth, and the further question of what it means for language to be the medium of that convergence. His pluralism opens the door; the social and the relational needed hands after his. But he opened the door. The conviction that the many are real is older than philosophy's memory; James was the first to claim it under its own name — and to make a life out of believing it.
