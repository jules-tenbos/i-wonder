Equal standing has consequences. If languages are inter-relational and have equal standing in potential — P4 — then nothing ranks above anything else. Orchestration introduces hierarchy: something above directing what's below. That creates friction against a principle where every participant stands on the same level. A decentralised approach conforms to equal standing with minimal friction. Not the only option, but the low-friction choice. And the brain — the decentralised system we use every day — has been working this way all along.

The brain does not start from a blank slate. Cortical areas are roughly sketched by molecular gradients during development, then sharpened and refined by the activity that flows through them. Specialisation is not centrally designed in detail — it emerges from the interaction between a genetic scaffold and functional demand. The coarse map is inherited. The fine map is earned through use. In the fabric, a similar pattern: the structure provides the scaffold, the processes that embed themselves in it are shaped by what works. What gets used persists. What doesn't, fades.

Gerald Edelman's Theory of Neuronal Group Selection — Neural Darwinism (1987) — drew the parallel explicitly. Edelman won the 1972 Nobel Prize for discovering the structure of antibodies, where Niels Jerne's clonal selection theory showed that the immune system works by selecting among pre-existing diverse antibodies, not by being instructed by antigens. Edelman extended this selectionist logic to the brain. Three components. First, developmental selection: vast numbers of neuronal groups form with enormous variety — no two brains are wired identically. Raw diversity of process capability. Second, experiential selection: through experience, connections that fire together strengthen, connections that don't weaken or die. Use patterns reinforce what works — no central mechanism choosing. Third, reentrant mapping: surviving groups connect through simultaneous, reciprocal signalling across parallel pathways between brain areas. Not simple feedback — parallel, bidirectional, peer-to-peer. Coherent behaviour emerges from this coordination, not from a central controller.

Bernard Baars proposed Global Workspace Theory in 1988, later grounded in neural mechanisms by Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux. Many processes run in parallel, unconsciously — each doing its thing independently. When one coalition of processes reaches sufficient activation strength — through interaction with other processes, not through a central selector — it ignites: a nonlinear wave of neural activity that broadcasts widely and becomes the dominant conscious content. The others don't disappear — they continue running and can win next time. Dehaene's group demonstrated clear neural signatures: the P3b wave on EEG and late widespread cortical activation on fMRI correlate with conscious perception, while subliminal stimuli produce only local, early activity.

Karl Friston's Predictive Processing framework (2006, formalised 2010) adds the mechanism of fit. The brain maintains a hierarchy of generative models producing top-down predictions at multiple levels. These predictions are compared against incoming signals. Where prediction and signal diverge, prediction errors propagate upward to update the model. A constant cycle of generate-compare-update. No central authority — the model that produces the least surprise dominates. Mismatch negativity on EEG, repetition suppression on fMRI, and single-neuron prediction-error coding in visual cortex provide direct observational evidence for these error signals.

Three models, three aspects of the same picture. Edelman: how process capability gets embedded through variation and selection. Baars and Dehaene: how one process becomes the dominant reality through coalition and ignition. Friston: how fit is determined through prediction and error correction. And threading through all three — reentrant signalling. Brain areas signalling bidirectionally, simultaneously, peers synchronising with peers. Coherence through mutual interaction, not hierarchy.

The parallel to decentralised process in the fabric is structural, not metaphorical. Processes are embedded in the data landscape through variation and experiential selection — what works persists. Activity is triggered by data state changes — incoming signals, internal state changes, results from other processes. The triggering mechanism is the data, not a scheduler. Dominance emerges through convergence, not command. The brain doesn't orchestrate. Neither does the fabric.
