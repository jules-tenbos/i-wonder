In the previous post I took time out from our journey into metaphysics to make an important point: each of us has the agency to shape their own reality with their own beliefs and convictions. Especially important when dealing with the values a subject or community holds towards other subjects or communities — the terrain of ethics. 

SPLectrum's ethics is not normative but descriptive, it aims to provide clarity and insight, not to dictate. The way we project values towards others is more evolved than one might at first sight suspect. We live in a sense in multiple realities simultaneously. Being part of a family, of a local community, going to school, being at work, being in public ... these often present themselves as very different realities. Each is governed by its own rules of engagement, each is a different 'language game'.

I see this as the base level, with values attached to roles within the game. Each language game as such ends up having its own ethics. The next question: when does it make sense to project values as good or bad at the community level, with so many in play? It doesn't make sense to shoehorn the games into an overall good / bad package. To be a good parent, a good citizen, a good judge, a good soldier - good means quite different things. A good soldier is there to kill, at times own citizens. A good judge is there to convict, and potentially cause hardship for innocent family and friends.

SPLectrum prefers an equal standing approach - there is no absolute ethical standard. Through belonging there is convergence on what a community considers appropriate - good if one wants to call it that - and this always spreads out across multiple language games. And often with a price to pay - a good soldier kills people, innocent people. 

I think focusing on a 'universal' good is misguided, it is better to make the consideration at the level of each individual activity on its local merits. Then it becomes easier to position each into a place in our life that we feel comfortable with — building up an overall picture, and the dominant values with it. A descriptive approach to ethics helps to shed light, a prerequisite to informed decisions - agency in action. One may think that (ethical) decisions are binary, but that is not the case. It isn't: do I do this or not? In fact it is: do I play the game and perform the role, do I refuse the role, or do I not play the game at all? Those are three options. To be able to say 'I don't play' is important, belonging is a choice best made actively.

Belonging to a community is not binary, there are choices to be made about the language games I participate in. Belonging is fuzzy, but no less belonging. Black and white only exists at the imagined threshold of being in or out. In reality it is a resonance, how strongly I belong. And our natural language reflects this: 'I have never felt really part of this.' is a statement of fuzzy belonging. And this inevitably impacts the values we emanate towards others.

SPLectrum's ethics, being concerned with the values a subject emanates towards others, is in my opinion similar to its ontology: static and descriptive. The still picture of ethics is there for a better understanding of values and language games, but the reality is a dynamic of co-existence where we live the resonance and dissonance of these values. It runs between communities, between a subject and its communities, and — because none of us is a single thing — within each subject too, among the games it holds. That dynamic is politics, and it is where this series goes next.
