In the previous post I took time out from our journey into metaphysics to make an important point: each of us has the agency to shape their own reality with their beliefs and convictions. This is particularly significant when dealing with ethics, the values a subject or community holds towards other subjects or communities. 

SPLectrum's ethics is not normative but descriptive. The picture of the values we project towards others is far more subtle than a lot of ethics discussions make you believe. The main reason is that - in a sense - we live in multiple realities. Being part of a family, of a local community, going to school, being at work, being in public ... the realities of these are very different. Each is governed by rules of engagement that are quite different. These are all different 'language games'.

And each language game has its own ethics. You are in a game, you have a role and roles have values attached. When does it make sense to project values as good or bad at the community level, while there are so many different games in play? It is not good enough to implicitly interpret language games in specific ways and shoehorn them into an overall good / bad package. To be a good parent, a good citizen, a good judge, a good soldier - good means quite different things for each of those games. A good soldier is there to kill, at times own citizens. A good judge is there to convict, and potentially cause hardship for innocent family and friends.

SPLectrum prefers an equal standing approach - there is no absolute ethical standard. Through belonging there is convergence on what a community considers appropriate - good if one wants to call it that. But always with a price to pay - a good soldier kills people, innocent people. 

Ethics, being concerned with the values a subject emanates towards others, is in my opinion a bit like ontology: static and descriptive. Once politics comes into play, it becomes dynamic: values are applied. Politics is for another post, I like to keep the focus here on the value landscape as presented by the multiple language games.

I think focusing on a 'universal' good is misguided, it is better to first consider each activity on its own merits. Then it becomes easier to give each a place in your life that you feel comfortable with - and from that, the dominant values follow. A descriptive approach to ethics helps to shed light, a prerequisite to informed decisions - agency in action. One may think that (ethical) decisions are binary, but that is not the case. It isn't: do I do this or not? In fact it is: do I play the game and perform the role, do I refuse the role, or do I not play the game at all? Those are three options. To be able to say 'I don't play' is important, belonging is a choice best made actively.

Belonging to a community is not binary, there are choices to be made about the language games I participate in. Belonging is fuzzy but no less belonging, black and white only exists at the imagined threshold of being in or out. In reality it is a resonance, how strongly I belong. And our natural language reflects this: 'I have never felt really part of this.' is a statement of fuzzy belonging. And this inevitably impacts the values we emanate towards others.

The still picture of ethics is there for a better understanding of values and language games, but the reality is a dynamic of co-existence where we live the resonance and dissonance of these values. It runs between communities, between a subject and its communities, and — because none of us is a single thing — within each subject too, among the games it holds. That dynamic is politics, and it is where this series goes next.
