In the previous post I took time out from our journey into metaphysics to make an important point: each of us has the agency to shape their own reality, to fill it with their beliefs and convictions. This is of no less importance when dealing with ethics, which deals with the values a subject or community holds towards other subjects or communties. 

SPLectrum's ethics is not normative but descriptive. The picture of the values we project towards others is far more subtle than a lot of ethics discussion make you belief. And the main reason for that is that - in a sense - we live in multiple realities. Being part of a family, of a local community, going to school, being at work, being in public ... these can be very different realities - where the rules of engagement are quite different from on to the other. These different realities are governed by different 'language games'.

It is important to realise this, because each of these games have their own rules, in a sense their own ethics. You are in a game, you have a role and the values ethics is concerned about are attached to that role. How come one often want to project values as good or bad at the community level, when there are so many different games in play within the community? Because implicitly language games get interpreted in specific ways and shoehorned into an overall good / bad package. But why? To be a good parent, a good citizen, a good judge, a good soldier - good means quite different things for each of those games. A good soldier is there to kill, at times own citizens. A good judge is there to convict, and potentially cause hardship for innocent family and friends.

SPLectrum prefers an equal standing approach - there is no absolute ethical standard. But through belonging there is convergence on what a community considers appropriate - good if one wants to call it that way. But there is always a price to pay - a good soldier kills people, innocent people. 

Ethics, being concerned with the values a subject emanates towards others, is in my opinion a bit like ontology: static and descriptive. It is with politics that the dynamic comes alive and the values are applied. Politics is for another post, it is important to first focus on the value landscape as presented by the multiple language games.

I think the focus on an 'universal' good is misguided, and it makes much more sense to first focus on the different activities one is involved in. I like a descriptive approach to ethics and see it as a prerequisite to informed decisions - agency in action. It is important to realise that (ethical) decisions are not binary. It is not: do I do this or not? In fact it is: do I play the game and perform the role or not, or do I not play the game? Those are three options. Not belonging is a valid and important option.

So my belonging to my community is not binary, as I may choose not to take part in a number of (language) games that are part of it. Belonging is fuzzy, black and white only exists at the imagined threshold of being in or out. It is all about resonance, how strong it is. And our natural language reflects this: 'I have never felt really part of this.' is a statement of fuzzy belonging. And this impacts inevitable the ethical side.

The still picture of ethics is there for a better understanding of values and language games, but the reality is a dynamic of co-existence where we live the resonance and dissonance of these values. It runs between communities, between a subject and its communities, and — because none of us is a single thing — within each subject too, among the games it holds. That dynamic is politics, and it is where this series goes next.
