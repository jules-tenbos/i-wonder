The [last post](/blog/label/seed/) left a child in the middle of a world already talking, going and getting language for itself — absorbing what the common holds, building its own from it, feeding its own back in. Active, engaged, at work. A warm picture, and once you see it, an obvious one.

It is worth asking why it took so long to draw, and why the schools that dominated the study of the mind for most of a century drew something else. Three of them, in fact, drew three different insides — and none of them drew that one.

The first left the inside empty. Behaviourism's subject was an automaton: stimulus in, response out, reinforce and repeat. Between the two there was, by doctrine, nothing worth discussing — a black box, and the point was that you needn't open it. Watson and Skinner made a virtue of the closure. But the active subject the developmental picture describes *is precisely what lives in that box*. Everything the child does — the reaching, the trying, the building — is the middle term behaviourism threw out on principle.

The second gave the inside a basement. Psychoanalysis had a vivid interior — drives, fantasy, buried history, a whole theatre running its own drama out of reach and often at odds with the plain world outside. But reality came in two, an inner and an outer, and the inner one held the reins. Here is the one place worth saying where SPLectrum stands, because it is easy to mishear. The point is not that the inner life is unreal — the drives and the private weather are real enough. It is that they are not a *second* reality. They belong to the subject's one reality, the same one it construes and builds and shares in. Nothing is denied a place; it is all placed in the open.

The third is the interesting one, and it is still going.

It begins with a genuinely great argument. Noam [Chomsky](/positioning/persons/c/chomsky/), in 1959, took Skinner's account of language apart — the child learns far too much, far too fast, from far too little, for conditioning to be the story. Nobody supplies a list of the sentences that are impossible, correction is rare and ignored, and yet every normal child lands a grammar in a few years. Something must be coming from the child's side. Behaviourism could not say what, because it had declared the question closed.

Then comes the move. If the child couldn't have got it from experience, said [nativism](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/), then it was already there. The inside is not empty and not split — it comes **pre-filled**. Grammar arrives installed; exposure sets the switches. The child doesn't build the grammar. It finds it.

What happens over the next sixty years is the part worth watching. The endowment starts out rich — whole systems of innate rules. By the 1980s it is universal principles plus a set of binary switches. By the 1990s the Minimalist Program has stripped it toward a single operation, *Merge*, that joins two things into one and can run on its own output. And the field is explicit about why it shrank: universal grammar has to be rich enough to explain what the child gets from thin evidence, and small enough to have plausibly evolved. Those two pull opposite ways. The theory retreated under the second, not because the first found new evidence.

Meanwhile the question of where the *pieces* come from — the things Merge combines — stays open. Chomsky's answer is that lexical concepts are too complex to have been extracted from experience, so they must already be there too, triggered rather than learned; he has said this holds even for *carburetor*. Jerry Fodor took it to the end of the line: concepts can't be learned at all, only triggered, so *car* is innate. Putnam could hardly conceal his astonishment. Dennett observed that on this view Aristotle had the concept of an aeroplane in his brain. The field's own verdict is that Fodor went too far — but nobody leaves the position that pushed him there. And in *Why Only Us*, Berwick and Chomsky write that the origin of those atoms "remains for us a mystery."

None of that is bad science. The people are serious, the argument that started it was right about behaviourism, and the whole thing is defended in public by people who disagree with each other sharply. It is something else: a stance taken early that then had to be held. Each move is reasonable on its own. Put them end to end and you can see where the effort went — into keeping the boat afloat, and less and less into what the boat was for.

Set beside that, the acquisition mechanisms look almost unremarkable, which is the point. Nobody has to defend them. They are not a position; they are a pile of findings that accumulated and happen to fit together — internalisation and use and construction and coupling, and underneath them a baby counting syllables it has no theory about. There is no commitment there needing shoring up, no boat, no waterline. That's what a picture looks like when it was drawn from watching rather than from having to be right.
