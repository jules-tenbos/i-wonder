A cook is carving an ox for his lord, and the carving is a dance — the knife moves in rhythm, nothing is hacked, the blade never meets bone. Asked for his secret, the cook gives the strangest answer in the history of expertise: he has stopped looking. Early in his career he saw the whole ox; after three years he no longer saw the ox at all; now perception and analysis have fallen away and something deeper moves the knife along the animal's natural seams. That is why the blade has stayed sharp for nineteen years. The story is Cook Ding's, from the *Zhuangzi*, written in China in the fourth century BCE — and it is a considered position on knowing, wearing an apron.

Of Zhuang Zhou the man we have a few lines and one perfect anecdote. A minor official in a lacquer garden in the state of Song, he was offered the premiership of Chu by a king who had heard of him. He declined: better to be a live turtle dragging its tail in the mud than a dead one venerated in a shrine. The refusal is the philosophy in miniature — he declined office the way he declined doctrine, and taught instead by story, joke, and reversal. When his wife died, his friend found him drumming on a tub and singing; grief had given way to seeing her death as one more transformation, like the turning of the seasons. He was, among the world's great philosophers, very possibly the funniest.

The skill stories are where his thinking cuts deepest. Wheelwright Bian interrupts his duke, who is reading the words of the ancient sages, with a craftsman's objection: the knack of making a wheel — not too slow, not too hurried — cannot be put into words. He knows it in his hands and cannot give it even to his own son, which is why he is still making wheels at seventy. So the books the duke is reading hold only what the sages could say — their leavings. What could be told was never the knack. The knowing lives in the doing, and nothing deliberative runs behind the knife.

Zhuangzi drew the consequence for language itself, gently. A fish trap matters because of the fish; once the fish is caught, the trap can be forgotten. Words matter because of their meaning; once the meaning is grasped, the words can be forgotten. Then the joke that seals it: where can I find someone who has forgotten words, so I can have a word with them? Language is an instrument, not a vault — which is why he wrote parables instead of treatises, and why the treatise-writers have been arguing about him ever since.

Here is the remarkable part. Twenty-three centuries later, Western philosophy arrived at the same place, by its own route and with no line of influence. Ryle showed that the picture of an inner act of theorising behind every intelligent performance ends in regress — knowing-how is not knowing-that applied. Polanyi built a whole epistemology on the craftsman's predicament: we know more than we can tell. Wittgenstein told philosophy to stop thinking of understanding as a mental process at all. None of them needed Cook Ding. That is precisely what makes the meeting worth noticing: when two traditions with no common ancestor carve along the same seam, the seam is likely in the territory, not in either map.

Where does Zhuangzi stop? Exactly where he chose to. He built no system, advanced no method, legislated nothing — and anyone who asks him how the knack is acquired, or how perspectives are to be weighed, is answered with another story. The refusal costs what refusals cost: the traditions that grew from him — Chan Buddhism among them — had to build with materials he deliberately left loose. But the refusal is also the position. Some things are shown in the cutting and lost in the telling; a philosophy honest about that will look, from the treatise-writer's bench, like a man telling jokes about butchers. The knife, meanwhile, is still sharp.
