Equal standing has consequences. If languages have equal standing in potential — P4 — then nothing ranks above anything else. Orchestration introduces hierarchy: something above directing what's below. That creates friction against a principle where every participant stands on the same level. A decentralised approach conforms to equal standing with minimal friction. Not the only option, but the low-friction choice. And the brain — the decentralised system we use every day — has been working this way all along.

The brain doesn't start from a blank slate. Cortical areas are roughly sketched by molecular gradients during development, then sharpened by the activity that flows through them. Specialisation isn't centrally designed in detail — it emerges from the interaction between a genetic scaffold and functional demand. The coarse map is inherited; the fine map is earned through use. In the fabric, the same pattern: structure provides the scaffold; processes that embed themselves are shaped by what works.

Three frameworks describe how a decentralised process system organises itself, each from a different angle.

Edelman's Neural Darwinism (1987) describes how capability gets embedded. Vast variety of neuronal groups form during development; experience strengthens connections that fire together and weakens those that don't. Variation and selection, no central designer.

Global Workspace Theory (Baars, 1988; Dehaene's neural mechanisms) describes how one process becomes dominant. Many processes run in parallel, unconsciously. When a coalition reaches sufficient activation strength, it ignites — broadcasts widely, becomes the dominant content. Others continue; they can win next time.

Friston's Predictive Processing (2006) describes how fit is determined. The brain maintains generative models that predict incoming signals; prediction errors propagate to update them. The model that produces the least surprise dominates.

Variation and selection. Coalition and ignition. Prediction and fit. Three angles on how a decentralised process flow organises itself. But coordination still needs to happen somehow — the parts have to stay in step without a conductor.

Edelman's other major contribution was reentrant signalling: parallel, bidirectional, simultaneous communication between brain areas. Not feedback in the control-systems sense — peers signalling peers, in both directions at once. Coherent behaviour emerges from this peer synchronisation, not from a control loop.

How do peers stay in sync without a clock? Oscillations. The brain is a thicket of overlapping rhythms — gamma waves around 40Hz, theta around 6Hz, theta-gamma coupling between them. Distributed neurons fire in time with these rhythms, coordinating their activity without a central conductor. The rhythms emerge from the network, not imposed on it.

This is part of the answer to a deeper puzzle: the binding problem. You see, hear, feel a single integrated moment, but the underlying neural activity is scattered across cortex. There is no place where the unified experience lives. Yet there it is — bound together, coherent. The emerging picture is that binding happens through coalitional dominance and oscillatory synchronisation. The unified moment is what wins the workspace, not what gets assembled in some central arena.

And the brain doesn't only run when triggered. The default mode network — active whenever the brain isn't focused on an external task — wanders, simulates, consolidates. Continuous internal activity, not idle. A decentralised system with its own background life.

That life extends through sleep. During sleep the brain replays the day's activity in compressed form, reorganising connections, reinforcing what mattered, letting the rest fade. Variation and selection in a different mode — temporal, offline, asynchronous. The fabric runs at multiple timescales.

These are the directions HAICC wants to explore. How processes embed in the fabric. How dominance shifts through coalition. How fit gets refined through encounter. How peers coordinate without a conductor. How distributed activity binds into coherent action. How a system runs continuously, not just when triggered. How it reorganises through cycles of activity and rest.

The brain doesn't orchestrate. Neither does the fabric.
