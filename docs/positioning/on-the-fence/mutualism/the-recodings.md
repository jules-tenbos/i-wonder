# The recodings

This page is Part 2 of the [mutualism fence](/positioning/on-the-fence/mutualism/), which examines the structural friction between a 19th century vocabulary and the relational framework of the SPLectrum seed. This page develops the 20th century recodings, names where residual meaning stays latent, and addresses contemporary evocations of the original framework.

---

## Moves of context recognition

The 20th century work in game theory and complex adaptive systems theory can be read as moves that recognised the 19th century framework needed updating in light of where science had moved. This is the fence's reading — an analytical claim at a deeper conceptual level than the field's own self-description. The field treats these as developments within mutualism studies; the dictionary keeps "mutual benefit between parties" as the criterion. The fence reads them as moves that took the resonances forward from mutualism into vocabulary fit for later science.

### Game theory

[Robert Trivers](/positioning/persons/t/trivers/)' reciprocal altruism (1971) and [John Maynard Smith](/positioning/persons/m/maynard-smith/)'s evolutionary game theory shift the resonances into formal language-game territory. The vocabulary becomes strategies, payoffs, equilibria — a grammar with its own internal rules. The categorical apparatus of cooperator and cheater is recoded into structural positions in a formal game.

The moral judge that lived inside the 19th century framework relocates rather than disappears. The framework itself no longer requires an internal vantage point — an equilibrium is impersonal, a structural outcome rather than a verdict. But players, modellers, and observers can still rank outcomes from outside the game. Judgment moves from being built into the vocabulary to being available to those who use it. The shift is real, and it is incomplete.

The formal vocabulary is productive. It generates testable predictions and has driven decades of research. It also carries its own assumptions — that organisms are something like strategic agents, that interactions can be modelled as exchanges between players with interests. Whether this vocabulary describes the mechanisms at work or projects human categories onto non-human systems remains an open question in the [philosophy of biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_biology).

### Complex adaptive systems

[Stuart Kauffman](/positioning/persons/k/kauffman/)'s *The Origins of Order* (1993) and [complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_adaptive_system) more broadly suggest that the patterns the field still calls *mutualistic* may be emergent properties of systems with many interacting components, local rules, and no central coordination. A fungus extending its hyphae into a root system is not cooperating — it is doing what the local interaction between its reality and its environment produces.

Pattern without unit-of-interest. Emergence without strategic actor. The structural description does not require the categorical name "mutualism" — but the name persists, carrying its 19th century connotations alongside the new structural reading. The retained labelling is itself an instance of the residual vocabulary the next section names.

When done cleanly — pattern, emergence, local interaction, no centre — the CAS framing sits at a different level from the 19th century vocabulary. It reaches into structural-relational territory where the deterministic and absolutist features of the original framework are no longer load-bearing.

### A creational reading

Read creationally, these recodings are P5 in motion within the philosophy of biology — new languages evolving at the overlap of older ones, each reaching further than what it came from. The game-theoretic vocabulary grows from the inadequacies of the categorical one. The CAS vocabulary grows from the inadequacies of the agent-centred one. Each is a language in the SPLectrum seed's sense: a committed way of expressing relation, with its own reach and its own limits.

The trajectory is toward where the SPLectrum seed sits — structural, relational, emergent. The recodings are moves in that direction. They do not arrive at the same place, but they move.

## Where residual vocabulary stays latent

The recodings have made the structural move. The words themselves have not entirely followed.

"Cooperation," "benefit," "mutualistic patterns" — these are redefined formally within the new vocabularies, but the older meanings keep importing silently. A reader encountering "cooperative strategy" in an evolutionary game theory paper carries the everyday meaning alongside the formal one. The formal redefinition coexists with the import; it does not cancel it.

The recodings do not position the redefinitions against the older meanings. They do not say: "when we say cooperation here, we do not mean what Kropotkin meant." The import happens without practitioners or readers noticing, because the disciplines' own vocabularies do not flag the shift. A term like "mutualistic" in a CAS paper points to an emergent pattern that does not require the concept of mutual benefit between identifiable parties — but the word still carries that concept in its etymology, its everyday use, and its disciplinary history.

This is handled architecturally within the SPLectrum seed. The [vocabulary section](/vocabulary/), the positioning relative to traditions — [Wittgenstein](/positioning/persons/w/wittgenstein/), [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/), [Merleau-Ponty](/positioning/persons/m/merleau-ponty/), [Rovelli](/positioning/persons/r/rovelli/), pragmatism, process philosophy — the explicit definitions. The discipline is to keep meaning on the surface, not in silent import. Where a term used within the SPLectrum seed could carry older connotations, the positioning apparatus makes the intended reading visible. The recodings do not carry this architectural commitment. That is why they sit partly with the SPLectrum seed — in structural territory, moving toward relational and emergent readings — and partly with what they came from, through the vocabulary that still carries the older framework's assumptions without flagging them.

## Contemporary evocations

The recodings made the move of recognising that the context had changed. Contemporary popular evocations of mutualism — across the many discourses where the term gets recruited for current ends — often have not.

Where mutualism is invoked today in discussions of decentralisation, cooperation, and community, the 19th century framework frequently arrives intact. The appeal to nature: "organisms cooperate, so should we." The interventionist mind: identify the right structure and build toward it. The deterministic assumption: if we arrange things correctly, the right outcomes follow. The categorical vocabulary: cooperators and free-riders, participants and defectors.

These assumptions travel silently with the vocabulary. The interventionist stance, applied to decentralised ends, reproduces the centralising structure it seeks to displace — someone must identify the right arrangement, implement it, and judge whether others are participating correctly. The command chain is in the path, even when decentralisation is in the destination. The vocabulary does not advertise this. It imports the assumptions structurally, and the import is difficult to see precisely because the vocabulary sounds like it describes what the user intends.

The fence sits here: not with what mutualism observed, which is real; not with what the recodings achieved, which moved the framework forward; but with the vocabulary as it travels now, carrying assumptions that silently undercut what it argues for.
