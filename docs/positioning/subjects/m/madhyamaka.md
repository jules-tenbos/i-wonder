# Madhyamaka

Madhyamaka — the "Middle Way" school — is the Buddhist philosophical tradition founded on [Nāgārjuna](/positioning/persons/n/nagarjuna/)'s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK, c. 2nd century CE), and one of the two great philosophical schools of Indian Mahāyāna, alongside [Yogācāra](/positioning/subjects/y/yogacara/). Its central claim is a single equation: emptiness *is* dependent origination. Everything is empty of inherent self-nature (*śūnyatā*) precisely because everything arises in dependence on conditions (*pratītyasamutpāda*) — emptiness is the absence of independent existence, not the absence of existence. The school holds the claim together with the two truths: conventional truth, the functional everyday descriptions by which the world is navigated, and ultimate truth, the emptiness of self-nature — two modes of understanding one dependently originated reality, not two realities. The founding analysis is [Nāgārjuna](/positioning/persons/n/nagarjuna/)'s and is treated on his page; this page carries the school that grew from it.

## The dispute over method

The tradition's formative internal fight was about how a philosopher who holds that all things are empty can argue at all. [Buddhapālita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhapalita) (c. 470–540) read Nāgārjuna's method as purely negative: *prasaṅga*, reductio ad absurdum applied to the opponent's own premises, with no counter-thesis advanced. [Bhāviveka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavaviveka) (c. 500–570) objected that refutation alone is philosophically incomplete — the Mādhyamika should advance independent arguments (*svātantra*) for emptiness, using the logical tools of Dignāga's school. [Candrakīrti](/positioning/persons/c/candrakirti/) (c. 600–650) sided with Buddhapālita and gave the negative reading its lasting defence: independent arguments require premises about determinately existing things, which is exactly what the Mādhyamika denies, so *prasaṅga* is not a preliminary but the method, full stop. The two positions hardened into the sub-schools later doxography calls **Prāsaṅgika** and **Svātantrika** — with the caution, pressed by David Seyfort Ruegg and others in modern scholarship, that the sharp two-school division is clearer in the Tibetan reception than in the Indian sources it was imposed on.

## The debates

**With Yogācāra.** The sibling school was also the standing opponent. Candrakīrti attacked the position he attributed to [Yogācāra](/positioning/subjects/y/yogacara/) — external objects denied, consciousness alone real — as subjective idealism, and rejected the storehouse consciousness as a covert substantialism undermining emptiness. Śāntarakṣita (8th century) built the synthesis instead: mind-only as the best account of the conventional, emptiness as ultimate — the Yogācāra-Madhyamaka in which form the material entered Tibet.

**With Vedānta.** [Śaṅkara](/positioning/persons/s/sankara/)'s charge became the canonical outside objection: emptiness without a positive ground is nihilism. The Madhyamaka response — that the charge confuses "empty of self-nature" with "non-existent," dependent origination being the positive content — has been restated in every generation, and the exchange remains one of the foundational disputes of Indian philosophy.

## Transmission

The school travelled east as Sānlùn, the "Three Treatise" school of China, on to Korea and Japan, where it fed the formation of Zen. In Tibet it became the philosophical mainstream: [Tsongkhapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsongkhapa) (1357–1419), founder of the Gelug school, established Candrakīrti's Prāsaṅgika reading as the correct interpretation of Nāgārjuna and the highest view, and through Gelug institutional dominance that reading became the standard of the Tibetan scholastic curriculum — a dominance so complete that the diversity of Indian Madhyamaka is often studied through the lens of disagreement with Candrakīrti rather than on its own terms.

## Modern reception

The school is a live participant in contemporary philosophy rather than a historical exhibit: [Jay Garfield](/positioning/persons/g/garfield/)'s translation of and commentary on the MMK carried the tradition into analytic philosophy's working vocabulary, and Madhyamaka positions on emptiness, dependence, and the two truths now appear in cross-cultural work on metaphysics and logic alongside their Western counterparts.
