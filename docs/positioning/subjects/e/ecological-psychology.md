# Ecological Psychology

Ecological psychology is the tradition in perceptual psychology that takes the animal–environment relation, not the individual mind, as its unit of analysis. Its home question is what perception is: not the construction of an inner picture from impoverished sensory input, but the direct pickup of information that is already structured — the ambient array reaching an active animal specifies the layout of its environment and the animal's own movement through it, and perceiving is detecting that specification. The position was built by [James J. Gibson](/positioning/persons/g/gibson-j/) across three books culminating in *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979), with the tradition's account of learning supplied by [Eleanor J. Gibson](/positioning/persons/g/gibson-e/); both principals are treated in full on their own pages.

---

## Roots

The position did not start with its conclusion. Behind it stand the new realism and radical empiricism that reached James Gibson through his mentor Edwin Holt — a lineage running back through William James, and further back to [Reid](/positioning/persons/r/reid/)'s direct realism; the Gestalt psychology of his Smith College colleague Kurt Koffka, whose conviction that the world is perceived already meaningful Gibson kept while rejecting the framework around it; and the wartime aviation research that broke the laboratory's frozen stimuli against the problem of what a pilot actually sees. The formation is told in full on the [James Gibson](/positioning/persons/g/gibson-j/) page; the point here is that the tradition's realism and its insistence on the moving observer both arrived with histories.

## The commitments

**Direct perception.** The founding claim is a denial: nothing needs to be added to the stimulation. The classical argument for inner construction rested on the poverty of the input — a flat, ambiguous retinal image demanding supplementation. The ecological reply is that the input was mismeasured: for a moving animal in a normal environment the ambient array is rich, its invariants under transformation specify their sources, and the ambiguity that made inference seem necessary is an artefact of frozen laboratory stimuli.

**Ecological optics and the environment.** Gibson gave the claim its own physics and its own ontology. Ecological optics describes light at the scale at which animals meet it — not rays and retinal images but an ambient optic array, structured by surfaces, sampled from a moving point of observation. And the environment it describes is not the physicist's space of bodies in a void but medium, substances, and surfaces — a furnished world described at the scale at which an animal lives in it. The 1979 book spends its opening chapters on this account of what the environment *is* for an animal before saying a word about perception.

**Affordances.** What is perceived, first of all, is what the environment offers the animal — climb-ability, graspability, passage, shelter. An affordance is a real but relational property: fixed jointly by the environment's layout and the animal's capabilities, the same surface affording walking to one animal and not to another. The concept was built to cut across the objective–subjective dichotomy: neither a physical property indifferent to the animal nor a value projected onto the world, but a fact of their relation.

**Self-specification.** The array specifies the perceiver along with the world: optic flow carries where one is heading, occlusion carries where one's own body ends, and in general the information for the environment and the information for the self arrive together. On this account there is no perceiving the world without co-perceiving oneself in it.

**Perception–action coupling.** Perception and action form one continuous system: the array is only rich for an animal that moves, and movement is only controlled through what is perceived. The senses are active, exploratory systems, not passive channels.

**Learning as differentiation.** The acquisition side of the tradition is Eleanor Gibson's: perceptual learning is coming to detect distinctions and structure always present in the stimulation but not yet picked up — including the affordances themselves, which infants learn to perceive as new action capabilities open new information. Development on this picture is a spiral of perceiving and acting rather than a timetable of stages.

## Schools and afterlife

**The formalising line.** Michael Turvey, Robert Shaw, and colleagues restated the framework in the language of dynamical systems and lawful information, founding the programme now called ecological dynamics. The tradition's institutions grew around this line: the International Society for Ecological Psychology and the journal *Ecological Psychology*.

**Radical embodied cognitive science.** Anthony Chemero's line extends the framework past perception, arguing that dynamical explanation without representation can reach cognition generally — an active programme that takes the tradition into territory its founders only gestured at, and whose reach is itself one of the field's open questions.

**The empirical afterlife.** The framework's concepts run through applied research on both its sides. From the perception–action side: optic flow in aviation and driving; David Lee's tau and the time-to-contact literature; ecological dynamics as a working framework in sport science and coaching, where affordance-based training is now standard vocabulary. From the acquisition side, the developmental line [Eleanor Gibson](/positioning/persons/g/gibson-e/)'s programme generated: the infant locomotion research of Karen Adolph, and its meeting with Esther Thelen's dynamic-systems account of motor development — learning studied posture by posture, as new action capabilities open new information.

For all of that, the tradition remains a minority position in its own field: vision science runs largely on computational and, increasingly, Bayesian lines, and the ecological school — with its society, its journal, and its applied reach — works from outside that mainstream, not within it.

**Reception.** Ulric Neisser, whose *Cognitive Psychology* (1967) named the field the Gibsons dissented from, later moved substantially toward the ecological position. His *Cognition and Reality* (1976) built the perceptual cycle — schema-directed exploration picking up information that in turn modifies the schema — taking information pickup into his framework while keeping its internal structure, and criticising Gibson on several points as he did so.

**Appropriations.** Donald Norman carried "affordance" into design in 1988, where it took on a shifted meaning — the perceivable suggestion of use rather than the relational fact — and spread through human–computer interaction, robotics, architecture, and sports science, largely detached from the ecological ontology; Norman himself later introduced "signifier" to undo the blur. The term now does load-bearing work in fields that have never read Gibson.

## The dispute with the computational mainstream

The tradition's defining external conflict began in earnest with Jerry [Fodor](/positioning/persons/f/fodor/) and Zenon Pylyshyn's "How direct is visual perception?" (1981): without a prior constraint on what counts as an invariant, they argued, "direct pickup" is either trivially true or does hidden inferential work. David Marr, from the computational side, credited the ecological questions but held that Gibson had underestimated the information-processing problem of detecting invariants. Ecological psychologists answer that both critiques reinstate the impoverished stimulus the approach began by rejecting.

The dispute's current form is with predictive processing and the free-energy line — the Helmholtzian inference picture returned in probabilistic dress, with perception as a generative model corrected against input ([Friston](/positioning/persons/f/friston/)). Ecological psychologists press the same reply against it — the models are doing work the information already does — while some in both camps explore reconciliations; the argument is active now, not historical. The tradition's nearest philosophical kin is [enactivism](/positioning/subjects/e/enactivism/).

## Live disputes

- **Whether information is lawful.** The formalising school built on *specification* — a lawful, one-to-one relation between array structure and its source, which is what makes pickup direct. Critics inside and outside the tradition argue that much of the information animals actually use is reliable rather than lawful; if the relation is reliable-but-defeasible, direct pickup becomes a claim about typical conditions rather than about the structure of the array, and how much of the founding claim survives the weakening separates the strict school from looser usage. The other disputes shift with this one.
- **What affordances are.** The tradition's most visible internal argument, and downstream of the first. Turvey and colleagues treated affordances as dispositions of the environment, complemented by the animal's effectivities — a reading that needs the lawful base; Chemero argues they are relations, existing only between animal and environment, which does not; Edward Reed treated them as resources, available in the environment prior to any animal and selecting for the perceptual systems that exploit them. The question is unresolved, and each answer redraws what the founding concept commits the tradition to.
- **How far the framework travels.** Whether ecological explanation reaches past perception — to memory, imagination, language — divides those who keep the framework where its founders largely did from the radical line that extends it; the extension's prospects are argued case by case.
