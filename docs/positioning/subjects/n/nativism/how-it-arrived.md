# How the Endowment Arrived

If the language faculty is a biological endowment, it evolved — and [nativists](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/) disagree fundamentally about how. The disagreement is internal to the programme, not between it and its critics: the positions below are all held by people who accept that acquisition requires an innate, language-specific endowment, and who differ on where it came from.

The dispute takes its shape from a constraint the field states openly. Universal Grammar must be **rich** enough to overcome the poverty of the stimulus and **restricted** enough to have plausibly evolved — and the [retreat toward Merge](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/the-retreat/) was substantially driven by the second demand. How much is left to explain therefore depends on how much the theory still claims, which is why the evolution question and the content question move together.

---

## The sudden origin

Noam [Chomsky](/positioning/persons/c/chomsky/) and Robert Berwick argue that the faculty arose abruptly and recently. Since minimalism holds the faculty to be minimal, what had to evolve is minimal: **Merge**, arriving through a chance discontinuity — a minor rewiring in a small population, somewhere between the emergence of anatomically modern humans and the African diaspora, on the order of a hundred thousand years ago or less.

The account is explicitly **saltationist**, and explicitly anti-gradualist. On their view there is no room for precursors: no language-like system with only short sentences, no intermediate stage with a weaker version of the operation. Merge either applies recursively or it does not, and there is no half-Merge.

It also holds that language did not evolve **for communication** at all. Language is primarily an *instrument of thought*, with externalisation into speech a secondary and ancillary matter — a problem of linking the system to the sensory-motor apparatus, solved afterwards and in different ways by different populations. Merge could therefore be selected for internally, through better planning and inference in a single individual, with no communicative function required at the outset. Chomsky's long-standing scepticism about adaptationist explanation, shared with Stephen Jay Gould, sits behind this.

---

## The gradual adaptation

Steven Pinker and Paul Bloom argue the opposite, from within nativism. In "Natural Language and Natural Selection" (1990) they hold that grammar is innate, specialised, and evolved the ordinary way: **gradually, by natural selection, for communication**.

Their argument is from design. Grammar is a complex mechanism tailored to transmitting propositional structures through a serial channel — the kind of intricate functional design for which natural selection is the only known explanation in biology. To exempt language from that explanation is to make it uniquely mysterious among complex adaptations. Their target was explicitly the Chomsky-and-Gould position that universal grammar confers no advantage and so cannot have been selected for, along with the by-product and exaptation stories.

The gradualist wing accepts intermediate stages as a consequence: proto-languages of increasing complexity, each conferring some communicative advantage on its holders, with the endowment accumulating incrementally.

## FLN and FLB

Marc Hauser, Chomsky and W. Tecumseh Fitch (2002) proposed the distinction the debate now largely runs on. The **faculty of language in the broad sense** (FLB) comprises everything language recruits — the sensory-motor systems, the conceptual-intentional systems, memory — much of it evolutionarily old and shared with other animals. The **faculty of language in the narrow sense** (FLN) is the uniquely human residue, and their hypothesis was that it might be **recursion alone**, recently evolved, and possibly not an adaptation for communication.

The proposal was intended partly as a research programme: sorting which components are shared with other species is an empirical question, answerable by comparative work, and most of the faculty may turn out to be old.

**Pinker and Jackendoff** (2005) attacked the recursion-only hypothesis directly. Recursion is not unique to language; and phonology, morphology, and the human conceptual system are each special, each show design for communication, and are each left out by an FLN containing only recursion. **Fitch, Hauser and Chomsky** (2005) replied that the critics had conflated the two senses of the faculty and had misread the recursion-only claim as an assertion rather than a hypothesis to be tested.

The exchange is the programme's live internal fault line, and it remains unresolved.

## The criticisms of the sudden origin

The saltationist account has drawn sustained criticism, including from those otherwise sympathetic to generative linguistics.

- **It skips the words.** The account grants that words preceded grammar but says little about how words themselves evolved — and a theory of the origin of syntax that presupposes a lexicon has not explained the harder half.
- **It severs syntax from behaviour.** By cutting the faculty off from speech and communicative behaviour, the account removes the mechanism through which selection could act at all, since changes in behaviour are what generate the selection pressures that reshape structures.
- **The internal benefit is too vague.** "Better planning and inference" is not a specific enough advantage to constitute a selection story. Critics who accept a thought-first account tend to propose something more concrete — Derek Bickerton's *displaced reference*, the ability to communicate about what is not present, being the standard candidate.
- **The single mutation does not follow.** That Merge is atomic as an operation does not entail that it arrived in one genetic event. External and internal Merge might have arrived separately, requiring at least two.
- **The vocal side is underexplained.** The account's treatment of how speech itself became possible leans on sexual selection through song, which critics hold lacks evidence and understates the neuroanatomical demands of speech.
- **The atoms are conceded.** Berwick and Chomsky write that the origin of human-specific concepts and of the atoms of computation that Merge operates on remains, for them, a mystery — which critics take to be the central problem rather than a remainder. The [dispute over concepts](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/) is where that question lives.

## Relocating the structure

Bearing on the dispute from outside nativism are accounts that make a rich endowment unnecessary by locating the structure somewhere other than the genome.

**Terrence Deacon**'s *The Symbolic Species* argues that language and brain co-evolved through **niche construction**: ancestors built a symbolic environment, and that environment generated the selection pressures that shaped minds able to use it. Deacon's further move is a **reverse Baldwin effect** — genetic specification *decreasing* rather than increasing over time, as the linguistic environment becomes reliable enough to carry the structure itself, so that what the environment supplies the genome need not.

**Morten Christiansen and Nick Chater** invert the arrow: **language evolved to be learnable**, rather than brains evolving to learn language. Language is the fast-moving system, shaped by the constraints of the brains it must pass through in every generation; cultural evolution outruns biological evolution, and can prevent biological adaptation to arbitrary linguistic features. The Baldwin effect, on the evidence, works for functional but not arbitrary features — so the assumption that learned structure gets assimilated into the genome is not freely available.

## Key works

- Steven Pinker & Paul Bloom, "Natural Language and Natural Selection" (1990) — the adaptationist case
- Marc Hauser, Noam Chomsky & W. Tecumseh Fitch, "The Faculty of Language: What Is It, Who Has It, and How Did It Evolve?" (2002) — the FLN/FLB distinction
- Steven Pinker & Ray Jackendoff, "The Faculty of Language: What's Special About It?" (2005) — the reply; and Fitch, Hauser & Chomsky, "The Evolution of the Language Faculty: Clarifications and Implications" (2005)

- Robert Berwick & Noam Chomsky, *Why Only Us: Language and Evolution* (2016) — the saltationist account
