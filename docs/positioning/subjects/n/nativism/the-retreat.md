# What Is Claimed to Be Innate

[Nativism](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/) holds that acquiring a language requires an innate endowment specific to language. What that endowment is held to *contain* has changed across sixty years, and has moved in one direction: downward. The stages below are the programme's own successive answers, each a response to problems in the last.

---

## Transformational grammar — rich rule systems

The Standard Theory of *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965) posited extensive innate rule schemas together with an **evaluation metric** for ranking candidate grammars. The child, on this account, is in effect searching a space of possible grammars and evaluating them against the data — the endowment supplying both the space and the criterion.

The difficulty that pressed on it was descriptive adequacy against learnability. The richer and more specific the rule systems needed to capture actual languages, the harder it became to explain how a child selects among them from the evidence available, and the more the endowment had to carry.

---

## Principles and Parameters — switch-setting

The Government and Binding framework (*Lectures on Government and Binding*, 1981) replaced the search with something more economical, and it is the model most people outside the field still picture when they picture universal grammar.

**Universal principles** hold across all languages and need no learning at all — they are simply how any human grammar works. Variation is confined to a finite set of binary **parameters** whose settings exposure fixes: whether the language puts heads before or after complements, whether it permits subjects to be dropped, and so on. Acquisition stops being rule-induction and becomes **switch-setting**. A child hearing a few decisive sentences fixes a parameter, and a cascade of surface consequences follows from that one setting.

The gain is in acquisition: the child no longer induces a grammar, it selects among pre-specified options. The price is one the field states openly — **whatever is not learned must be endowed**. The richer the principles, the heavier the debt owed to evolution, since every principle is biological structure that had to arrive somehow.

Principles and Parameters is no longer the theoretical basis of Chomskyan linguistics, though it remains widely used in descriptive and comparative syntactic work, where the parameter vocabulary continues to earn its place.

---

## The Minimalist Program — Merge, and as little else as possible

The Minimalist Program (from 1993) is the response to that debt. It strips the language-specific component toward a minimum, in the limit to a single operation.

**Merge** takes two syntactic objects and combines them into an unordered hierarchical set, with the output available as input to the next application. That recursive re-entry is what yields unbounded hierarchical structure from one operation. On the strongest version, syntax *is* Merge.

Everything else is pushed outward. The interfaces carry what they can — the **conceptual-intentional** system on the meaning side, the **sensory-motor** system on the sound side — and general principles of computational efficiency carry the rest. The **Strong Minimalist Thesis** holds that language is an optimal solution to the problem of linking meaning and sound: not a system with arbitrary design features to be catalogued, but close to the only system that could satisfy the conditions the interfaces impose. Older systems — semantics, the phonological-motor apparatus — are held not to be linguistic at all, which reduces what has to be language-specific in the first place.

---

## The motive

The direction of travel is not disputed within the programme, and neither is its motive. Universal Grammar must be **rich** enough to overcome the [poverty of the stimulus](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/) — the thinner the child's evidence, the more the endowment must supply — and **restricted** enough to have plausibly evolved, since a large, intricate, language-dedicated module is precisely the kind of thing that is hard to get from evolution in the time available. Those two goals pull in opposite directions. Minimalism is the attempt to satisfy the second without abandoning the first, and the [dispute over how the endowment arrived](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/how-it-arrived/) is where the pressure shows most directly.

Whether the retreat succeeds is contested from both sides. Critics hold that the properties minimalism offloads onto interfaces and general principles have not actually been derived from them — that shrinking the stated endowment is not the same as showing the work can be done without it. Its proponents hold that the programme is a research direction rather than a finished theory, and that the reduction is the thing to be pursued.

---

## Jackendoff's parallel architecture

The substantial nativist dissent, and an alternative to the whole shape above rather than a variation within it.

Ray Jackendoff's **parallel architecture** rejects syntax-first organisation — the picture in which syntax is the generative engine and phonology and semantics are interpretive. Instead it posits several **independent generative systems** — syntax, semantics, morphology, phonology — each with its own combinatorial machinery, linked by **interface rules** that establish correspondences between structures in one and structures in another.

The consequences run against minimalism at several points. Innateness is **distributed** across systems rather than concentrated in one operation, so there is no single locus like Merge to have arrived in one event. The conceptual system has its own generative structure rather than merely receiving syntax's output. And the account requires a **longer evolutionary history** — an incremental accumulation of systems and interfaces rather than a single recent discontinuity — which aligns it with the adaptationist wing of the [evolution dispute](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/how-it-arrived/).
