# Nativism

Nativism is the position that significant structure of the mind is present in advance of experience rather than drawn from it — that a learner brings to the world more than the world could have given it. As a doctrine about knowledge it is ancient; as a research programme it is modern, and its centre of gravity is language, where the claim takes its sharpest and most defended form: that a child could not acquire a language from experience alone, and that acquisition therefore rests on an innate endowment specific to language. Founded in that form by Noam [Chomsky](/positioning/persons/c/chomsky/) in the late 1950s and dominant in theoretical linguistics for decades, it turned the study of language toward the mind and made the child's achievement — a complex grammar, acquired fast, from fragmentary evidence, without instruction — the central explanandum of the field. It remains live, defended, and contested, and its internal disagreements are as sharp as those between it and its critics. This page is chiefly about the linguistic programme; it marks the older lineage and the cognitive variants where they touch it.

**The rationalist prehistory.** The modern programme inherits an argument older than psychology: Plato's slave boy in the *Meno*, drawing out geometry he was never taught; [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/)'s innate ideas; Leibniz's reply to Locke that nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses — *except the intellect itself*; and Kant's categories, the forms any experience must already have to be an experience at all. Chomsky claimed the lineage explicitly in *Cartesian Linguistics* (1966), tracing a line through Descartes, the Port-Royal grammarians, and Humboldt, for whom language reveals the structure of mind. Critics of that book — including historians of the period — have disputed how much of the lineage is really his ancestor and how much is read back into it, but the philosophical shape of the argument is the same one: experience underdetermines what the mind ends up with, so the mind must bring the difference.

The vernacular version — "language is innate" — is not what anyone defends. No nativist claims a language is present at birth, or that a child knows grammatical rules the way it later knows facts. The careful claim is narrower and turns on a single word: a developed linguistic trait is innate if it cannot be explained by the learner's sensitivity to the environment, but must be explained by a **language-specific** learning system. **Domain-specificity is the whole dispute.** Everyone in the debate grants that some innate structure is at work — a rock does not learn Dutch, and the nativists' most prominent opponents are explicit that infants bring a great deal to the task. What divides the field is whether what does the work is dedicated to language or general-purpose. Arguments establishing that *some* endowment is needed do not by themselves establish nativism, and its proponents say so.

## The poverty of the stimulus

The argument at the centre, from which the rest follows. It runs: children come to know things about their language — call it the target; children encounter only limited and imperfect primary linguistic data; domain-general learning could not get from that data to that target; therefore something innate and specific to language must bridge the gap.

The supporting observations are the ones the tradition returns to. Children receive **positive evidence only** — nobody supplies a catalogue of the sentences that are impossible, and correction of grammar is rare and, when offered, largely ignored. The input is **degenerate** in scope and quality: fragments, false starts, errors, and nothing approaching a systematic curriculum. Different children encounter **different data** yet converge on the same grammar. And no other organism raised in the same environment acquires it.

The cases the argument is built on are specific, and each marks a place where a child's knowledge appears not to be in the evidence:

- **Structure-dependence** — the flagship. In forming a yes-no question, English moves the structurally correct auxiliary rather than the linearly first one. The sentences a child hears are consistent with both the structural rule and the simpler linear one, and yet children are reported never to try the linear rule.
- **Binding** — children know that *he* cannot refer to the Ninja Turtle in *He ate pizza while the Ninja Turtle was dancing*. Impossible interpretations are exactly what ordinary speech never marks.
- **Anaphoric *one*** — eighteen-month-olds take *one* to mean *red ball* rather than *ball*, though the input is systematically ambiguous between the two readings.
- **Island effects** — constraints on where a *wh*-question can extract from. Violations are absent from what a child hears; but so are the grammatical long-distance cases that would teach the contrast.
- **Phonology** — stress systems and plural allomorphy, where speakers generalise over natural classes that are not present as such in what they heard.
- **Semantics** — the problem of fixing what *dog* picks out; and attitude verbs such as *think* and *wonder*, whose meanings have no observable correlate in the situation of use.

The argument's burden is threefold and its proponents acknowledge it as non-trivial: establishing what adults know, what children actually encounter, and why general learning could not bridge the two. They note a corresponding burden on the other side — that rejecting the argument means answering it for every phenomenon, not for one.

**The emergence cases.** Where the poverty of the stimulus reasons from what is missing in the input, a second body of evidence turns on structure appearing that demonstrably was not there at all. **Creoles** arise within a generation from pidgins that lack settled grammar, and Derek Bickerton's *language bioprogram hypothesis* took their regularities as the innate endowment showing through where tradition had nothing to hand down. **Nicaraguan Sign Language** emerged among deaf children brought together in the 1970s and 1980s with no shared language between them, and grammaticalised over successive cohorts — the younger signers systematising what the older ones had not. **Homesign** systems, invented by deaf children without linguistic models, exhibit structural regularities their gesturing hearing parents do not supply (Susan Goldin-Meadow's work). These are nativism's most concrete positive bet: not an inference from thin evidence, but cases where the structure has no source in the environment to be traced to. The counter-readings hold that the children are not creating from nothing but drawing on general cognition, interactional pressures, and whatever the surrounding input does supply, and that the cohort effects show cultural transmission at work rather than a bioprogram unfolding.

## What is claimed to be innate

Nativism's answer to *what is innate* has changed across sixty years, and has moved in one direction: downward. The rich innate rule schemas of transformational grammar gave way to the universal principles and binary switch-settings of Principles and Parameters, and those in turn to the Minimalist Program, which strips the language-specific component toward a minimum — in the limit to **Merge** alone, a single operation combining two objects into a hierarchical set, with everything else pushed outward onto the interfaces with meaning and sound. The motive is not disputed within the programme: Universal Grammar must be rich enough to overcome the poverty of the stimulus and restricted enough to have plausibly evolved, and those goals pull opposite ways. Whether the reduction succeeds — whether what minimalism offloads has actually been derived from elsewhere — is contested, as is the syntax-first architecture itself, which Ray Jackendoff's parallel architecture rejects in favour of several independent generative systems linked by interfaces.

The stages, their motives, and Jackendoff's dissent are set out in [What is claimed to be innate](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/the-retreat/).

## Concepts

Merge combines objects; it does not supply them. What the objects are, and where they come from, is the least settled part of the programme, and nativists answer it in incompatible ways.

That *which* words a child ends up with comes from the environment is not in dispute — Leeds gives you *cat*, Ljubljana *mačka*. The mainstream nativist account of word-learning is accordingly about constraints rather than contents: a **whole-object bias**, a **taxonomic bias**, **mutual exclusivity**, and **syntactic bootstrapping**, in which the grammatical frame a word appears in helps fix its meaning. Innate heuristics guiding acquisition, not innate vocabulary.

**Chomsky's own position is considerably stronger**, and is an outlier among nativists. He argues from conceptual necessity that lexical concepts have properties too complex to have been extracted from experience, and so must in substance already be there, triggered rather than learned — the child discovering which sounds attach to concepts it already has. He extends this explicitly to culturally specific artefact words, holding that the argument goes through "at least in substantial measure" even for *carburetor* and *bureaucrat*. It is the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument applied to the lexicon.

**Jerry Fodor** took the position to its limit. On his **language of thought** hypothesis, thinking occurs in an inner symbolic system — *Mentalese* — that is innate, compositional, and prior to any natural language; a concept is a Mentalese word together with what it denotes. Natural language, on this account, is not the vehicle of thought but an encoding of it: learning a word is forming and testing a hypothesis about which already-held concept it denotes. His argument for concept nativism is that this is the only viable learning mechanism and that it is circular — testing a hypothesis about a property requires already possessing the concept of that property — so concepts cannot be learned, only triggered. Since Mentalese words are concepts, the mental language is innate. **Radical concept nativism** is the consequence he accepted: *car* is innate. The reception is a matter of record — [Putnam](/positioning/persons/p/putnam/) could barely conceal his astonishment, and [Dennett](/positioning/persons/d/dennett/) replied that on this view Aristotle had the concept of an aeroplane in his brain. The standard characterisation, from broadly sympathetic quarters, is that Fodor went too far.

**Core knowledge** is the main developmental alternative, and it is nativist about cognition rather than about language. On Elizabeth Spelke and Susan Carey's account — a programme that took its lead from Chomsky's modularity — human cognition begins from a small set of evolutionarily old **core systems**: objects, agents and actions, number, space, and social partners. They are domain-specific, encapsulated, present in preverbal infants, and shared in some form with other animals. What language does, on this account, is **combine core systems into uniquely human concepts**: the prelinguistic stock is real but fragmented, and language is the mechanism that integrates across the encapsulation boundaries, yielding abstract, culturally transmissible thought. This inverts Fodor exactly. For him, language externalises a conceptual system already complete; for Spelke, language is what does the combining.

Against all of these stand the accounts that deny thought is language-like at all: the empiricists' **ideas** combining by association, and their contemporary descendants in image-like representation (Prinz, [Barsalou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Barsalou)) and mental maps (Armstrong); **connectionism**, on which networks model cognition without discrete symbols, against which Fodor and Pylyshyn argued that only compositional symbol systems explain the productivity and systematicity of thought; and Patricia Churchland's eliminativism, which rejects representational mental states outright.

## How the endowment arrived

If the endowment is biological, it evolved — and nativists divide internally on how, the disagreement running between people who all accept the endowment rather than between the programme and its critics. **Chomsky and Robert Berwick** argue for a sudden, minimal origin: since the faculty reduces to Merge, one chance rewiring could deliver it, with no precursors and no gradual approach — and language, on their account, evolved as an instrument of thought rather than for communication, with externalisation secondary. **Steven Pinker and Paul Bloom** argue the reverse from within nativism: grammar is complex functional design, and gradual natural selection **for communication** is the only known explanation for such design. The **recursion-only hypothesis** — Hauser, Chomsky and Fitch's proposal that the uniquely human component might be recursion alone — is the live fault line, attacked by Pinker and Jackendoff and defended in reply, and the question is open.

The positions, the FLN/FLB distinction and the exchange it provoked, the criticisms of the sudden origin, and the accounts that relocate the structure out of the genome altogether are set out in [How the endowment arrived](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/how-it-arrived/).

## The challenges

**That the stimulus is not that poor.** Geoffrey Pullum and Barbara Scholz questioned whether the input is impoverished as claimed and whether specific poverty-of-the-stimulus arguments have their empirical basis; corpus work on child-directed speech has been offered to show the relevant patterns are present more often than the argument allows; Reali and Christiansen argued the point for structure-dependence specifically. Berwick, Chomsky and Massimo Piattelli-Palmarini replied at length that the poverty of the stimulus stands and that the critics' cases do not hold.

**That general learning suffices.** [Statistical learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_learning_in_language_acquisition) shows infants extracting far more from distributional structure than the argument assumes. Connectionist and neural-network models learn hierarchical structure without an explicit constraint built in. Bayesian inference, analogy, and construction grammar have all been proposed as domain-general mechanisms sufficient to the task.

**That the endowment is not language-specific.** [Tomasello](/positioning/persons/t/tomasello/)'s usage-based account is the sharpest form of this, and it attacks precisely the line the debate turns on. It grants substantial innate structure — intention-reading, joint attention, pattern-finding, the capacity for shared intentionality — and denies that any of it is dedicated to language. Grammar is built from use, piecemeal, from concrete instances toward abstract schemas. Alongside it, construction and cognitive grammar (Goldberg, Langacker, Lakoff) contest the autonomy of syntax.

**That the universals are not there.** The typological attack, aimed at the claim rather than at the argument for it. [Daniel Everett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Everett) argued from long fieldwork on Pirahã that the language lacks recursion — against the claim that recursion is the one uniquely human, universal component — and the dispute over his data and its interpretation has been protracted and unresolved. More broadly, Nicholas Evans and Stephen Levinson's "The Myth of Language Universals" (2009) marshalled typological diversity to argue that the proposed universals dissolve on inspection: languages vary at every level, the exceptionless generalisations are few and mostly trivial, and what recurs is better explained by shared function and cultural-historical descent than by an innate template. Generativists reply that surface diversity was never the claim — universal grammar constrains the space of possible grammars, not the observable inventory — and that typological counter-examples must be shown to violate the principles rather than merely to look unusual.

**That the target is mis-described.** Whether the knowledge attributed to adult speakers is real, or whether apparently sophisticated competence reflects simpler learned patterns.

**Circularity.** Howard Lasnik's formulation of a worry the field records against itself: identifying the units a theory of language ranges over already relies on notions like *utterance* and *word* — the big step is going from noise to word. If picking out linguistic units presupposes linguistic categories, the theory may assume what it set out to explain.

**Explanatory emptiness.** Paul Churchland's objection that calling a trait innate without explaining its origin is not an explanation but a way of declining to give one.

**Development.** Parameter-setting predicts something like switch-flips; children's competence in fact emerges piecemeal and gradually. Generativists appeal to maturational and performance factors, which critics hold to be underspecified.

**Morphology and semantics.** Acquisition in these domains tracks input frequency closely — the pattern empiricist learning predicts — and nativist accounts here fall back on constraints rather than innate content.

**Large language models.** The current front, and fast-moving. Steven Piantadosi has argued that large language models undermine virtually every strong claim for the innateness of language, since they acquire sophisticated syntactic competence from exposure alone with no universal grammar. The standard reply is that these models are trained on orders of magnitude more data than a child encounters, and so do not touch a claim that is precisely about the poverty of the child's input. Generativist responses have been published and the exchange is ongoing.

**Third-way proposals** hold that statistical sensitivity and innate grammar interact rather than compete — the faculty comprising complementary components for storage and computation, its categories both innate and learned.

Bearing on the debate from outside it are accounts that make a rich endowment unnecessary by relocating the structure — Terrence Deacon's co-evolution of language and brain through niche construction, and Christiansen and Chater's inversion, on which language evolved to be learnable rather than brains evolving to learn language. These are taken up alongside the evolution dispute in [How the endowment arrived](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/how-it-arrived/).

## Key works and further reading

- Plato, *Meno* — the slave boy, and recollection as the first form of the argument
- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *New Essays on Human Understanding* (written 1704) — the reply to Locke: nothing in the intellect that was not first in the senses, except the intellect itself
- Noam Chomsky, "Review of B.F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*" (1959) — the emblematic opening of the case against learning-by-conditioning
- Noam Chomsky, *Cartesian Linguistics* (1966) — the claim to the rationalist lineage
- Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975) — Mentalese and the case for concept nativism
- Derek Bickerton, *Roots of Language* (1981) — creoles and the language bioprogram hypothesis
- Susan Goldin-Meadow, *The Resilience of Language* (2003) — homesign and what children invent without a model
- Michael Tomasello, *Constructing a Language* (2003) — the usage-based alternative
- Elizabeth Spelke & Katherine Kinzler, "Core Knowledge" (2007) — the developmental alternative
- Nicholas Evans & Stephen Levinson, "The Myth of Language Universals" (2009) — the typological case against universals
- [*Innateness and Language*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/innateness-language/), Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — an authoritative map of the debate and its varieties
- [*The Language of Thought Hypothesis*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/language-thought/), Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
