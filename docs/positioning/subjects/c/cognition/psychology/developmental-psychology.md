# Developmental Psychology — the Three Streams

Of psychology's many branches, developmental psychology is the one that studies the acquiring itself: how knowledge and understanding are built up through experience, watched longitudinally as it happens. To the question at its centre — how does the acquiring work? — the field has given three rival answers, and the dispute among them found its arena in language acquisition: the one capability every child acquires, on schedule, from wildly varying input. Each stream is treated in full on its own pages; here each appears as its answer to the question.

## Shaping from outside

[Behaviourism](/positioning/subjects/b/behaviourism/) answered that the acquiring is done *to* the child. [Skinner](/positioning/persons/s/skinner/) extended the framework to its most ambitious case in *Verbal Behavior* (1957): language as behaviour like any other, shaped by conditioning and differential reinforcement, with no inner process worth studying — the organism's history of consequences carries the whole explanation. [Chomsky](/positioning/persons/c/chomsky/)'s 1959 review of that book became the hinge on which the field turned: the argument that shaping from outside cannot account for what children actually produce — novel sentences never uttered before, errors never modelled for them — marked the limit of the answer and opened the door to the study of internal process.

## The endowment unfolding

[Nativism](/positioning/subjects/n/nativism/) answered that the acquiring is largely an unfolding of what is already there. Chomsky's poverty-of-stimulus argument holds that the input a child receives is too thin and too noisy to determine the grammar every child nonetheless converges on; what bridges the gap is an innate endowment — in its strongest form a universal grammar, a built-in structure that experience triggers and tunes rather than builds. On this answer, experience selects among possibilities the child brought along; the deep structure of the capability was never acquired at all.

## The builder in the encounter

The third stream answered that the acquiring is the child's own construction, worked in the encounter with world and others — and it carries the substance of the field's positive account.

[Piaget](/positioning/persons/p/piaget/) found the acquiring constructive. Knowledge is not received but built: the child assimilates what experience brings into the structures of understanding it already has, and accommodates those structures when the new will not fit — and out of that two-way work the structures themselves grow, stage by stage. The process builds its own capability as it runs: what has been acquired shapes what can be acquired next.

[Vygotsky](/positioning/persons/v/vygotsky/) found the acquiring socially fed. The capabilities that end up running privately — inner speech, deliberate memory, reasoning — arrive first as shared activity: carried in language, in tools, in interaction with others who can already do what the child cannot yet do alone. What becomes the individual's own was internalised from the shared.

[Tomasello](/positioning/persons/t/tomasello/) brought the stream's answer to the arena itself. Language is learned from use: the child reads what others mean — joint attention, intention-reading — and builds grammar up out of concrete instances, pattern by pattern, with no innate grammar required. The usage-based account meets nativism on its home ground, offering a constructive answer to the very case the poverty-of-stimulus argument was built on.

---

What the three streams bring to cognition, taken together, is the question itself made empirical: whether the acquiring is imposed, unfolded, or built is not settled by definition but watched, in children, longitudinally. And what the watching keeps returning is activity — at every point where the streams have met the evidence, the child has turned out to be doing something: extracting, constructing, reading intentions, reworking structure. The acquiring has an owner.
