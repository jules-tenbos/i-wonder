# Developmental Psychology

Of psychology's many branches, developmental psychology is the one that studies the acquiring itself: how knowledge and understanding are built up through experience, watched longitudinally as it happens. Two of its founders carry the substance, and both are treated in full on their own pages.

[Piaget](/positioning/persons/p/piaget/) found the acquiring constructive. Knowledge is not received but built: the child assimilates what experience brings into the structures of understanding it already has, and accommodates those structures when the new will not fit — and out of that two-way work the structures themselves grow, stage by stage. The process builds its own capability as it runs: what has been acquired shapes what can be acquired next.

[Vygotsky](/positioning/persons/v/vygotsky/) found the acquiring socially fed. The capabilities that end up running privately — inner speech, deliberate memory, reasoning — arrive first as shared activity: carried in language, in tools, in interaction with others who can already do what the child cannot yet do alone. What becomes the individual's own was internalised from the shared.

Between them the two findings give psychology's angle on cognition its shape: the process is watched building itself, and it builds from both sides — constructing its structures in the encounter with the world, and drawing its means from what others already hold.

For context: the word's broader career in psychology — the cognitive revolution that re-founded the discipline as the study of internal processes, and the information-processing framework that followed — is carried by the [behaviourism](/positioning/subjects/b/behaviourism/) page and the pages of [Chomsky](/positioning/persons/c/chomsky/) and [Fodor](/positioning/persons/f/fodor/).
