# Cognition — The Sciences

Two scientific claims on the word pull in opposite directions. Psychology made cognition a science by fencing it — internal processes, paradigmatically human, studied as information processing. Biology's claim runs the other way: that the fence was the mistake, and cognition is a property of life — perhaps of any system that maintains itself against its surroundings. The dispute between them is the extension dispute in its sharpest scientific form.

---

## The word's institutional home

The word's modern home is psychology. The **cognitive revolution** of the 1950s and 60s displaced [behaviourism](/positioning/subjects/b/behaviourism/)'s refusal to look inside the organism, and re-founded psychology as the study of internal processes — perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning — with [Ulric Neisser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulric_Neisser)'s *Cognitive Psychology* (1967) naming the field and [Chomsky](/positioning/persons/c/chomsky/)'s attack on Skinner as one of its founding documents. Its strongest theoretical form is **cognitivism**: the mind as an information-processing system, cognition as computation over internal representations — a picture given philosophical rigour by [Fodor](/positioning/persons/f/fodor/)'s language of thought and computational theory of mind. On this picture the dictionary's "mental" is doing honest work: cognition is what minds (paradigmatically human minds, and whatever relevantly resembles them) do, and the research programme is to specify the processing. The interdisciplinary umbrella of **cognitive science** — psychology, AI, linguistics, neuroscience, philosophy, anthropology — was built largely on this foundation, and much of the field still works within it.

## Life's claim on the word

The sharpest extension dispute is biology's. [Von Uexküll](/positioning/persons/v/von-uexkull/)'s *Umwelt* gave every animal its own world of significance, built by its own sensory and motor repertoire — as many worlds as kinds of subject. The **Santiago theory** of [Maturana](/positioning/persons/m/maturana/) and [Varela](/positioning/persons/v/varela/) states the widest claim in the literature: living as a process *is* a process of cognition — a living system, by maintaining itself, already knows its world in the only sense that matters, nervous system or none. [Piaget](/positioning/persons/p/piaget/)'s genetic epistemology made knowledge a biological achievement — assimilation and accommodation as the organism's adaptation carried into thought. [J. J. Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_J._Gibson)'s ecological psychology put the significance in the environment itself — *affordances*, what the surroundings offer the animal — against the inner-processing picture. And the contemporary **basal cognition** programme ([Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_cognition), Levin and others) pursues the claim empirically below the nervous system: sensing, memory, valence and problem-solving in cells, plants and tissues. Each of these forces the definitional question — if a cell or a colony does this, is it cognition or metaphor? — and the field has no agreed answer.

## Territory still to be taken up

The sciences' front keeps widening, and further pieces will take up its parts as they are engaged: the process models of the brain — selectionist, workspace, predictive — and their contest; machine learning and AI, whose systems now perform feats the word was built for; animal cognition and its vocabulary disputes; and the distributed and collective forms — crews, institutions, markets — where the process outruns any single head.
