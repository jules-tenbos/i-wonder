# The Seed and the Origin of Life

Working notes from a discussion on what the Splectrum seed principles say about the origin of life — and what they say cannot be said.

Inspired by the Santa Fe Institute podcast *The Information Theory of Biology and Origins of Life with Sara Walker*.

---

## The universal constructor

Von Neumann asked what minimal architecture a system needs to construct any configuration, including a copy of itself. His answer: a constructor that builds from instructions, a set of instructions describing what to build, and a copying mechanism that duplicates those instructions for the offspring.

The crucial insight is the dual role of the description. The instructions must function in two completely different modes: as something to be read and executed (the constructor follows them), and as something to be copied passively (duplicated as data, handed to the offspring uninterpreted). Without this duality, you get construction without reproduction or copying without construction. Self-reproduction requires both.

Molecular biology confirmed the logic before the biology was fully understood. DNA is read (transcribed into RNA, translated into proteins) and copied (replicated). The ribosome is the constructor. The genome is the description. The replication machinery handles the copying.

Von Neumann's dual-role observation is correct. But it only goes that deep. It is not turtles all the way down. There is a lowest level.

## The universal constructor as outside view

The concept of a universal constructor is a product of the outside view. It is defined by asking: what must *any* self-reproducing system look like? That question is only askable from a position that surveys all possible self-reproducing systems — the view from nowhere. No actual system is universal. Every actual system is a specific differentiation that happened to become self-sustaining.

The term is doubly an outside-view concept. First, "universal" requires surveying all instances. Second, "constructor" presupposes a language to construct in. A constructor operates within an already-given set of differentiations — it follows instructions, manipulates materials, all within a language. But the origin question is about the moment before any language is given. The constructor metaphor breaks at the point it is most needed.

## P0 cuts differently

P0 — being implies language — is creational. Being comes into existence through the act of differentiation. There is no being without not-being. The boundary is the creation.

P0 does not say "something constructed the language." It says being and language arise together through differentiation. The differentiation is not performed by a constructor working in a prior language. It is the creational event. No prior language, no prior constructor. The boundary and what it separates come into existence in the same act.

An analogy: higher-level programming languages create "concepts" from what is already present in the binary language. The concepts do not add new power (P5 — complexity grows in expression, not in power). They create named differentiations within what was already computationally present. The bootstrapping problem then becomes having the right combination of such differentiations to kick-start a self-sustaining system. But that is always a specific kick-start, not a universal one.

If one insists on keeping the universal constructor concept, the only honest place to put it is the undifferentiated itself. The undifferentiated already contains every possible differentiation — every possible language, every possible self-sustaining configuration. Not as a blueprint, not as instructions, not as architecture. Just as possibility. The full power was always there. What grows is expression, not power.

The "universal constructor" is a name for the ground, but a misleading one. "Constructor" imports architecture, instruction-following, mechanism — all features of specific differentiations, not of the undifferentiated itself. The undifferentiated does not construct. Specific differentiations arise from it — each one a P0 event, each one a particular language becoming.

## The vacuum analogy

Consider how physics understands the vacuum. It is not nothing. There is energy, there are virtual particles. But this is not a universal description. It is an inside view — insight obtained within the language of quantum field theory, without us being able to judge what other ways of engaging the same ground might exist.

The vacuum in quantum field theory is already a differentiated concept. It is what "nothing" looks like through physics. It has structure, fluctuations, symmetry. All real, but real within a specific language. P2 — the subject experiences reality through their language — applies. Physics is a language. The vacuum is what the undifferentiated looks like when physics looks at it.

The claim that this is what reality "really is at the fundamental level" is a claim for meta-privilege — the move P4 denies. The empirical success of physics shows the language works brilliantly within its own domain. It says nothing about what other differentiations might access the same undifferentiated ground in ways we cannot currently conceive.

This is the structural error that repeats at every scale: mistaking the limits of one's language for the limits of what is. The same move that led us to deny intelligence in animals because we defined intelligence within our vocabulary and tested other beings against our criteria.

## Co-evolution of being and the other

P0 says the boundary creates both sides. In origin of life research, attention falls almost entirely on the "life" side — what molecule, what membrane, what replication mechanism. The other side is treated as "conditions" or "environment," as if it were passive scenery.

But the other is not a static stage. It co-evolves. Where you place the P0 boundary determines what story you see.

Earth's mineral diversity expanded from roughly 250 species early on to over 5000 today. Most of that diversification was driven by biological activity — oxidation, biomineralization. Life changed the other, and the changed other enabled new forms of life. The chemistry of the planet and the biology of the planet did not evolve independently. They co-created each other across the boundary.

This means "origin of life" is already a misleading framing. It presupposes a clean separation: dead chemistry, then a transition, then life. But if the other co-evolves with the being — if the mineral environment was already being shaped by proto-biological processes before anything we would call a cell existed — then there is no clean line. There is a gradient of co-evolving differentiations, and "life begins here" is a language choice, not a discovery.

The bias is structural. We frame the question from inside P2 — from the subject's perspective, the subject is the interesting part. The other is just what is out there. But P0 is indifferent to which side you stand on. The differentiation is one act, both sides equally created.

## The wrapper and the recipe

If there were conditions on Earth that allowed for stable bounded existence — liquid held within a membrane — and the right chemistry in the environment to build a constructor and propagate it, those would be considered origin of life conditions. The wrapper (persistent container) and the recipe (constructive chemistry) co-arise. Without the wrapper, the differentiations dissipate. Without the recipe, the wrapper degrades.

This is structurally close to autopoiesis — the observation that a living system produces and maintains its own boundary. But arrived at from P0 rather than from biology, the pattern is not restricted to cells. A computer is the same shape: a persistent container housing differentiations that maintain the system's operational identity.

And the "against a specific other" matters. The background is not neutral. What the differentiation differentiates from constrains what differentiations can persist. Earth chemistry, temperature ranges, energy gradients — these are not context. They are the other half of the P0 event. Different other, different possible beings.

## The boundary is fuzzy, contingent, and multiple

Where is the exact boundary between not-yet-life and life? It will be fuzzy and probably multiple. Multiple proto-life configurations would have bubbled up and collapsed, some persisting longer than others, some merging, some competing, none cleanly "the first living thing." The boundary between the last non-living configuration and the first living one is drawn retrospectively, in our language, from our side of billions of years of evolution.

This is the cognition boundary problem in a different domain. When does chemistry become life? Same answer as when does processing become cognition: the boundary is a perspectival artefact, not an ontological line. We experience being alive from inside, so the transition feels like it must be a threshold — a spark, a moment. But that feeling is P2 at work. The subject experiences through its own language, and from inside that language the boundary looks absolute.

Neither life nor consciousness is a magical transition. They are what self-sustaining differentiations look like from the inside at sufficient complexity. The magic is an artefact of perspective, not of ontology.

## What the seed says

The origin of life, honestly asked through the seed:

- **P0**: The origin is a creational event — a specific differentiation becoming self-sustaining against a specific other. Not a universal mechanism. Not a constructor following instructions. A boundary that creates both sides simultaneously.
- **P1**: The first living system is relational through and through. What it is depends entirely on what it relates to — the chemistry, the environment, the energy gradients. No intrinsic properties; only relational ones.
- **P2**: Our account of the origin is always from inside our language. What we call "life," where we draw the boundary, what we consider the relevant variables — all of this is our language at work. Other languages might carve the territory differently.
- **P3**: Scientific consensus about the origin of life is shared reality — convergence among subjects working in shared languages. Legitimate, but not the view from nowhere.
- **P4**: The language of biochemistry, the language of geochemistry, the language of thermodynamics, the language of information theory — each gives access to different structure in the same territory. None is privileged. The origin of life is not owned by any single discipline.
- **P5**: What we see as the emergence of life is growing complexity in expression — new differentiations, new languages, new ways of engaging what was always there. The power was always in the undifferentiated. Life is one of its expressions.

The question is not "what is the origin of life?" but "what were the conditions under which specific self-sustaining differentiations became possible?" That is an empirical question about this Earth, this chemistry, this history. Not a universal one.

## Walker's generalization and the meta-privilege trap

Sara Walker's work — applying information theory to the origin of life — represents a genuinely important generalization. Bringing a new language to bear on the territory is exactly what P4 encourages: a different language giving access to different structure. Working inside a language is not a structural problem. It is the only thing anyone can do, and P2 says so explicitly. The contribution stands on its own terms.

The structural issue arises only where outside-view claims infiltrate the discourse — where the language stops saying "here is what we can see with these tools" and starts saying "this is how reality fundamentally is." That shift from inside-view tool to outside-view claim is the move the seed identifies as illegitimate.

And this infiltration is often not the researcher's own instinct. It is institutional pressure. Academic culture demands that significant work be framed in philosophical terms to be considered legitimate. A publisher or advisor says: you need the big-picture chapters, you need to say what this means for reality. The available philosophical vocabulary for doing that is still saturated with implicit outside-view assumptions — materialism, fundamentalism, the expectation that a deep enough theory must be describing how things really are.

The result is that genuine contributions — new tools, new languages, new ways of seeing — get wrapped in philosophical packaging that contradicts their own best insights. The work says "here is a new way of looking." The packaging says "and this is how it really is." The packaging undermines the work.

This is a P4 problem at the institutional level. Academic culture does not yet have a way of valuing a contribution without demanding it claim meta-privilege. The work has to be "fundamental" to be considered important. Which is the conflation the seed dissolves: value and meta-privilege are not the same thing. A language can be extraordinarily powerful, can open entirely new territory, can transform understanding — without claiming to be the language that describes reality as it ultimately is.
