# Heraclitus

Working notes on Heraclitus for the historicity post. Captures the philological status of the river fragment, the two main readings, why the dichotomy is less interesting than it looks, and where it all lands in the seed.

---

## The fragment problem

The famous version — *you cannot step in the same river twice* — comes from Plato (B91) and Plutarch. Modern scholarship (Reinhardt, Kirk, Marcovich, Kahn) has largely concluded this is not actually Heraclitus. It is a Cratylan/Platonic reading projected back onto him.

Three forms have come down:

- **B12** (Arius Didymus, via Stobaeus): *On those stepping into the same rivers, ever-different waters flow.* This is the fragment with the strongest claim to authenticity.
- **B49a** (Heraclitus Homericus): *We both step and do not step into the same rivers, we both are and are not.*
- **B91** (Plato's *Cratylus*, Plutarch): *It is not possible to step into the same river twice.*

B12 says the *rivers are the same*. The waters are different. Both clauses, in the same breath. That is structurally a different claim from what the popular saying has made of it.

## Two readings

**Radical flux** (Plato, Aristotle, Cratylus). Universal change. Nothing endures. Cratylus pushed it further: you cannot step in even once.

**Moderate flux / unity of opposites** (Reinhardt, Kahn, Kirk, Marcovich, Graham). Some things stay the same *only* by changing. The river is a river precisely because the water flows through it; stop the flow, and it ceases to be a river. Constancy through flux, not constancy despite it.

Kahn pushes the moderate reading further: it is the *people* who remain the same against the changing waters, and the encounter with the flowing environment is what helps constitute the perceiving subject as the same. That is retention named in his vocabulary. The flux sits at being-level. The same-through-change is the subject side.

## The dichotomy dissolves

The radical reading is self-undermining at the level of its own articulation. The moment the fragment uses the word *river*, retention has already happened. The reading thinks it is denying constancy, but the utterance is using a stable handle to do the denying. Plato's version does not escape historicity; it just does not notice itself relying on it.

So the choice is not which Heraclitus to lead with. Both readings sit downstream of the move. They disagree about how much of the upstream they want to acknowledge.

- Kahn/Graham acknowledge it: the same-river *depends on* the changing waters.
- Plato/Cratylus deny it but use it anyway.

The post does not need to argue for retention. It can show that anything spoken about already has it. The river is itself the proof.

## The body case

The cleanest example is closer than the river. The matter you were made of seven years ago is not the matter you are made of now. Cells flow in and out. The flux is total at the cellular level. And the person persists.

So retention cannot be in the matter. The matter has nothing to retain.

It has to live somewhere else. The body is held together by what it speaks — chemical, neural, metabolic, immunological signalling by which cells coordinate, replace, repair. Disrupt the language and the pattern dies even if every cell is fine. Sustain the language and the pattern persists even though no cell does.

No one can deny this about themselves. That is the rhetorical strength of the example.

## Where retention lives

Not in matter. Not in pattern-as-such, because there is no pattern-as-such waiting to be picked out. The concept is what does the holding. The river is held by the concept *river* doing its work. The person by the concept *person* doing its work. The flux is what is there. The concept is what retains.

Creation — being and language together — is contingent. The cut could have been otherwise. Different beings, different moments make different cuts, and the same flux gets retained differently, or not retained at all. The body could have been carved at cell-level (no person, just cells), at species-level (no individual, just species-flow). What we call *the same person* is one cut among many. The cut is what retains.

There is no pattern living anywhere prior to the cut. The cut makes the pattern.

## Where this lands in the seed

- **P0** — being implies language, both arising through differentiation. The differentiation is the cut.
- **P1** — language is relational. The concept is the relational hold maintained over time.
- **P2** — the subject experiences reality through its language. What the subject experiences as "the same body persisting" is what its language has made of the underlying flux.

Historicity is concept-work persisting. Concept-work is what creation is doing, all the way down.

## The gradient

Concepts are not only human. The cell's self/non-self distinction is concept-work at the cellular level — a cut the cell maintains immunologically and metabolically. Membrane physics does inside/outside as a concept-cut. Historicity is not an upper-tier achievement that begins with humans; it begins wherever any cut is being held. The gradient runs from chemistry to humans because concept-making accumulates, not because it switches on somewhere.

This connects directly to the storyline point on self-creation through language accumulation.

## Logos as additional doorway

Heraclitus's *logos* — the account that holds — is what makes the flux intelligible. A reading-layer that lets recognition happen. Structurally close to where Splectrum puts language. Worth a brief acknowledgement in the post but not the main move; the main move is the river → body → concept arc.

## For the post

Two opening structures available:

1. **Popular fragment as misreading hook.** Open with *you cannot step in the same river twice*, reveal it is probably not what Heraclitus said, use the misreading itself as the frame for the historicity move. Rhetorical lift; spends a paragraph on scholarship.
2. **B12 directly.** Cleaner. Loses the hook of "the thing everyone remembers about Heraclitus is wrong in an interesting way."

Two opening examples available:

1. **River first, then body.** Body lands as the move that reveals the river was about us all along. More punch.
2. **Body first, then river.** Most concrete and undeniable. More direct.

The body example is the strongest individual move — no one can deny it about themselves — but it works best as the second beat after the river has done its setup.

Open question: how much of the philological subplot to keep on stage. Trade-off is between rhetorical lift and pacing.
