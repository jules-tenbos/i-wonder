# The Persistence of the Absolute

## From Descartes to the Relational Turn

---

## Summary

The history of modern Western philosophy, from Descartes to the twentieth century, can be read as a single sustained attempt to secure absolute certainty about the relationship between a knowing subject and the world it inhabits. Each major figure in this tradition identifies the limitations of their predecessors and produces a more sophisticated response — but none relinquishes the underlying demand for the absolute. What changes is where the absolute is located: in God, in the transcendental subject, in the dialectical totality, in the transcendental ego, in Being, in the linguistic system, in process itself. The structural role persists. A ground is always sought. An arbiter — some third position standing outside both subject and world to guarantee their connection — keeps appearing, however disguised.

What finally changes is not a better answer to the same question, but a refusal of the question itself. That refusal is the relational turn, enabled by three thinkers: Wittgenstein and Rorty, who open the door from the analytic tradition into relational thinking through a fundamental reorientation of how we understand language; and Merleau-Ponty, who provides the experiential, embodied ground that the language turn opens toward but cannot itself deliver.

The following narrative traces the historical arc that makes the relational turn both intelligible and necessary — not as a dialectical culmination, but as a step sideways once the tradition has exhausted its own possibilities.

---

## The Structure: Subject, World, Arbiter

Descartes inaugurates modern philosophy by splitting reality into two substances — *res cogitans* (thinking) and *res extensa* (extension) — and then demanding absolute certainty about the connection between them. His method of radical doubt strips away everything that can be doubted until only the act of thinking remains: *cogito, ergo sum*.

But the *cogito* yields only one certainty: that I, as a thinking thing, exist. The external world, other minds, even mathematics — all remain under suspicion. To recover them, Descartes needs a guarantee that his clear and distinct perceptions correspond to reality. The subject alone cannot provide this guarantee (that would be circular), and the world cannot provide it either (since the subject's access to the world is precisely what's in question). A third party is required: something standing outside both subject and world, capable of underwriting the link between them.

Descartes fills this role with God. A perfect, non-deceptive God would not allow systematic error in careful reasoning. God becomes the guarantor of knowledge.

The crucial point is not the theological content but the structural necessity. The demand for the absolute — for certainty that is total, immune to doubt, and guaranteed from a fixed point — *generates* the need for an arbiter. The moment you have a subject on one side and a world on the other, and you insist the relationship between them be absolutely secured, you create a gap that neither side can close. The arbiter is not an optional addition to the Cartesian framework; it is its structural consequence.

This sets the pattern for everything that follows. Each subsequent thinker will try to solve the problem by relocating the arbiter — but as long as the demand for the absolute persists, the arbiter keeps reappearing in new forms.

## Kant: The Arbiter Internalised

Kant recognises that an external guarantor cannot do the work Descartes needs. His response — the "Copernican turn" — is to invert the question: instead of asking how the mind conforms to objects, he asks how objects conform to the mind.

His answer: the mind actively structures experience. Space, time, causality — these are not features discovered in the world but conditions the mind imposes on experience for it to be intelligible at all. He calls these the transcendental conditions of possibility. The world as we experience it is always already shaped by the subject's cognitive apparatus. We never access things "in themselves" (*Ding an sich*) — only things as they appear through our structuring activity.

This is a genuinely radical move. The arbiter is no longer external. The transcendental categories — causality, substance, unity — perform the mediating role, but they belong to the subject's own cognitive structure rather than to God or any external authority. The guarantee of knowledge is not something beyond us; it is the way we are constitutively built.

But Kant does not escape the triadic structure. The thing-in-itself remains — sitting behind appearances, forever inaccessible, doing no describable work yet seemingly indispensable to the framework. And the transcendental subject that does the structuring floats in a strange no-man's-land: neither empirical nor straightforwardly absolute, yet carrying all the hallmarks of the absolute in its necessity, universality, and ahistorical fixity. The arbiter has changed address, from God to the internal architecture of cognition, but it has not changed function. The demand for absolute grounding persists.

## Hegel: The Arbiter Totalised

Hegel sees Kant's thing-in-itself as an incoherent remainder. If you can say nothing about it, it does no work. If you can say something about it — that it exists, that it is unknowable — then you are already applying the very categories it was supposed to lie beyond. The limit Kant drew around knowledge is itself a piece of knowledge, which means it is not really a limit.

From this, Hegel draws a stunning conclusion: there is no outside. Thought and reality are not two separate domains requiring a bridge. They are the same self-developing process, viewed from different angles. What exists is not a static world that a subject then represents — it is a dynamic, self-developing totality that comes to know itself through the activity of thinking.

His dialectic describes how this works: every position contains internal tensions that drive it beyond itself into something richer. A claim generates its negation; the confrontation produces a more comprehensive understanding that incorporates both — which then generates new tensions, and so on. This is not a method applied from outside; it is how reality itself moves.

In terms of the arbiter problem, Hegel's move is to dissolve the separation that creates the need for one. Subject and object, thought and being, knower and known — these are not independent terms requiring mediation. They are moments within a single self-differentiating process. The absolute is not a third party guaranteeing the link; it *is* the link, the movement, the totality.

But the cost is revealing. The absolute has not been abandoned — it has been expanded to swallow everything. Instead of sitting outside as a guarantor, it becomes the whole of reality knowing itself. The arbiter has been promoted to the totality. Contingency, particularity, genuine otherness — all risk being absorbed into the dialectical machine, treated as moments that serve the whole rather than as things that might resist or exceed it. The demand for the absolute is not questioned; it is universalised.

## Husserl: The Arbiter Described

Husserl approaches the problem from a different angle entirely. His frustration is that philosophy has become tangled in debates *about* knowledge without examining what is actually given in experience before theoretical frameworks get imposed.

His key insight is intentionality: consciousness is always consciousness *of* something. There is no bare awareness that then reaches out to contact a world. Consciousness and its object are structurally inseparable — always co-constituting. His method, the phenomenological reduction (*epoché*), brackets all assumptions about independent reality to focus on the structures of experience as they are given.

This looks as though it should dissolve the subject-object split. If consciousness and world are always already correlated, there is no gap to bridge and no arbiter required. And Husserl's descriptive analyses — of time, perception, intersubjectivity, the lifeworld — are extraordinarily rich, genuinely opening new territory for philosophical investigation.

But Husserl still reaches for apodictic certainty. The transcendental ego — the purified consciousness revealed by the reduction — becomes the site where all meaning is constituted. It plays the same grounding, constituting role that Kant's transcendental subject played, now described with far greater phenomenological richness but structurally occupying the same position. The method is new; the foundational ambition reinstates the old architecture. The arbiter appears again, this time as the transcendental ego that constitutes the world's meaning from a position of indubitable self-givenness.

## Heidegger: The Arbiter Ontologised

Heidegger, Husserl's most important student, sees most clearly what is wrong with his teacher's project — and, by extension, with the entire tradition since Descartes.

His argument is that the Cartesian starting point is already a distortion. We do not begin as detached thinking subjects contemplating a world of objects. We begin as *Dasein* — beings who are always already *in* a world, practically engaged, using things, caring, thrown into situations we did not choose. The subject-object split is not a problem to be solved; it is a secondary abstraction from a more primordial mode of being that was never split in the first place. Being-in-the-world comes first; theoretical contemplation comes after, and is derivative of it.

This is genuinely devastating to the Cartesian tradition. Heidegger does not relocate the arbiter; he argues that the situation requiring one was a philosophical fabrication. There was never a detached subject needing to reach across to a distant world. We were always already involved, embedded, worldly.

But Heidegger has his own version of the pattern. His later thinking turns toward Being itself — not beings, not entities, but the condition under which anything can show up at all. Being *gives* itself, *sends* itself through history, withdraws even as it reveals. Language becomes "the house of Being." Thinking becomes a kind of waiting, a listening for Being's address.

Being is not God, not the transcendental subject, not the dialectic. But it functions similarly: it is the ungraspable ground that makes everything else possible, that can never itself be fully grasped or brought to presence. The arbiter has become ontological rather than theological or epistemological, but the structural position — something deeper underneath that grants intelligibility while remaining beyond reach — persists. Heidegger gets closer than anyone before him to the relational turn, dismantling the subject-object structure more thoroughly than any of his predecessors, and still reaches for a ground. His is the last and most subtle form of the pattern before the refusal becomes possible.

## Saussure: The Arbiter Systematised

Saussure enters from linguistics rather than philosophy, but his structural insight strikes at the same problem from the side of meaning.

Before Saussure, the dominant assumption about language was representational: words are names for things, and understanding language means understanding how names attach to referents. Saussure breaks with this by arguing that the linguistic sign is arbitrary and differential. Neither the signifier (sound-image) nor the signified (concept) has any positive content in itself. Both are defined entirely by their differences from other elements in the system. The sound "bat" means what it means not because of some intrinsic quality but because it is not "cat," not "bit," not "but." Identity is a product of difference, not the other way round.

This is radical because it inverts the assumption running through the entire philosophical tradition: that meaning begins from something positive, some self-identical term that then enters into relations. Saussure shows that there are no positive terms — only differences. Meaning is relational all the way down.

But Saussure contains his own insight. He distinguishes between *langue* (the abstract system of language) and *parole* (actual speech), and privileges langue as the proper object of study. The system is treated as synchronic, self-enclosed, and formal — existing above or behind the messy reality of language use. In this move, *langue* itself becomes a new kind of absolute: a closed totality that determines meaning from above. The system becomes the arbiter.

The post-structuralists — Derrida above all — saw this immediately. If meaning really is differential, the system can never be closed. Every sign refers to other signs, endlessly. There is no point at which the chain of differences bottoms out in a self-identical meaning. Derrida's *différance* names this endless deferral: Saussure radicalised, with the closure removed. But the step from Saussure's contained insight to its full implications requires the relational turn that his own framework, caught between its radical principle and its systematic ambition, could not complete.

## Whitehead: The Arbiter Processualised

Whitehead comes from mathematics and logic — he co-wrote *Principia Mathematica* with Russell — and attacks what he calls the "fallacy of misplaced concreteness": the habit of taking abstractions (substance, subject, object, matter, mind) and treating them as basic realities, when they are derivative simplifications of something richer.

For Whitehead, what is truly concrete is not things but events — "actual occasions of experience." Reality is composed of momentary, self-constituting happenings, each of which *prehends* (takes in, feels) other occasions. Prehension is not representation and not cognition in the Cartesian sense; it is a more fundamental responsiveness. Even an electron prehends its environment. Experience goes all the way down — not as human consciousness, but as felt relatedness.

This dissolves the mind-body split at the root. Every actual occasion is both physical and experiential. There are no self-identical substances that then enter into relations; relations are ontologically primary. The subject-object gap never opens because each occasion *is* its process of self-constitution from the relations it inherits. No gap, no arbiter needed.

But Whitehead still builds a total system. He introduces God — not as a transcendent creator, but as the "primordial nature" that orders the realm of eternal objects (pure possibilities) and a "consequent nature" that receives and preserves completed occasions. God is in the process, affected by it, changed by it. Yet the eternal objects themselves look uncomfortably like Platonic forms — a fixed repertoire of what can be actualised — and the primordial nature that orders them still provides a kind of guarantee of coherence. The arbiter has been made immanent, processual, and relational in its character, but it has not disappeared. Whitehead's systematic ambition — the drive to produce a complete speculative cosmology — carries the structure forward in its most subtle and most generous form.

## The Pattern

Across all these thinkers, the pattern repeats. Each sees the limitations of their predecessors more clearly than anyone before. Each produces a more sophisticated, more nuanced, more internally consistent response. And each, in doing so, relocates the absolute rather than relinquishing it.

| Thinker | Where the arbiter sits |
|---|---|
| Descartes | God (external guarantor) |
| Kant | Transcendental subject (internal guarantor) |
| Hegel | The dialectical totality (everything as self-knowing system) |
| Husserl | Transcendental ego (constituting consciousness) |
| Heidegger | Being (ontological ground of disclosure) |
| Saussure | Langue (the closed linguistic system) |
| Whitehead | Process with God and eternal objects (immanent but systematic ground) |

The content changes; the structural role persists. A ground is always sought. The demand for the absolute generates the need for an arbiter, and the arbiter keeps reappearing because the demand is never itself questioned.

The tradition does not fail through lack of brilliance. It fails because the problem is structural. As long as you begin from a separation — subject from world, mind from body, language from reality — and demand that the connection be absolutely secured, you will need something to do the securing. The question is not *which* arbiter, but whether an arbiter is needed at all.

---

## The Turn

What finally breaks the pattern is not a better answer but a different kind of move: a refusal of the demand that generated the pattern in the first place.

This is where Wittgenstein, Rorty, and Merleau-Ponty enter — the subject of a separate discussion. Together, they do not complete the tradition but step sideways from it.

Wittgenstein and Rorty accomplish the language turn. Wittgenstein, beginning from logic and the philosophy of language, shows the representational picture of meaning dissolving once you look at how language actually works — in use, in practice, in forms of life. Meaning is not secured by correspondence to objects or by position in a formal system; it happens in context, among people, without foundations. Crucially, Wittgenstein does not replace the old ground with a new one. He shows that the demand for a ground was based on a confusion. The door on the arbiter is closed without another being opened.

Rorty provides the meta-narrative. He reads the entire tradition from Descartes through Kant to analytic philosophy as one sustained story about the failed search for a "mirror of nature" — the mind as representing reality, philosophy's task being to verify the accuracy of the representation. His argument is that the epistemological project at the heart of modern philosophy should be abandoned, not reformed. Where Wittgenstein shows the representational picture failing from the inside, Rorty historicises the absolute — reveals it as a contingent cultural commitment rather than an inescapable feature of thought.

Merleau-Ponty provides what the language turn opens toward but cannot itself deliver: the experiential, embodied ground. Wittgenstein is deliberately austere about positive claims; Rorty can sound as though experience has been replaced by discourse. Merleau-Ponty shows that relational, situated meaning is not just a feature of language or social practice — it is how embodied perception works at the most basic level. The body is already meaningful before any linguistic or conceptual act. Perception is already relational, already a dialogue between organism and world. This gives the relational account roots in lived experience rather than leaving it as a purely linguistic or pragmatic thesis.

The architecture is clean: Wittgenstein dismantles the representational theory of meaning. Rorty dismantles the philosophical culture that sustained it. Merleau-Ponty provides the positive experiential basis for what replaces it. And the story from Descartes to Whitehead is not a progression toward the relational view but an exhaustion of alternatives — each failure making the underlying structure more visible until the relational move becomes not a culmination but the only coherent step remaining.

The tradition does not arrive at the relational turn. It runs out of reasons not to take it.
