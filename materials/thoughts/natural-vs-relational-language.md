# Natural Language vs Relational Language

## The Statement

English is a natural language — a shared representation system (sounds, grammar, vocabulary). But within English, many languages live: physics, poetry, law, religion. Each has its own rules of engagement and standards of validity. These are relational languages — expressions of relation with their own commitments.

Natural language is the substrate. Relational language is the game played within or across it. The same relational language can be expressed through different natural languages (physics in English, physics in German — same game, different substrate). The same natural language hosts many relational languages.

A natural language is relationally capable — it can express relations but doesn't commit to any particular ones. A relational language is relationally committed — it commits to certain relations as meaningful, certain methods as valid, certain standards as binding.

The term "relational language" connects directly to the principle: "language is relational" — a relational language is a language that commits to specific relations as its domain.

## How This Fits — Research Findings

The two-level structure (substrate and game) has substantial precedent. The specific combination of properties — substrate/game layering, cross-linguistic portability, equal standing, defined by expressing relation — is not captured by any single existing term.

### Closest Structural Match

- **Lotman's secondary modeling systems** — Natural language is the primary modeling system (substrate). Cultural/intellectual domains (science, law, myth) are secondary systems built on top of it. Explicitly treats natural language as the carrier and cultural sign systems as layered on top, with their own rules. The closest match to the two-level structure.

### Partial Matches

| Framework | What it captures | What it misses |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Wittgenstein's language games** | Rule-governed activities with internal standards | No substrate/game layering; no cross-linguistic portability |
| **Lyotard's genres of discourse** | Heterogeneous, incommensurable rule systems | No substrate layer |
| **Cassirer's symbolic forms** | Autonomous domains with own modes of meaning | Treats natural language as one form among many, not the substrate |
| **Oakeshott's modes of experience** | Non-hierarchical autonomous domains | Framed epistemologically, not linguistically |
| **Register (Halliday)** | Variation within a natural language | Tied to linguistic features of one natural language — legal English and legal German are different registers, not the same game |
| **Foucault's discursive formations** | Domain-specific rules of statement | About power, not epistemic autonomy |
| **Hjelmslev's connotative semiotics** | Layered semiotic structures — one system built on another | Formal mechanism for layering, but not concerned with rule-governed domains of meaning |
| **Bakhtin's heteroglossia** | Multiple games coexisting within one natural language | Does not separate substrate from what is played on it; no cross-linguistic portability |

### What Is Distinctive

1. Explicit two-level structure: substrate (natural language) and game (relational language)
2. Cross-linguistic portability — the same relational language can be expressed through different natural languages
3. Equal standing in potential as a commitment, not a finding
4. Defined by what it expresses (relation), not by structure, social function, or textual features
5. The distinction between relationally capable (natural language) and relationally committed (relational language)

The term "relational language" with this meaning does not appear in any of these traditions.

### Other Types of Language for Reference

| Term | What it is | Relation to this distinction |
|------|-----------|------------------------------|
| **Formal language** | Strings over a finite alphabet with explicit formation rules (logic, mathematics, programming) | A subset of relational languages — formal languages have explicit rules, but many relational languages (poetry, religion) do not |
| **Metalanguage / Object language** (Tarski) | A language used to talk about another language, vs the language being described | A level distinction (about vs. in), not a substrate/game distinction |
| **Sign language** | A natural language using visual-gestural modality | A different substrate, not a relational language |
| **Anti-language** (Halliday) | A language of a counter-society | Closer to a relational language — a game with its own rules, played through natural language |
