# The Principle in Plain Sight

The five statements of the founding principle are not describing something exotic. They are describing what is already happening — in your kitchen, at your dinner table, in every conversation you have ever had. The structures the principle names — individual experience, the us-them relation, privacy, shared knowledge, intertwined languages — are already present in ordinary life.

This walkthrough uses everyday language and concrete examples to make that visible. Not because the principle is limited to humans, but because starting from lived experience makes the structures easiest to see.

---

## 1. Language is relational

*What a language gives access to depends on what it relates to.*

In plain terms: what you can do and understand grows from what you have engaged with. Your abilities are not abstract — they are shaped by what you have actually touched, tasted, practised, lived through.

**Food.** You learn to cook by relating to ingredients, heat, timing, taste. Your cooking develops as you relate to more — new ingredients, new techniques, new cuisines. Someone who has never cooked has no access to what you experience when you handle a ripe tomato. They see a tomato. You see texture, ripeness, a dozen possible dishes, the right moment to use it. That difference is not intelligence. It is relation. Your language of cooking grew from what you related to.

**Music.** A guitarist's playing develops through relating to the instrument, to songs, to other musicians, to audiences. Ten years of playing blues gives access to something a beginner cannot hear, let alone play. The language of blues guitar is not a thing you acquire — it is a web of relations you have built.

**Parenting.** A first-time parent relates to their child and discovers a language they did not have before — a language of feeding schedules, crying patterns, tiny signals. It does not come from a book. It grows from the relating itself.

The pattern: the reach of any language depends on what it has related to. No relation, no access.

---

## 2. A subject experiences reality through language

*Experience is always within the reach of a language.*

In plain terms: your experience is always yours. It comes through your own history, your own body, your own relations. There is always a you and a not-you. That boundary is not hostile — it is structural. It is the basic fact of being somewhere rather than nowhere.

**Food.** Two people eat the same dish. One grew up with it — grandmother's kitchen, childhood afternoons, a whole world of association. The other encounters it for the first time. Same dish, different experience. Neither is wrong. Each experiences the dish through their own history, their own palate, their own language of food. Your experience is private. Not secret — private. It belongs to you because it comes through you.

**Music.** Two people hear the same song. One played it at their wedding. The other has never heard it before. Same sound waves, different realities. The song each person hears is filtered through everything they bring to it.

**Neighbours.** Two people live on the same street. One has been there thirty years — knows the sounds, the rhythms, who lives where, what the place used to look like. The other moved in last week. Same street, different experience. The long-time resident inhabits a richer version of the street — not because they are better, but because they have more relations to it.

This is where the us-them appears. Not as conflict, but as structure. Your experience is yours. Mine is mine. We are each somewhere — and that somewhere is not the same place. The us-them is the basic fact that there is always a perspective, always a position. Being nowhere is not an option.

---

## 3. Subjects share knowledge

*Language is the source of the only objectivity known: convergence of subjects.*

In plain terms: you experience privately, but you do not stay private. Through conversation, through sharing, something emerges that no one had alone. That overlap — what survives when different perspectives meet — is the closest thing to objectivity there is.

**Food.** The family around the dinner table. Each person tastes the meal privately. One finds it too salty. Another thinks the texture is perfect. A third remembers it tasting different last time. Through conversation — "needs more salt," "no, it's fine," "the garlic is stronger than usual" — something emerges: a shared understanding of the meal that no single person had alone. That convergence is not the truth about the meal. It is the knowledge — what survives the overlap of private experiences.

**Music.** A band rehearsing. The drummer feels the tempo is right. The singer thinks it drags. They play it again, adjust, discuss, play again. What emerges is not what any one member heard — it is what they converged on. The song they end up with is knowledge: the product of sharing.

**Neighbours.** A street meeting about parking. Everyone has their own experience of the problem. Through conversation, a shared picture forms — not identical to anyone's private view, but richer than any single view alone. Knowledge is what survives the overlap.

Notice what this means. Privacy is not a problem to be overcome. It is the starting condition. Without private experience, there is nothing to share. Without sharing, there is no knowledge. Both are needed. The principle does not choose between the individual and the group — it needs both.

---

## 4. Languages are inter-relational with equal standing

*Languages, as committed ways of expressing relation, are not isolated games. They interact, overlap, and inform each other, all having equal standing in potential.*

In plain terms: there is not just one way to access reality. Different languages reach into the same world from different angles. None of them owns it. Each accesses something the others cannot. And they are not sealed off from each other — they bleed into one another continuously.

**Food.** A meal sits at the intersection of many languages. Growing food, cooking food, eating food, writing about food, studying its chemistry, understanding its economics, placing it in a culture. The farmer, the chef, the nutritionist, the food critic, the dinner guest — each brings a different language, each reaches into the same reality from a different angle. The farmer knows something about the tomato that the chemist does not. The chemist knows something the farmer does not. Neither perspective is more real. Each has access the other lacks.

And they are not isolated. The farmer's knowledge informs the chef. The chef's demands shape what the farmer grows. The nutritionist's findings change how the chef cooks. The food critic's language shifts what people value at the table. These are different games — different vocabularies, different standards, different expertise — but they are intertwined. No prescribed boundaries.

**Music.** The musician's language, the sound engineer's language, the music theorist's language, the listener's language. Different angles on the same phenomenon. The engineer hears frequencies where the musician hears emotion. Neither is wrong. Both are partial. And they inform each other — the musician learns from the engineer, the theorist from the listener. Equal standing means no one language gets to declare itself the real one.

**A parent and a paediatrician.** The parent knows the child through daily life — rhythms, moods, the particular cry that means tired rather than hungry. The paediatrician knows the child through clinical language — growth charts, developmental milestones, diagnostic categories. Different languages, equal standing. Neither replaces the other. The best care happens when they overlap.

---

## 5. Together they form a web of growing complexity

*Relational density increases as knowledge grows.*

In plain terms: every conversation creates more language. Every new relation opens new possibilities. The web grows — not because someone designs it, but because relating generates more to relate to.

**Food.** Every new recipe is a new relation. Every new technique opens new possibilities. Fusion cuisine happens when two food traditions meet and their languages intermingle. Food science creates a new language that changes how chefs work. Dietary movements create vocabularies that reshape what people grow and eat. A hundred years ago, "umami" was not in the Western food vocabulary. Now it shapes how millions of people think about flavour. The web grew — not because someone planned it, but because new relations kept generating new things to relate to.

**Music.** Jazz emerged from the meeting of West African rhythms, blues, and European harmony. It created a new language that generated its own sub-languages — bebop, cool, free jazz, fusion. Each conversation between musical traditions created more music to have conversations about. The web of music is vastly more complex now than it was a century ago — not by design, but by relating.

**A street over decades.** New families arrive, languages meet, a corner shop opens, a community garden starts, conflicts arise and get negotiated, stories accumulate. The street develops its own language — its own references, its own history, its own way of doing things. Complexity grows from the density of relating.

The growth is not accidental and it is not designed. It is structural. Relating produces new relations. Knowledge creates conditions for more knowledge. The web cannot help but grow.

---

## Where this opens

Walk through the five statements and something becomes visible. If every subject has its own experience — privacy. If knowledge requires sharing — convergence. If languages have equal standing — no hierarchy.

Then how we treat other subjects, other languages, other ways of knowing has consequences. Dismissing someone's experience is not just rude — it cuts off a perspective that the overlap needs. Declaring one language superior to all others is not just arrogant — it shrinks the web. Refusing to share is not just selfish — it blocks the only path to knowledge.

The principle does not prescribe an ethics. But it surfaces the ground from which ethics grows: the structural fact that we are separate, that we need each other, and that the web is richer when nothing is excluded.

That is the door this walkthrough opens. What lies beyond it is work still to be done.
