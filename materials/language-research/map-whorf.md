# Whorf — Map

## Initial phrases

"Every language is a vast pattern-system, different from others, in which are culturally ordained the forms and categories by which the personality not only communicates, but analyzes nature, notices or neglects types of relationship and phenomena, channels his reasoning, and builds the house of his consciousness."

"We dissect nature along lines laid down by our native languages."

"The background linguistic system (grammar) of each language is not merely a reproducing instrument for voicing ideas but rather is itself the shaper of ideas, the program and guide for the individual's mental activity."

"We are thus introduced to a new principle of relativity, which holds that all observers are not led by the same physical evidence to the same picture of the universe."

Standard Average European (SAE) — Whorf coined this term to expose how shared features of European languages were being treated as universal, masking what is in fact one language group's pattern.

Cryptotypes — hidden, unverbalized patterns that shape language use beneath the surface. Inobvious meanings operating within a language even when speakers are unaware of them.

---

## Mapping statement

Whorf says language "builds the house of his consciousness" — the personality not only communicates through it but analyzes nature, notices or neglects types of relationship, channels reasoning through it. This is close to how we think about experience: language is the medium through which a subject experiences reality, and what a language gives access to depends on what it relates to. His "notices or neglects" is our reach.

His principle of linguistic relativity — "all observers are not led by the same physical evidence to the same picture of the universe" — parallels Einstein's relativity explicitly and arrives, from the study of human languages, at the same structural observation Rovelli arrives at from physics: the observer's frame shapes what is observed. There is no view from nowhere.

Whorf was more careful than the "strong hypothesis" label suggests. His concern was habitual thought — how language creates patterns of thinking and behaviour that shape what we see and how we act, not absolute walls. Workers smoking near "empty" gasoline drums: the word habituated them to treat the drums as inert, overriding the physical reality of flammable vapors. Language shaping behaviour through habitual reach.

His Standard Average European is an equal standing observation: one group of languages' features treated as universal, one cloth mistaken for the fabric.

His cryptotypes — hidden patterns shaping use beneath the surface — point to structure operating within language that speakers do not see but are shaped by. The fabric at work within the cloth.

Where Whorf stays within human spoken languages, our principle extends the same structural pattern across the full range of language types. His framework does not require that extension, and ours does not depend on his. But the overlap is there: language structures experience, different languages structure differently, and no language's structuring is the universal standard.
