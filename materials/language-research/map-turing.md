# Turing — Map

## Initial phrases

"A language, at its most reduced, is a set of symbols plus rules for their transformation."

Universality — a sufficiently powerful symbol system can simulate any other.

---

## Mapping statement

Turing defines language at bare bones: symbols and rules for their transformation. The substance is not the symbols but the relationships and transformations between them. Each language defined by its relational structure, not by what its symbols look like.

His transformation — rule-governed change from one state to another — describes language in action at its most formal. The executor (the machine, in his case) moves through states according to rules. From where we stand, this is recognisable more broadly: a machine executes code, a reader follows a text, a musician follows a score. The pattern — moving through states according to a relational structure — appears across domains, though Turing's own concern was strictly computational.

From this minimal starting point, universality emerges: one sufficiently powerful language can simulate any other. At bare bones, inter-relational is already there. The less he puts in, the more it overlaps with how we think about equal standing and inter-relational.

Turing sits with Shannon and Hilbert in the formal/minimal cluster — each stripping language to bare bones, each finding relational structure at the bottom. Turing strips to transformation, Shannon to difference, Hilbert to syntax. Together they describe the minimum, and the minimum is relational. Godel completes the group by proving that no single formal system, however powerful, can be self-sufficient — inter-relational is structurally inevitable.

Turing's universality also grounds the programming language hierarchy: machine language → assembly → high-level → domain-specific. Each level is a new language built on the one below, expanding the reach. Universality makes this possible — and the hierarchy is inter-relational made engineering — a concrete case of what the principle describes.
