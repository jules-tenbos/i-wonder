# Step 2 — Discussion

Discussion following the research on the axis: Wittgenstein, Rorty, Merleau-Ponty.

---

## Why this axis

Wittgenstein and Rorty are clearly axis — Wittgenstein unlocks (frees languages), Rorty crosses (places language at the centre and moves between traditions). The question was Merleau-Ponty or Heidegger for the third position.

Heidegger is already in the room. Rorty carries his historicism and critique of metaphysics. Wittgenstein independently arrives at similar territory (forms of life as being-in-the-world). Even Merleau-Ponty was directly influenced by him. Heidegger's pieces are distributed across the axis without him needing to be on it.

Merleau-Ponty brings what none of the others carry — the body. Rorty has no body at all ("all awareness is a linguistic affair"). Without the body, the seed can't extend beyond human verbal activity. And Merleau-Ponty's framework — input, processing, response through bodily engagement — structurally allows extension to non-human cases, even though he stays with the human case himself.

The axis stays: Wittgenstein, Rorty, Merleau-Ponty.

## Wittgenstein — read relationally

His main moves, read for the relational pattern underneath:

**1. Meaning is use.**
"For a large class of cases the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (PI 43). Meaning doesn't live inside a word — it lives in what the word does, in relation to other words, practices, speakers. Use is relational: someone uses something for something in some context.

**2. Language games — irreducible plurality.**
An indeterminate number of language games, each with its own grammar. "New types of language, new language-games, come into existence, and others become obsolete and get forgotten" (PI 23). The plurality is open-ended. No master game contains all others.

**3. Family resemblance.**
"What is common to all these activities and what makes them into language or parts of language?" (PI 65). His answer: no single common feature. "A complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing" (PI 66). The relation between games is one of overlapping similarity, not shared essence.

**4. Forms of life.**
Language games are embedded in shared practices, activities, ways of living. "The speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life" (PI 23). Language doesn't float free — it's situated in relation to how people live.

**5. Rule-following requires community.**
No rule determines its own application. "When I obey a rule, I do not choose. I obey the rule blindly" (PI 219). Rule-following is grounded in shared practice. The private language argument: a language understandable by only one person is unintelligible, because "whatever seems right to me is right" collapses the distinction between correct and incorrect. Language is constitutively social and relational.

**6. Essence is expressed in grammar.**
"Essence is expressed in grammar" (PI 371). "Grammar tells what kind of object anything is" (PI 373). The essence of a thing is not discovered in reality — it is constituted by grammar, by the rules governing how we use the word. Grammar is the rules of use — how words relate to each other, to practices, to forms of life. Grammar is entirely relational.

**7. Therapeutic method.**
"Philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity" (Tractatus 4.112). "The philosopher treats a question; like an illness" (PI 255). Philosophical problems arise from misapplied metaphors, from the gap between surface grammar and depth grammar. The aim is to dissolve problems, not to build theories.

**8. Structural observations — accumulated.**
Throughout the later work, structural preconditions for language games accumulate: regularity and agreement (PI 240-242), custom and institution (PI 199), form of life (PI 241), an unquestioned background — hinge propositions (On Certainty), very general facts of nature (PI Part II), publicity of rules (private language argument). The riverbed metaphor (OC 96-99): flowing water (empirical propositions), sand (hinge propositions that can shift), hard rock (practically immovable). "The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty" (OC 114-115). The materials for a structural account accumulate. The account itself stays unbuilt.

## Wittgenstein's thinking context

### The room

Logic and the philosophy of language after Frege and Russell. Language has one logical structure that mirrors the structure of reality. The Tractatus is the purest expression of the room: "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world" (5.6). A meaningful proposition pictures a state of affairs by sharing its logical form. Names go proxy for simple objects. The structure of language mirrors the structure of reality.

The room assumes: language is singular (one logical language underneath the mess of ordinary speech). Meaning is reference (words stand for things). The job of philosophy is to uncover the logical structure. What doesn't fit the structure — ethics, aesthetics, the mystical — must be passed over in silence.

Wittgenstein built this room with the Tractatus. Then spent the rest of his career dismantling it from inside.

### The dismantling

The Sraffa gesture: Wittgenstein insisted a proposition and what it describes must have the same logical form. Sraffa made a Neapolitan gesture — brushing the underneath of his chin — and asked: "What is the logical form of *that*?" Wittgenstein credited Sraffa with "the most fruitful ideas" of the Investigations.

The builder's game (PI 2): a complete primitive language with four words. Not a fragment of a richer language — a self-contained system. This alone breaks the assumption of one logical language underneath.

The list (PI 23): giving orders, describing objects, reporting events, speculating, making jokes, praying — deliberately open-ended. Each one a language game with its own grammar. The plurality is the point.

### Observations about the room

The Tractatus picture theory — language mirrors reality — was itself a language game, with its own grammar, its own rules of use. The room didn't contain a discovery about language. It contained a vocabulary.

"If one tried to advance theses in philosophy, it would never be possible to debate them, because everyone would agree to them" (PI 128). The room's theses felt substantial because they were framed as discoveries. Restated as descriptions of use, they dissolve.

## "Essence is expressed in grammar" — research findings

Traditional essentialism says things have fixed natures that exist in reality, and we discover them. Wittgenstein inverts this. The essence of a thing is constituted by grammar, by the rules governing how we use the word. Grammar comes first. What we call the "nature" of a thing is a reflection of how we've set up the grammatical category.

Ring (2019) argues this is still essentialism — a novel kind. Things do have essences, but essences live in grammar, not in reality. This is compatible with family resemblance because they target different things. Family resemblance rejects essence-in-reality (no single defining feature out there that all games share). The grammar/essence passages place essence-in-grammar (the grammar of each game tells you what kind of thing that game is).

Grammar is entirely relational. There's no grammar of a word in isolation. "Essence is expressed in grammar" is saying: essence is expressed in relations. Not in the thing itself, not in a fixed nature out there, but in how it functions in a network of use.

Kant: the knower structures what counts as experience. Wittgenstein: the grammar structures what counts as a thing. Merleau-Ponty: the body structures what counts as perception. Same move at different levels. The structuring is the relating.

## From our context

"Meaning is use" — relational through and through. Use is always use by someone, of something, in some context. Meaning lives in the connections, not in the word.

"Language games" — languages. Each with its own grammar, its own reach, its own way of relating. The plurality and the open-endedness are familiar. Along the lines of language games, and beyond human verbal activities — the extended definition includes what Wittgenstein's list doesn't: music, mathematics as practice, animal signalling, bodily engagement.

"Family resemblance" — an observation about how languages relate to each other. Overlapping, criss-crossing. Inter-relational. The pattern of relation between languages is itself relational.

"Forms of life" — language embedded in practice, in how subjects live and act. A language's reach is defined by what it relates to. Forms of life are what it relates to.

"Rule-following requires community" — language is constitutively social. Knowledge as convergence of subjects. What survives the overlap of experience. The same relational pattern.

"Essence is expressed in grammar" — essence is expressed in relations. Grammar is relational. This resonates directly: what a language gives access to depends on what it relates to.

"Structural observations accumulated" — regularity, agreement, custom, institution, form of life, hinge propositions, very general facts of nature. These are relational conditions. Language requires shared practice, an unquestioned background, publicity. All relational.

## What relational is — and how far it stretches

What relation structurally entails has to be filled in. Unpacking "relational" — what does a relation structurally require? Positions (things that relate), the relating itself, and distinguishing (you can't relate without distinguishing — the Hegel observation from Step 1). That setup may turn out to be common wherever there is relation.

The extension question: how far does the pattern travel? Human language — high confidence. Animal behaviour — reasonable. Cellular processes — less certain. Quantum interactions — Rovelli's RQM is explicitly relational, but we don't know. At each level, confidence thins. An honest gradient, not a claim.

We observe relation from within relation. Whatever we find as the setup of relation, we can't know whether it's a feature of what's there or a feature of how we think about what's there. Kant's shadow never fully lifts. The whole universe might just be a storm in a teacup. And we'd be inside the teacup trying to describe the storm. No way to check.

A principle that claimed to see beyond itself would contradict itself.

## Rorty — read relationally

Rorty's thinking context: a room full of epistemologists, running for three hundred years on one assumption — that the mind mirrors reality, and the job of philosophy is to polish the mirror. Descartes, Locke, Kant — each added a layer, but all accepted the basic picture: knowledge is accurate representation. Rorty says: there is no mirror.

His main moves, read for the relational pattern underneath:

**1. Vocabularies are tools, not mirrors.**
Meaning doesn't live inside a vocabulary — it lives in what the vocabulary does, in use, in practice. A vocabulary relates a community to its world. No vocabulary has a privileged relationship to reality — each one relates differently. "Our vocabularies have no more of a representational relation to an intrinsic nature of things than does the anteater's snout" (TP, 48). The anteater comparison is interesting — it naturalises vocabularies as ways of relating to an environment, not ways of seeing it correctly.

**2. Vocabularies are contingent.**
"Languages are made rather than found" (CIS, 7). A vocabulary is a historical product — it arises from a particular community, moment, set of needs. This means vocabularies are always situated in relation to their context. They don't float free. The French Revolution showed "the whole vocabulary of social relations could be replaced almost overnight" — the relation between community and vocabulary can shift completely.

**3. Change happens through redescription, not refutation.**
You don't defeat a vocabulary from inside it. You offer a different one and let the relation between the two make the old one irrelevant. The work is in the *between*. Rorty borrows Nietzsche: "One error after another is coolly placed on ice; the ideal is not refuted — it freezes to death." New metaphors don't argue — they displace by relating differently.

**4. Experience is discourse.**
"All awareness is a linguistic affair." What philosophers call experience is already vocabulary in action. Within his context this dissolves a false separation — there was never a pre-linguistic given that then got put into words. The relation (linguistic activity) was always already there. Rorty takes this from Sellars's attack on the Myth of the Given.

**5. Knowledge is social justification.**
"We understand knowledge when we understand the social justification of belief" (PMN, 170). Knowledge is not a relation between a mind and the world. It's a relation between subjects — what they share, what they let each other get away with saying. "Only a belief can justify a belief." The whole thing stays within the relational — subjects relating to subjects through language.

**6. Conversation is what happens between vocabularies.**
Not translation (finding equivalences). Not adjudication (deciding who's right). Creative interpretive encounter — Davidson's "passing theories," constructed on the fly. Conversation doesn't require shared foundations. It requires willingness to relate — to listen, redescribe, be changed. The relation is generative — something new comes from the encounter.

**7. No vocabulary can rank another.**
"There is nothing beyond vocabularies which serves as a criterion of choice between them" (CIS, 80). There's no position outside the relational from which to judge. You can ask which vocabulary is more useful for a purpose — but that's a relational question (useful for whom, in what situation), not an absolute one.

**8. Moral progress is expanding "we."**
"Thinking of more and diverse people as 'one of us.'" Not discovering universal truths about human nature. Extending the circle of relation. Including more subjects in the overlap. The Danes who sheltered Jews didn't work out Kantian theory — they saw neighbours. The relation came first, the justification after.

## Rorty's thinking context

### The room

Epistemology since Descartes. Reality exists independently and has a fixed structure. The mind mirrors that structure (or tries to). Experience is what arrives at the mirror — raw, pre-linguistic, given. Language is how we report what the mirror shows. Knowledge is accurate representation. Truth is correspondence between description and reality. The debate is about which descriptions get the reflection right. Rorty is in this room his entire career. Everything he says is addressed to what's in this room.

### Dismantling the room — contemporary interactions

Sellars: the Given is a myth. There is no raw pre-linguistic experience that grounds knowledge. One wall of the room comes down.

Quine: the analytic-synthetic distinction doesn't hold. You can't separate what language contributes from what experience contributes. Another wall comes down.

Rorty's synthesis: these two attacks target the same thing — the idea that there's a foundation beneath discourse.

Davidson: there is no such thing as a language (as a shared fixed code). Communication works through passing theories — ad hoc interpretive adjustments. And the scheme-content distinction is incoherent — you can't separate the organising vocabulary from the raw material it organises.

Kuhn: vocabulary change isn't incremental improvement toward truth. It's replacement — a new paradigm makes the old one obsolete. Rorty generalises this from science to all discourse.

### Observations about the room

The mirror metaphor has been running since Descartes and nobody questioned the metaphor itself — only which way to hold the mirror.

"Experience," "truth," "foundation" are furniture in the room. They look like they refer to things. They're actually moves in a discourse.

The room itself is a vocabulary. It can be replaced.

## From the seed's context

We identify with "all awareness is a linguistic affair" — and express this through an extended language definition, along the lines of Wittgenstein's language games and beyond human language. This allows us to keep experience as the private side of that discourse: a subject's relation through a language. Experience and discourse are not opposed — they are the private and shared sides of the same relational activity.

"Languages are made rather than found" — in Rorty's context, "found" means mirrored, discovered as already there. "Made" means constructed, not corresponding to a pre-existing structure. The opposition is between representation and construction. From our context, the opposition doesn't arise — a language's reach is defined by what it relates to. The question of whether a relation is "found" or "made" doesn't come up at the structural level.

"Vocabularies are tools" — tools relate a user to a task. A relational system connects. The pattern is the same: something that works by connecting, not by representing.

"Knowledge is social justification" — sharing, all over it. With experience as discourse, Rorty recognises the similarity between the dynamic of the personal and the shared. Knowledge is where discourse converges — what subjects share as the same. "Social justification" and "convergence of subjects" describe the same relational pattern.

"No vocabulary can rank another" — 100% portable. Equal standing in potential: each language can stand in its own right.

"Conversation is what happens between vocabularies" — inter-relational. Languages overlap, interact, inform each other. Conversation is one way of describing what that looks like in practice.

"Moral progress is expanding we" — extending the circle of relation, including more subjects in the overlap. Relational density increases. A web of growing complexity.
