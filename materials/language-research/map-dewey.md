# Dewey — Map

## Initial phrases

"Of all affairs, communication is the most wonderful... That things should be able to pass from the plane of external pushing and pulling to that of revealing themselves to man, and thereby to themselves; and that the fruit of communication should be participation, sharing, is a wonder by the side of which transubstantiation pales." (Experience and Nature, Ch. 5)

"Communication is the establishment of cooperation in an activity in which there are partners, and in which the activity of each is modified and regulated by partnership."

"Language is the tool of tools — the instrument that makes all other instruments possible."

"A meaning is a method of action, a way of using things as means to a shared consummation."

"Meanings are properties of behavior first, and properties of objects only derivatively."

On naming (Dewey and Bentley): "Naming does not only distinguish, it immediately links and pulls together phenomena, that is, it relates, it groups, and is therefore part and parcel of our ordering activity."

"In the process of cooperative action through language the thing referred to gains both meaning and heightened potential."

"Once we have words and essences, their meanings can become independent of their original objects."

---

## Mapping statement

Dewey sees communication as cooperation in which partners are modified and regulated by each other's activity. The relation changes both sides. The fruit of communication is participation, sharing — and this is close to how we think about language as where subjects share knowledge about reality.

His naming relates, groups, links — it is part of the ordering activity. Language is not representation; it is action that relates. This is close to how we think and to Wittgenstein's language-game: language is the doing, the relational activity.

Through cooperative action in language, things gain both meaning and heightened potential. Events pass from "external pushing and pulling" to revealing themselves — through language, undifferentiated interaction becomes structured, meaningful, shared. Mind itself emerges from cooperative activity mediated by language — it is not prior to language but arises from it.

Once expressed, meanings can become independent of their original objects — used in new contexts, carried forward. This connects to Ricoeur's distanciation: expression cast off as an independent entity, available for interaction beyond the original situation.

Dewey's framework is instrumental and pragmatist throughout. Language is for doing things — inquiry, problem-solving, cooperation. Our framework is relational and structural. His descriptions of what language does — creates shared meaning, modifies partners, transforms events into objects, gives things heightened potential — overlap with what we think language is. Two framings, instrumental and relational, describing the same activity. His stands in its own right.
