# Aquinas — Map

## Initial phrases

"Words signify things through the mediation of concepts."

Certain terms can be predicated of radically different domains analogically — with a meaning that is proportionally related rather than univocal or equivocal.

Analogy sits between univocal predication (same meaning across domains) and equivocal predication (completely different meaning). Analogical meaning is proportionally connected — not identical, not unrelated.

---

## Mapping statement

Aquinas's analogy describes a mechanism for how meaning works across radically different domains. Not the same meaning (univocal), not completely different meaning (equivocal), but proportionally connected. A term bridges domains without reducing one to the other and without splitting into total difference. Each domain retains its own standing while the proportional connection holds.

His problem was specific: how can human language say anything about God? The gap between finite and infinite seems unbridgeable — if "good" means the same thing for God and for a human, God is reduced to human scale; if it means something completely different, language about God is empty. Analogy is his solution: "good" applied to God and to a human are proportionally related, each meaningful within its own domain, connected without collapsing the difference.

From where we stand, this describes the mechanism of inter-relational. When we note that Heraclitus's logos and Wittgenstein's form of life and Bateson's "a difference which makes a difference" overlap, we are predicating analogically — not claiming the same meaning across domains, not treating them as unrelated, but recognising proportional connections. Aquinas gave a precise name to what we do in every mapping: analogical predication across domains that maintain their own standing.

His context is theological — grounded in a metaphysics of divine participation. Ours is structural. But the mechanism he identified — proportional meaning bridging domains without reducing one to the other — operates independently of its theological origin.
