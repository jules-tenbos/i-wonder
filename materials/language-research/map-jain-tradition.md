---

## Mapping statement

The Jain tradition sees reality as infinitely complex (anekanta) — language captures it partially, from one perspective at a time, and there is always an aspect that language cannot capture at all. Their concern is epistemological humility: no single viewpoint captures the whole. The syadvada (sevenfold predication) formalises this — any statement is conditionally true, qualified by the perspective from which it is made.

From where we stand, we recognise something in this partiality. If each perspective captures only a part, and reality exceeds any single capture, then the relationship between perspectives matters — what one captures, another misses. This connects to how we think about inter-relational: languages overlap and inform each other, each with its own reach.

The Jain tradition and Godel's incompleteness arrive at a similar structural observation from very different directions: no single system — whether a perspective (Jain) or a formal system (Godel) — captures everything within its domain. The partiality is not a deficiency to be overcome but a condition of how languages work. What lies beyond any single language's reach is accessible, if at all, through other languages.
