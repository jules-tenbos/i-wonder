## 1. What does Wittgenstein mean by "grammar"?

Wittgenstein uses "grammar" in a distinctly philosophical sense that has almost nothing to do with grammatical rules as taught in school. His concept of grammar is one of the central organising ideas of his later philosophy.

### Grammar as the totality of rules governing use

For Wittgenstein, grammar refers to the complete set of rules that determine the meaningful use of a word. As Baker and Hacker put it in their analytical commentary, grammar encompasses "the rules for using a word" -- everything that determines sense, everything that has to be settled antecedently to questions about truth. Grammar includes not just syntactic rules but the full network of what you can and cannot do with a word meaningfully.

> "One cannot guess how a word functions. One has to look at its use and learn from that." (PI 340)

In the *Blue Book*, Wittgenstein already moves toward this: "Studying the grammar of the expression 'explanation of meaning' will teach you something about the grammar of the word 'meaning' and will cure you of the temptation to look about you for something which you might call the 'meaning'."

### Grammar as constitutive, not descriptive

This is the crucial move. Grammar does not describe pre-existing facts about objects; it *constitutes* what kind of object anything is. Grammar establishes logical categories. When we ask "what kind of thing is the mind?", Wittgenstein says we are really asking how the word "mind" functions in our language. The grammatical role determines the ontological status.

> "Grammar tells what kind of object anything is." (PI 373)

Grammar is thus "the form of representation we employ which determines reality and what forms it has." This is a significant claim: grammar doesn't merely reflect or track reality; it shapes how we encounter the world and categorise entities and events.

### Grammar as autonomous

A central and controversial doctrine: grammar is "autonomous" or "arbitrary." As Wittgenstein puts it, grammar is not accountable to reality. One cannot justify grammatical rules by pointing to reality itself, because the rules must already be operational for meaningful reference to occur. Grammar "consists of conventions."

But this arbitrariness is not randomness. Wittgenstein holds that grammar is arbitrary in one sense (it cannot be justified by appeal to external facts) and non-arbitrary in another (it is constrained by human physiology, needs, desires, and "very general facts of nature"). This dual claim runs through his later work and remains one of its productive tensions.

In the *Philosophical Grammar* (written 1930-33), Wittgenstein makes this explicit: grammatical rules do not determine the truth or falsity of statements about the world. Their role is to provide the *conditions* for comparing empirical propositions with reality in order to determine whether they are true or false.

### Grammar replaces logic

In the *Tractatus*, logical form provided the "scaffolding" for language and world. In the later work, grammar replaces this. Where the *Tractatus* sought a single logical structure underlying all meaningful propositions, the later Wittgenstein finds grammar -- diverse, practice-embedded, conventional, but structurally prior to any particular claim about the world.

### Key sources
- Baker & Hacker, *Wittgenstein: Rules, Grammar and Necessity* (Vol. 2 of Analytical Commentary on the PI)
- P.M.S. Hacker on grammatical propositions as "norms of representation"
- Tamara Dobler, *Wittgenstein on Grammar and Grammatical Method* (PhD thesis, available at core.ac.uk)
- Forster, *Wittgenstein on the Arbitrariness of Grammar* (Princeton UP)

## 2. What does Wittgenstein mean by "essence" in PI 371-373?

### The key passages

> "Essence is expressed in grammar." (PI 371)

> "Grammar tells what kind of object anything is. (Theology as grammar.)" (PI 373)

These are remarkable passages. They appear to commit Wittgenstein to the view that things *have* essences -- just not the kind traditional philosophy was looking for.

### Merrill Ring's argument (2019)

The most direct scholarly treatment is Merrill Ring's paper "Wittgenstein on Essence" (*Philosophical Investigations* 42(1):3-14, 2019). Ring's central claim is that PI 371 and 373 "commit Wittgenstein to holding that things have essences and which offer an account of what essences consist in." These passages "have been ignored by commentators, chiefly because it is thought that in #65ff (family resemblances) Wittgenstein rejected essentialism."

Ring argues that Wittgenstein produced "a major change in the conception of an essence" -- somewhat following in Kant's footsteps. Essence is not a set of necessary and sufficient conditions that we discover through analysis; it is what is expressed in the grammatical rules governing our use of a word. To know the essence of something is to know its grammar.

### The reorientation

The traditional picture: essences are features of reality that we discover, and which ground the correct use of words. Wittgenstein inverts this: "natures or essences are merely the shadows cast by grammar." Grammar comes first; what we call the "nature" or "essence" of a thing is a reflection of how we have constituted its grammatical category.

As one commentator (drawing on Waismann's account of Wittgenstein's views) puts it: "What these words mean shows itself in the way they are used -- the nature of understanding reveals itself in grammar, not in experiment."

This is not eliminativism about essences. Wittgenstein is not saying there are no essences. He is saying that essences are constituted by grammar rather than discovered in reality. "To define a concept is to define a word, and to define a word is to state a convention, a rule for using that word."

### The parenthetical: "Theology as grammar"

PI 373 ends with the provocative aside "(Theology as grammar)." This suggests that theological claims about the nature of God are best understood as grammatical claims -- rules for how the word "God" may be meaningfully used, rather than empirical claims about a super-entity. This exemplifies the wider point: questions about the nature of X are really questions about the grammar of "X."

### Is this in tension with family resemblance?

This is the central interpretive puzzle. See Question 7 below for the full treatment.

### Key sources
- Ring, "Wittgenstein on Essence" (2019, *Philosophical Investigations*)
- Adam See, "Wittgenstein on the Essence of Grammar" (Space of Reasons blog, 2009)
- Hacker on grammatical propositions as norms of representation

## 3. The grammar/essence thread across Wittgenstein's works

### Blue Book (1933-34)

The Blue Book is where Wittgenstein first develops his attack on the "craving for generality" and begins articulating the grammar-based alternative. The key passage:

> "Instead of 'craving for generality' I could also have said 'the contemptuous attitude towards the particular case'."

He identifies this craving as "the resultant of a number of tendencies connected with particular philosophical confusions," including "the tendency to look for something common to entities which we commonly subsume under a general term." Yet even here, the method of examining grammar as a way of dissolving philosophical problems is already in place.

The Blue Book establishes the principle that examining "the grammar of a word" -- how it is used -- is the proper method for philosophical investigation, replacing the search for hidden essences.

### Brown Book (1934-35)

The Brown Book develops the concept of "language games" through simpler forms of language, showing that most attempts to draw general theories of language are misguided. The family resemblance concept is developed here as an alternative to the demand for definitions based on common features.

### Philosophical Grammar (1930-33, published posthumously 1969)

The *Philosophical Grammar* makes explicit the autonomy of grammar. Grammatical rules are not answerable to reality. The application of grammar to reality is not shown by the grammar itself. This work also develops the idea that grammar provides the *conditions* for comparing propositions with reality -- a clearly structural claim about what must be in place before truth-evaluation is possible.

### Philosophical Investigations (1953)

Beyond 371-373, the grammar/essence thread runs through many passages:

- **PI 50**: The paradigm (e.g., the standard metre in Paris) functions as a grammatical rule, not an empirical fact. It is the *standard* by which measurement is possible. This is a structural precondition.
- **PI 65-67**: The family resemblance passages. Seemingly anti-essentialist. See Question 7.
- **PI 126**: "Philosophy simply puts everything before us, and neither explains nor deduces anything. Since everything lies open to view there is nothing to explain." Grammar is not hidden; it is the open structure of use.
- **PI 199**: "To follow a rule, to make a report, to give an order, to play a game of chess, are customs (usages, institutions)." Rule-following requires practices, customs, institutions -- structural preconditions.
- **PI 240**: "Disputes do not break out (among mathematicians, say) over the question whether a rule has been obeyed or not. People don't come to blows over it, for example. That is part of the framework on which the working of our language is based." Wittgenstein uses the word "framework" here -- structural language.
- **PI 241**: "It is what human beings say that is true and false; and they agree in the language they use. That is not agreement in opinions, but rather in form of life." Agreement in form of life as a structural precondition.
- **PI 242**: "It is not only agreement in definitions but also (odd as it may sound) in judgments that is required." For language to work, you need shared judgments, not just shared definitions. This is a structural observation.
- **PI Part II, xii**: On "very general facts of nature." If certain very general facts of nature were different, "the formation of concepts different from the usual ones will become intelligible." Our grammar corresponds to very general facts of nature -- it is not accountable to reality, but it is not unrelated to it either.
- **PI Part II, xii (p.226)**: "What has to be accepted, the given, is -- one could say -- forms of life." This is Wittgenstein's bedrock statement.

### Zettel

The *Zettel* continues developing the relationship between grammar, regularity, and agreement. It emphasises that following a rule presupposes regularity -- "a repeated or recurring way of acting." The relationship between concepts and "very general facts of nature" (such facts as "mostly do not strike us because of their generality") is developed here. What would be undermined if very general facts of nature were different is "the importance and the point" of certain concepts or language-games.

### Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics (1937-44)

In this work, mathematical propositions are treated as *grammatical*: they function as rules or norms of representation, not as descriptions of mathematical reality. Wittgenstein describes how proofs are "concept-forming" -- they alter the grammar of a proposition.

A key passage: "It is as if we had hardened the empirical proposition into a rule." Empirical regularities, once sufficiently established, become "hardened" into grammatical rules. This is a structural observation about how grammar emerges from practice.

In Part VI, Wittgenstein brings together "the view that in mathematics proofs are 'concept forming' and the view that language and logic and mathematics 'presuppose' common ways of acting and of living." The word "presuppose" here is significant -- these are structural conditions.

### On Certainty (1949-51)

See Question 5 below for the full treatment. This is where the structural thread reaches its most explicit form.

### Where does he reach toward structural observations? Where does he pull back?

**Reaching toward structure:**
- PI 240 ("framework on which the working of our language is based")
- PI 241-242 (agreement in form of life and judgments as required for language)
- PI 371-373 (essence in grammar)
- PI Part II, xii (very general facts of nature; forms of life as the given)
- RFM Part VI (presupposition of common ways of acting)
- On Certainty passim (hinge propositions, riverbed metaphor)

**Pulling back:**
- PI 65-67 (refusing to identify what is common to all language games)
- PI 126 (philosophy puts everything before us, neither explains nor deduces)
- Blue Book (the craving for generality is a source of philosophical confusion)
- PI 109 ("we may not advance any kind of theory")

The pattern is consistent: whenever Wittgenstein appears to make a structural observation, he frames it as a *description* of what is already open to view, not as a *theory* about hidden structures. He reaches toward structure but resists systematising it.

## 4. Depth grammar vs. surface grammar

### The key passage

> "In the use of words one might distinguish 'surface grammar' from 'depth grammar'. What immediately impresses itself upon us about the use of a word is the way it is used in the construction of sentences, the part of its use -- one might say -- that can be taken in by the ear. And now compare the depth grammar, say of the word 'to mean', with what its surface grammar would lead us to suspect. No wonder we find it difficult to know our way about." (PI 664)

### Surface grammar

Surface grammar is the immediately apparent, syntactical aspect of how words are used in sentences. It is what "can be taken in by the ear" -- the formal, audible structure of language. Two words may have similar surface grammars (appearing in similar syntactic positions, taking similar complements) while having radically different depth grammars.

### Depth grammar

Depth grammar is the actual use of a word -- the full range of its employment in language-games, integrated with other human activities. It is not a hidden level beneath surface appearances in the metaphysical sense; it is the wider context of use that is not immediately apparent from syntactic form alone.

The distinction is philosophical, not linguistic. "By 'surface grammar' Wittgenstein does not mean the sign-symbol distinction only, but also what we are inclined to take the meaning of the sign to be (often on the basis of misleading syntactic analogies)."

### The philosophical function

The distinction explains why philosophical problems arise. Words like "mind," "time," "meaning," and "understanding" have surface grammars that suggest they name objects or processes (like "chair" or "running"), when their depth grammars are entirely different. The philosopher's task is to expose these misleading surface analogies by attending to depth grammar.

Example: "I know what I had for breakfast" and "I know what pain feels like" have similar surface grammars but radically different depth grammars. The first reports access to a fact; the second expresses something about the grammar of sensation-words.

### Relationship to structural observations

The depth grammar/surface grammar distinction implies that there *is* a structural level beneath appearances -- not hidden in a metaphysical sense, but obscured by the misleading uniformity of surface forms. Philosophical investigation uncovers this structure. This is compatible with the claim that "everything lies open to view" (PI 126) -- the depth grammar is not hidden, just not immediately obvious.

Importantly, scholars have noted that depth grammar has a "transcendental" dimension -- not transcendent (beyond experience) but constitutive of the conditions under which language functions. As one scholar puts it: "depth grammar needs to be considered as transcendental, but not transcendent, as its rules are constituted by concrete social practice."

### Key sources
- PI 664
- Dobler thesis on grammar and grammatical method
- The Oxford Handbook of Wittgenstein, chapter on grammar
- Lemberger, "Depth Grammar and Surface Grammar of Religious Belief"

## 5. Hinge propositions in On Certainty

### The concept

In *On Certainty* (written 1949-51, the last eighteen months of Wittgenstein's life), hinge propositions are empirical-seeming propositions that function not as claims about the world but as structural preconditions for inquiry. They are what must stay put for the door of inquiry to turn.

> "The questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn." (OC 341)

> "If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put." (OC 343)

Examples include: "There are external objects," "The earth has existed for a long time," "This is a hand." These are not items of knowledge; they are preconditions for knowledge.

### The inherited background

> "But I did not get my picture of the world by satisfying myself of its correctness; nor do I have it because I am satisfied of its correctness. No: it is the inherited background against which I distinguish between true and false." (OC 94)

> "The propositions describing this world-picture might be part of a kind of mythology. And their role is like that of rules of a game; and the game can be learned purely practically, without learning any explicit rules." (OC 95)

Note the structural language: "inherited background," "world-picture," rules of a game that can be learned without explicit formulation. These are structural observations about what must be in place for the language game of truth-and-falsity to operate.

### The riverbed metaphor

> "It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened, and hard ones became fluid." (OC 96)

> "The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other." (OC 97)

> "And the bank of that river consists partly of hard rock, subject to no alteration or only to an imperceptible one, partly of sand, which now in one place now in another gets washed away, or deposited." (OC 99)

This is one of the most structurally rich passages in all of Wittgenstein. The metaphor distinguishes three levels: flowing water (empirical propositions), sand (hinge propositions that can shift over time), and hard rock (propositions that are practically immovable). The structure itself -- the fact that there *must be* a bed for the river to flow -- is a structural observation about the conditions for language.

### Certainty as structural precondition

> "If you are not certain of any fact, you cannot be certain of the meaning of your words either [...] If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty." (OC 114-115)

This is an explicitly structural claim: certainty is a *precondition* for doubt. The language game of doubting presupposes the language game of being certain. You cannot have one without the other. This is not a contingent observation; it is a claim about the logical structure of inquiry.

### Language games as groundless ground

> "You must bear in mind that the language-game is so to say something unpredictable. I mean: it is not based on grounds. It is not reasonable (or unreasonable). It is there -- like our life." (OC 559)

### Scholarly interpretations

**The framework reading** (McGinn, Coliva): Hinge propositions are "judgments of the frame" -- conditions making epistemic practice possible. Like logical truths constitute our techniques of inference, empirical hinges constitute "techniques of empirical description." This is a structural reading.

**The non-epistemic reading** (Strawson, Moyal-Sharrock): Certainty manifests "only as a way of acting" -- it is "animal certainty" expressed through embodied behaviour, not propositional knowledge. Moyal-Sharrock calls these "basic certainties" and ties them to forms of life.

### How far does Wittgenstein take this?

Very far. *On Certainty* is arguably Wittgenstein's most structurally committed work. He comes close to saying: for any language game to operate, there must be an unquestioned background. The riverbed metaphor, the inherited background, the game-of-doubting-presupposes-certainty argument -- these are all structural observations about what must be in place for language to function.

But he still does not systematise this into a theory. He describes the structure without claiming to have discovered a universal law.

## 6. Forms of life as structural

### The concept

*Lebensform* (form of life) is used sparingly in Wittgenstein's work -- five times in the *Philosophical Investigations*, and a handful of times in *On Certainty* and the *Nachlass*. Despite this, it has become one of the most discussed concepts in Wittgenstein scholarship.

> "The speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life." (PI 23)

> "It is what human beings say that is true and false; and they agree in the language they use. That is not agreement in opinions, but rather in form of life." (PI 241)

> "What has to be accepted, the given, is -- one could say -- forms of life." (PI Part II, p.226)

### The scholarly divide

The central debate is between two readings:

**The ethnological/social reading** (Baker & Hacker, and others): Forms of life are "ways of living" -- culturally and historically specific patterns of human activity. Different communities have different forms of life. On this reading, "forms of life" is roughly synonymous with "ways of living" or "cultural practices." This makes the concept purely descriptive and pluralistic.

**The organic/biological reading** (Garver, and to some extent Cavell): A form of life is something biological -- there is one *human* form of life, distinguished from other animal forms of life by the capacity to use language. Garver notes that Wittgenstein uses the term in the singular four times in the PI and only once in the plural, suggesting he is thinking of the human biological form rather than cultural plurality.

**Cavell's dual reading**: Stanley Cavell argued there are both a biological axis (natural history) and an ethological axis (cultural practice) to the concept.

**Moyal-Sharrock's synthesis**: Moyal-Sharrock argues Wittgenstein intended "both a singular and a plural use of the concept; with, where the human is concerned, a single human form of life characterized by innumerable forms of human life." She distinguishes carefully between "form of life" (biological/structural), "patterns of life" (shared regularities), and "ways of living" (cultural diversity).

**Simpson's rejection of the dichotomy**: Simpson claims that neither the ethnological nor the organic interpretation is wholly satisfactory, suggesting instead that Wittgenstein develops a type of naturalism which rejects the dichotomy, with "form of life" expressing "the interconnected complex of language, action, the biological, social processes and psychological processes."

### Does it carry structural weight?

On the organic reading, yes -- strongly. If there is one human form of life, characterised by language capacity and shared natural reactions, then this form of life is a structural condition for any language game. It is what must be in place.

Even on the ethnological reading, there is structural weight. The passage "agreement not in opinions, but rather in form of life" (PI 241) identifies shared form of life as a *condition* for language. Without it, communication fails. And PI 242 adds: "It is not only agreement in definitions but also (odd as it may sound) in judgments that is required." These are structural conditions for language, not just descriptions of cultural variety.

The passage "What has to be accepted, the given, is -- forms of life" (PI p.226) makes the structural claim most explicit. Forms of life are the *given* -- the bedrock that is not explained but accepted. They are what makes everything else possible.

Some scholars have read this as performing a transcendental function: forms of life are "the grounds of phenomena, much like Kant's *Bedingungen der Moglichkeit der Erfahrung* (conditions of the possibility of experience)."

### Garver's Kantian reading

Newton Garver, in *This Complicated Form of Life* (1994), analyses Wittgenstein's relationship to Kant and argues that Wittgenstein completes Kant's revolt against Cartesian epistemology. The givenness of "this complicated form of life" positions Wittgenstein closer to Aristotle than to Kant -- but the structural function is still there.

## 7. The tension: therapeutic anti-essentialism vs. structural observations

### The apparent tension

On one hand: PI 65-67 (family resemblance), the Blue Book's attack on the "craving for generality," PI 126 ("philosophy puts everything before us, and neither explains nor deduces anything"), and PI 109 ("we may not advance any kind of theory").

On the other hand: PI 371-373 ("Essence is expressed in grammar"), PI 240 ("framework on which the working of our language is based"), PI 241-242 (agreement in form of life and judgments as required for language), and the whole of *On Certainty*.

How can the same philosopher both refuse to say what is common to all language games and assert that essence is expressed in grammar?

### Scholarly positions

**1. Sorin Bangu (2005, *Metaphysica*)**: Bangu argues that "the family resemblance idea is not meant to reject essentialism, but to render this doctrine irrelevant by dissipating its philosophical force." On this reading, PI 65-67 is not a positive claim that language games lack common features; it is a therapeutic move designed to make the *question* of whether they have common features lose its philosophical grip. The family resemblance point is about method, not metaphysics.

Bangu reads PI 133 as the key: "the philosophical problems should completely disappear." The goal is dissolution of the problem, not a counter-thesis.

**2. Merrill Ring (2019)**: Ring argues the apparent tension arises from misreading. Wittgenstein did not reject essentialism at PI 65-67; he rejected a *particular conception* of essence (essence as a set of necessary and sufficient conditions discoverable by analysis). He then offers an *alternative* conception at PI 371-373: essence as expressed in grammar. These are compatible, not contradictory.

**3. The therapeutic reading (new Wittgensteinians)**: Some scholars (Crary, Read, Diamond) hold that Wittgenstein makes no positive philosophical claims at all -- everything is therapeutic. On this view, PI 371-373 are themselves therapeutic remarks designed to dissolve a particular confusion, not positive assertions about the nature of essence. This reading has the virtue of consistency but the cost of making Wittgenstein say nothing.

**4. The Cavell-inflected reading**: Cavell and those influenced by him hold that family resemblance shows that "concepts do not usually have, and do not need 'rigid limits'" -- but this is compatible with concepts having a grammar, and that grammar expressing what we might call their essence. The family resemblance argument is about rigid boundaries; the grammar/essence passages are about constitutive rules. These operate at different levels.

### A coherent resolution?

The most persuasive resolution runs as follows:

- At PI 65-67, Wittgenstein rejects the demand for a *definition* of "language game" in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions. There is no single feature common to all games.
- At PI 371-373, Wittgenstein says that the essence of any particular thing is constituted by its grammar -- the rules governing its use.
- These are compatible because "essence expressed in grammar" does not require a *single* essence shared by all language games. Each language game has its own grammar, its own constitutive rules. The claim is that for any given phenomenon, its nature is constituted by its grammar -- not that all phenomena share a nature.

The tension remains real, however, regarding the *structural* observations -- the passages where Wittgenstein seems to identify conditions that any language game must meet (agreement in form of life, certainty as precondition for doubt, etc.). These go beyond saying "each game has its own grammar" and approach saying "there are conditions any game must satisfy." Wittgenstein never quite resolves this -- which is what makes *On Certainty* so philosophically charged.

## 8. Does Wittgenstein ask what structural conditions must be met for something to count as a language game?

### The short answer

He comes close. Repeatedly. But he never quite asks the question in that form, and he never gives a systematic answer.

### The passages that come closest

**PI 199**: "To follow a rule, to make a report, to give an order, to play a game of chess, are customs (usages, institutions)." This identifies practice, custom, and institutional context as conditions for rule-following -- and rule-following is central to language games.

**PI 240**: "Disputes do not break out (among mathematicians, say) over the question whether a rule has been obeyed or not [...] That is part of the framework on which the working of our language is based." The word "framework" is explicitly structural.

**PI 241-242**: Agreement in form of life and agreement in judgments are "required" for language. This is a statement of necessary conditions.

**PI Part II, xii**: Very general facts of nature correspond to our concepts. If those facts were different, our concepts would be different. This implies that language games have natural preconditions.

**OC 94-99**: The inherited background, the riverbed. Language games require an unquestioned background of certainties. This is a structural precondition.

**OC 114-115**: "The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty." An explicit statement that one language game (doubting) has a structural precondition (certainty).

**OC 559**: "The language-game is so to say something unpredictable [...] It is not based on grounds. It is there -- like our life." Even this anti-foundationalist statement carries structural weight: language games are *there*, as given conditions of human existence.

### What he identifies as structural conditions (implicitly)

Across his works, the following emerge as conditions for language games:

1. **Regularity and agreement**: Shared patterns of action and judgment (PI 240-242)
2. **Custom and institution**: Practices embedded in social forms (PI 199)
3. **A form of life**: Shared biological and cultural background (PI 241, p.226)
4. **An unquestioned background**: Hinge propositions or certainties that are not themselves subject to the game (OC 94-99, 114-115, 341-343)
5. **Very general facts of nature**: Natural conditions that make our particular concepts intelligible (PI Part II, xii)
6. **Publicity of rules**: Rules must be followable by others, not merely private (the private language argument, PI 243ff)

### Why he never asks the question directly

Three reasons, all consistent with his method:

1. **Therapeutic resistance**: Asking "what conditions must all language games meet?" is itself a form of the "craving for generality" he diagnoses as philosophical error.
2. **Anti-theoretical commitment**: PI 109 -- "we may not advance any kind of theory." To specify structural conditions would be to advance a theory of language.
3. **The family resemblance point**: His own argument suggests there may be no single set of conditions common to all language games -- just overlapping similarities.

Yet the observations are there. He *describes* conditions that he finds language games meeting, while refusing to *theorise* that these are universal requirements. The descriptions accumulate -- across PI, Zettel, RFM, and especially On Certainty -- into something that looks very much like a structural account, without ever being presented as one.

### The gap this leaves

Wittgenstein opens a space but refuses to occupy it. He identifies structural features of language games -- regularity, agreement, background certainty, form of life, natural facts -- but declines to ask whether these are universal conditions. A philosopher who wanted to ask "what must any language game have in order to count as a language game?" would find in Wittgenstein all the materials for an answer, but not the answer itself.

This is either principled restraint (the therapeutic method forbids the question) or an unfinished project (the structural observations point toward a question he never quite posed). Scholars divide on this, and the question may not have a determinate answer.

## Summary of key findings

1. **Grammar** is Wittgenstein's term for the full system of rules governing meaningful use. It is constitutive (determines what kind of object anything is), autonomous (not answerable to reality), and replaces logical form as the structural backbone of language.

2. **Essence is expressed in grammar** means that the nature of a thing is constituted by the grammatical rules governing its use -- not discovered in reality but established through practice. This is a novel form of essentialism, not anti-essentialism.

3. **The grammar/essence thread runs across all of Wittgenstein's later works**, from the Blue Book through On Certainty. It becomes progressively more structural in the later works, especially in On Certainty's hinge propositions.

4. **Depth grammar vs. surface grammar** (PI 664) is the distinction between how a word appears to function (syntactic form) and how it actually functions (full context of use). This implies a structural level beneath appearances.

5. **Hinge propositions** in On Certainty are structural preconditions for language games -- they must remain unquestioned for inquiry to proceed. This is Wittgenstein's most explicitly structural work.

6. **Forms of life** carry structural weight on most scholarly readings -- either as biological/organic conditions (Garver) or as transcendental conditions of possibility (the Kantian reading). Moyal-Sharrock's synthesis preserves both dimensions.

7. **The tension** between therapeutic anti-essentialism and structural observations is real but resolvable. The family resemblance argument rejects a particular *kind* of essentialism (necessary and sufficient conditions); the grammar/essence passages offer an alternative (essence as constitutive grammar). The structural observations in On Certainty push further than the PI's therapeutic method would seem to allow.

8. **Wittgenstein never directly asks what conditions must be met for something to count as a language game**, but he describes such conditions repeatedly: regularity, agreement in form of life, background certainty, very general facts of nature. The materials for a structural account are there; the account itself is not.
