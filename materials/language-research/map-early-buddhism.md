# Early Buddhism (Pali Canon) — Map

## Initial phrases

"Language is conventional (sammuti), useful for communication but unable to capture reality as it truly is."

Language is rooted in papanca (conceptual proliferation/reification) — carving the flux of experience into apparently stable entities, leading to attachment.

The Buddha used language skillfully (upaya) — skillful means to guide beings toward liberation.

---

## Mapping statement

Early Buddhism sees reality as dependent origination (paticca-samuppada) — everything arises in dependence on conditions, nothing exists independently. Everything is relationally constituted. This is deeply consistent with how we think: nothing exists on its own, everything depends on relations.

The three marks of existence — impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), non-self (anatta) — describe a reality in continuous flux, where what we call "things" are conventional labels applied to patterns. Language is conventional (sammuti) — it carves the continuous flux of experience into apparently stable entities. Useful for communication, but the carving itself creates the conditions for attachment.

The Buddha used language skillfully (upaya) — as a means, not an end. This is an observation about how language works: it is a tool within the practice of liberation, employed with awareness of what it does and what it cannot reach.

Two truths sit alongside each other: conventional truth (sammuti-sacca) — everyday reality, real enough for practical purposes; and ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca) — reality as it actually is, beyond what conventional language carves. From where we stand, this parallels our distinction between epistemological reality (reality within a language) and reality in potential (what sits beyond all reach). Two frameworks arriving at a similar structure from very different concerns.

Buddhism's concern is soteriological — liberation from suffering caused by attachment to what language carves out of the flux. Our concern is structural — mapping the relational across languages. The relational nature described is the same; the purpose of the description differs. Buddhism seeks release from the relational entanglement. We seek to recognise and trace it.

There is deep thought here that we want to return to.
