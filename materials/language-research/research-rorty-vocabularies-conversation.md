## Vocabularies — Rorty's unit of analysis

### What a "vocabulary" is

For Rorty, "vocabulary" replaces traditional units like "proposition," "belief," or "idea." A vocabulary is a coherent set of words, metaphors, and descriptions that a community or individual uses to make sense of the world, justify actions, and articulate hopes and doubts. "What matters in the end are changes in the vocabulary rather than changes in belief, changes in truth-value candidates rather than assignments of truth-value" (CIS, 47-48).

The term is deliberately chosen over alternatives:

**Vocabulary vs. language.** "Language" implies a stable, shared system governed by conventions. Rorty, drawing on Davidson's argument that "there is no such thing as a language, not if a language is anything like what many philosophers and linguists have supposed" ("A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs," 1986), uses "vocabulary" to denote something more fluid, contingent, and purpose-driven.

**Vocabulary vs. paradigm.** Although Rorty draws heavily on Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts, his concept is broader. Kuhn's paradigms are discipline-specific scientific frameworks. Rorty generalises to all discourse: moral, political, aesthetic, personal.

**Vocabulary vs. conceptual scheme.** The sharpest contrast. Drawing on Davidson's demolition of the scheme-content distinction ("On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme," 1974), Rorty argues that "we should discuss vocabularies in relation to other vocabularies" rather than vocabularies in relation to reality. There is no neutral content that different schemes organise differently.

### Vocabularies as contingent tools

The core argument from CIS Chapter 1 ("The Contingency of Language"):

1. Truth is a property of sentences, not the world. "Only descriptions of the world can be true or false" (CIS, 5-7).
2. Vocabularies are made, not discovered. "Languages are made rather than found" (CIS, 7).
3. The tool metaphor replaces the mirror metaphor. Vocabularies are "tools for coping, not bits of a jigsaw puzzle" (CIS, 77).
4. Progress is metaphorical, not convergent. History is "a history of increasingly useful metaphors" (CIS, 20).

"Our vocabularies have no more of a representational relation to an intrinsic nature of things than does the anteater's snout or the bowerbird's skill at weaving" (Truth and Progress, 48).

### How vocabularies relate to each other

**No meta-vocabulary.** "There is no meta-vocabulary to distinguish the adequacy of one final vocabulary above others." No neutral standpoint, no God's-eye view from which vocabularies can be ranked.

**Comparison without neutral criteria.** Rorty does not deny we compare vocabularies — we do so all the time. The ironist compares by "pitting [them] against each other," using "redescription rather than inference" (CIS, 80). We can ask which vocabulary is more useful for particular purposes, but not which is "truer."

**Incommensurability.** Defined as: "able to be brought under a set of rules which will tell us how rational agreement can be reached on what would settle the issue on every point where statements seem to conflict" (PMN, 316). Incommensurability means this is not possible — but this is not the impossibility of communication. It is the impossibility of algorithmic adjudication.

The public vocabulary of justice and the private vocabulary of self-creation are "equally valid, yet forever incommensurable" (CIS, xv). Not a defect to be overcome but a permanent feature.

**Conversation, not commensuration.** Different vocabularies meet "obliquely" — they "acknowledge each other and enjoy an oblique relationship which neither requires nor forecasts their being assimilated to one another" (Oakeshott's formulation, adopted by Rorty).

### Redescription as the engine of change

New vocabularies do not refute old ones — they make them look bad. Drawing on Nietzsche: "One error after another is coolly placed on ice; the ideal is not refuted — it freezes to death."

Key examples from CIS: the French Revolution showed "the whole vocabulary of social relations could be replaced almost overnight." Galileo's heliocentric framework replaced Ptolemaic geocentrism without claiming to uncover an eternal cosmic order. Freud's vocabulary of the unconscious "didn't uncover truth but offered tools for self-description that stuck."

The mechanism: metaphors "do not have meanings" in the traditional sense but produce "extra-linguistic effects." Old metaphors "constantly die off into literalness and then serve as a platform and foil for new metaphors."

## The final vocabulary and the ironist

"All human beings carry about a set of words which they employ to justify their actions, their beliefs, and their lives" (CIS, 73). "Final" not because it is the last word, but because "it is one which we cannot help using, for when we reach it, our spade is turned."

An ironist fulfils three conditions (CIS, 73):
1. She has "radical and continuing doubts about the final vocabulary she currently uses, because she has been impressed by other vocabularies."
2. She realises arguments in her present vocabulary "can neither underwrite nor dissolve these doubts."
3. She does not think "her vocabulary is closer to reality than others."

The metaphysician believes it is possible to discover the true nature of reality. The ironist understands that any description will be adopted for contingent reasons.

The ironist's stance implies a kind of equal standing: "there is nothing beyond vocabularies which serves as a criterion of choice between them" (CIS, 80). No vocabulary has a privileged ontological position. But this is not the same as saying all vocabularies are equally good for all purposes.

## Conversation — what happens between vocabularies

### The "conversation of mankind"

Borrowed from Oakeshott's *The Voice of Poetry in the Conversation of Mankind* (1959). Philosophy is one voice among many — not a privileged arbiter but a participant.

Conversation replaces epistemology. "We understand knowledge when we understand the social justification of belief, and thus have no need to view it as accuracy of representation" (PMN, 170). Philosophy's role shifts from foundational policing to participation in open-ended dialogue.

### Normal vs. abnormal discourse

Normal discourse: conducted within "an agreed-upon set of conventions about what counts as a relevant contribution, what counts as answering a question" (PMN, 320). Generalised from Kuhn's normal science to all discourse.

Abnormal discourse: "when someone joins in the discourse who is ignorant of these conventions or who sets them aside" (PMN, 320). Can produce "anything from nonsense to intellectual revolution."

Systematic philosophers work within normal discourse. Edifying philosophers — Wittgenstein, Heidegger, Dewey — engage in abnormal discourse, aiming not to discover truth but to keep the conversation going.

### Davidson's passing theories

Davidson argues in "A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs" (1986):

- A "prior theory" is what a hearer brings to an encounter — expectations about what words mean. A "passing theory" is what she constructs on the fly to interpret a particular utterance.
- Communication succeeds not because we share a language but because we are endlessly creative at constructing passing theories.

Rorty seizes on this: conversation across vocabularies does not require a shared framework. It requires only the ability to adjust, to construct passing theories, to interpret charitably. Communication is not governed by conventions but by practical interpretive flexibility.

### Davidson's triangulation

Davidson's later work: knowledge of oneself, knowledge of other persons, and knowledge of a common objective world form "an interdependent set of concepts no one of which is possible in the absence of the others." Communication requires at least two creatures interacting with each other and with common objects.

This gives language an inherently social character — meaning is essentially intersubjective — without requiring speakers to share a fixed language system.

### The Gadamer connection

Key convergences: both reject understanding as requiring a shared method; both see understanding as dialogical; both emphasise historical situatedness.

Key divergence: Gadamer rejects incommensurability. His "fusion of horizons" implies real achievement of understanding across difference. Rorty is more sceptical: "all we can do is to show how the other side looks from our own point of view." Rorty strips Gadamer's hermeneutics of its ontological ambitions and uses it as a model for non-foundational conversation.

## Solidarity — the social ground

### Solidarity vs. objectivity

Two incompatible self-images for inquiry ("Solidarity or Objectivity?" 1985). The "objectivity" camp seeks something non-human — God, Reason, Nature. The "solidarity" camp grounds knowledge in community practices. "There is nothing to be said about either truth or rationality apart from descriptions of the familiar procedures of justification which a given society — ours — uses."

### Extending "we"

Moral progress is "thinking of more and diverse people as 'one of us'" — expanding the circle of solidarity. Not through rational argument from universal principles but through exposure to suffering: novels, journalism, ethnography that make distant pain present. The Danes who sheltered Jews did so because they saw them as neighbours, not because they worked out a Kantian theory.

### Ethnocentrism

"The option to be non-ethnocentric does not exist." We always argue from within a tradition. But Rorty's ethnocentrism is expansive — Western liberal democracy is "the ethnocentrism of a 'we-community' which is dedicated to enlarging itself." This is not relativism because the relativist claims "every belief on a certain topic is as good as every other," which Rorty denies.

## Language, experience, and reality

### The rejection of "experience"

Beginning with "Dewey's Metaphysics" (1977), Rorty argued Dewey should have dropped "experience" and replaced it with language/discourse. Experience is "a fifth wheel" — it courts representationalism by suggesting something given prior to language. Where Dewey said "experience," Rorty says "discourse."

Toward the end of his life: "I regard [Dewey's theory of experience] as the worst part of Dewey. I'd be glad if he had never written *Experience and Nature*."

**Koopman's critique.** Colin Koopman ("Language is a Form of Experience," *Transactions of the Charles S. Peirce Society*, 2007) identifies the experience-vs-language divide as the most divisive conflict in pragmatism. His third way: "language is itself a form of experience" — not opposed to it. Classical pragmatists who centre experience risk foundationalism; neopragmatists who centre language risk losing the richness of non-linguistic life.

**Shusterman's critique.** Even if Sellars's critique of the Myth of the Given shows immediate somatic experience cannot ground epistemological foundationalism, this does not preclude its philosophical value. "The anti-foundationalist thrust of the notion of language is not as clear as Rorty sees it" — language has frequently been used as a foundationalist category too. Dewey wanted to celebrate "the richness and value of immediate experience, including the immediate dimension of somatic experience" — not to ground knowledge claims.

### Anti-representationalism

"The world is out there, but descriptions of the world are not" (CIS, 5). The world exists independently of us, but truth is a property of sentences, and sentences are human creations.

This is not idealism. Rorty does not deny reality exists. He denies reality has an intrinsic conceptual structure our language must mirror. The world causally constrains us but does not dictate one privileged description. He rejects both realism and anti-realism as "products of mistaken representationalist assumptions about language."

"Language is more like an arm than it is a veil."

### Mediation vs. constitution

The phrase "all awareness is a linguistic affair" originates from Wilfrid Sellars ("Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind," 1956) — Sellars's thesis of "psychological nominalism." Rorty endorsed and extended it: if there is no pre-linguistic "given" that grounds knowledge, then awareness is always already shaped by language.

Rorty's position leans toward constitution rather than mediation. There is no access to experience independent of how we talk about it. But he does not say reality is made of language — he says our awareness of reality is linguistic.

### Rorty and Kant

Rorty accepts Kant's anti-empiricist thrust — the subject structures experience. But he radically rejects Kant's execution: "Kant's attempts to find the necessary presuppositions of any possible experience" are exactly the foundationalism Rorty dismantles.

Where Kant posited universal, necessary categories, Rorty makes them historical, contingent, culturally specific. "The common message of Wittgenstein, Dewey, and Heidegger is a historicist one" — philosophy should not attempt "to escape from history."

The Davidson-Rorty move against the scheme/content distinction: the idea that you can distinguish within knowledge between a conceptual component (scheme) and an empirical component (content) is incoherent. Kant's transcendental account involves subsumption of intuitions (content) under concepts (scheme). Davidson shows this distinction cannot be maintained. Rorty draws the consequence: there is no neutral ground from which to compare scheme and content, language and world.

### Knowledge and objectivity

"We understand knowledge when we understand the social justification of belief" (PMN). Knowledge is "a compliment paid to the beliefs which we think so well justified that further justification is not needed."

Justification is not a relation between sentences and the world but between sentences and other sentences — a social practice. "Only a belief can justify a belief."

## Rorty and Wittgenstein — where they diverge

Rorty adopts the core Wittgensteinian insight (meaning is use, language consists of multiple irreducible games). But:

**Contingency vs. stability.** Wittgenstein treats forms of life as bedrock — what cannot be further explained. For Rorty, forms of life are thoroughly contingent and historical. Where Wittgenstein stops ("This is simply what I do"), Rorty historicises: this is what we happen to do now, and it will change.

**Therapeutic vs. revolutionary.** Wittgenstein's aim is to dissolve philosophical confusion by returning us to ordinary language. Rorty's aim is the creation of new vocabularies that replace old ones. Wittgenstein is quietist about ordinary language; Rorty is an enthusiast for vocabulary change.

**Pragmatic evaluation.** Where Wittgenstein asks "What are we doing with this language?", Rorty asks the Davidsonian question: "Does our use of these words get in the way of our use of those other words?" An evaluative, forward-looking question Wittgenstein would not have asked.

## Rorty and Peirce — why Rorty dismissed him

"Rorty's least favourite pragmatist is Peirce, whom he regards as subject to both scheme-content dualism and to a degree of scientism" (Stanford Encyclopedia). "Indifference to Peirce's work — among self-styled pragmatists from the 1980s on — is, by now, a badge of honor among the more Rortyan of the Dewey enthusiasts."

**Triadic vs. dyadic.** Popescu ("Unlimited Semiosis or Endless Redescriptions," *Revue Roumaine de Philosophie*, 2022) argues that Peirce's unlimited semiosis envisions interpretation through a triadic structure (Sign-Object-Interpretant), whereas Rorty's redescription is a dyadic relation between what is interpreted and the interpreter. Peirce's interpretant constrains interpretation; for Rorty, "there are no limits to interpretation that are not self-imposed."

**Scientism.** Peirce's convergence theory of truth — inquiry in the long run converges on the truth — struck Rorty as scientistic.

**Scholastic realism.** Peirce's commitment to the reality of generals (universals) is metaphysical realism Rorty flatly rejected.

**System-building.** Rorty "prefers the 'Jamesian,' or 'literary,' Dewey to the 'Peircean,' or theoretical Dewey."

## Rorty and Habermas — the universal presuppositions debate

This debate extended over three decades.

**Habermas's position.** Every act of communication oriented toward understanding raises three validity claims: truth (fit with the outer world), rightness (fit with social norms), and sincerity (fit with the speaker's inner world). These are structural presuppositions of communicative competence, not optional. A truth claim must be "defensible on the basis of good reasons at any time and against anybody."

**Rorty's rejection.** The only "ideal presupposed by discourse" is "that of being able to justify your beliefs to a competent audience" — a specific community, not "anybody at any time." "Everybody is ethnocentric when engaged in actual debate" — being so is "to divide the human race into the people to whom one must justify one's beliefs and the others."

**Habermas's critique.** Rorty cannot distinguish "manipulation from argumentation," "learning from indoctrination," or "motivating through reason" from "causal exertion of influence." By "flattening out truth" into practices of justification, Rorty "loses sight of the potential power of validity claims to explode contexts of justification."

**The performative contradiction charge.** When Rorty argues that there is no truth beyond social justification, he presents this claim as... true. The very act of discourse presupposes universal pragmatic commitments (sincerity, truthfulness, rightness) that transcend any particular vocabulary.

Both agree philosophy should serve freedom and democracy. The disagreement is about how.

## Brandom — the structural account Rorty refused to give

Robert Brandom (Rorty's doctoral student at Princeton) dedicated *Making It Explicit* to Sellars and Rorty. He dedicated *Between Saying and Doing* (2008) to Rorty, "for whom I am still rewriting my dissertation."

### What Brandom takes from Rorty

"The normative pragmatist insight that all matters of authority and responsibility are ultimately matters of social practice, and never matters of ontology." Justification is social. Meaning is use. Norms are instituted by communities.

### What Brandom adds

**Inferentialism.** Meaning consists in inferential role — what follows from an assertion, what would justify it, what it is incompatible with. *Making It Explicit* (1994) is "the first attempt to work out in detail a theory that renders linguistic meaning in terms of use."

**Deontic scorekeeping.** Participants in discourse track each other's commitments (what you are bound to assert) and entitlements (what you are permitted to assert). These are "the currency of social practice."

**Rehabilitated objectivity.** Rorty says we should stop looking for non-human authority over our claims. Brandom argues objectivity can be reconstituted within the social framework: objects acquire "normative significance of serving as standards for assessment" through scorekeeping practices. He describes "a kind of authority over the correctness of our thinking" that we grant "to the things we are (thereby) thinking about." Objectivity emerges from social practice rather than preceding it.

**Explicitation.** Brandom's project renders explicit "the norms which implicitly govern the social and historical structure that language is." Where Rorty pointed to the social character of language and stopped, Brandom maps the normative structure in detail.

### Does Brandom fill Rorty's gaps?

He fills the theoretical gap — Rorty gestured at language as social practice; Brandom explains how it works. But Brandom's rationalist framework may not accommodate "excluded or nonconforming voices" as well as Rorty's open-ended conversationalism. Rorty's model "better accommodates normative rupture" — the kind of abnormal discourse that breaks existing norms. Brandom systematises what Rorty left fluid; the question is whether the systematisation loses something essential.

## The convergent critique — what Rorty presupposes but refuses to theorise

Multiple critics converge on the same structural objection: Rorty's framework implicitly relies on something common across vocabularies that he officially denies.

### Putnam

Rorty "abandons the normative task of rational legitimation central to pragmatism." If justification is merely sociological, "how can we meaningfully say the majority is wrong?" Rorty's framework "leaves no room for the idea that norms, either epistemic or ethical ones, could be genuinely better or worse."

### Habermas

The performative contradiction: by arguing for his position, Rorty presupposes the very normative standards he officially denies. The very act of discourse presupposes universal pragmatic commitments that transcend any particular vocabulary.

### Geras

*Solidarity in the Conversation of Humankind* (1995). Rorty's own moral commitments — "cruelty is the worst thing we do" — presuppose universal facts about human nature (the capacity for suffering) that his framework officially denies.

Geras identifies four meanings of "no human nature" in Rorty, often contradictory: (1) no commonly shared traits; (2) no distinctively human traits; (3) no traits of universal moral significance; (4) no universal aspiration to one goal. Geras accepts (4) as innocuous. He rejects (1)-(3) as false and notes Rorty himself contradicts them: "all human beings share the ability to be humiliated."

The Holocaust rescuers: Rorty claimed they acted from local solidarity ("one of us"). Geras counters with testimony — their self-declared motive was "the feeling that the victims were simply fellow human beings." Not fellow Danes, not fellow workers, but fellow humans.

### Bernstein

Rorty misses what is genuinely emerging: not the collapse of all standards but a new understanding of rationality that is neither objectivist nor relativist. The conversational model requires genuine mutual understanding, not just parallel monologue.

### McDowell

Rorty's "view of the relation between agent and world as merely causal runs afoul of the notion that our very concept of a creature with beliefs involves the idea of a rational constraint of the world on our epistemic states."

### The convergence

All press on the same point: **the very possibility of conversation between vocabularies, of redescription, of the ironist being "impressed by other vocabularies" — all of this requires that something is shared or sharable across vocabularies.** Whether this is called truth (Habermas), rational acceptability (Putnam), human nature (Geras), rational constraint (McDowell), or fusion of horizons (Bernstein/Gadamer), the point is the same: Rorty's practice presupposes something his theory denies.

## Rorty on domain-specific vocabularies and pluralism

### Different domains, different vocabularies

Rorty treats vocabularies as purpose-relative tools. Just as you would never ask which tool is "most adequate to reality" in the abstract, you cannot ask which vocabulary is truer without specifying purpose. "Questions about usefulness can only be answered once we give substance to our purposes."

He refuses to rank: natural science vocabularies have no ontological superiority over literary, moral, or political ones. Science is admirable for "the moral virtues of scientific communities" — openness, willingness to revise — not for getting closer to reality. Scientism repeats the error of monotheism: "substituting the notion of Truth as One."

Normal discourse maps onto domain-specific language: each domain has its "agreed-upon practices of inquiry" with its own criteria for relevance and adequacy. Commensuration is "a characteristic of any form of inquiry where there are agreed-upon practices — as easily in academic art, scholastic philosophy, or parliamentary politics as in normal science."

### No ecology of vocabularies

Rorty does not develop a formal framework for how vocabularies coexist, interact, or form ecosystems. He uses Darwinian imagery: vocabulary change is evolutionary — "new forms of life constantly killing off old forms — not to accomplish a higher purpose, but blindly." But the ecology metaphor stays implicit. Rorty provides no structural account of how vocabularies relate — no theory of translation, partial overlap, or vocabulary landscape.

His point is precisely that no such framework is possible or needed: vocabularies are incommensurable, conversation is all we have.

### The gap in the literature

There is no major systematic study mapping Rorty's vocabulary pluralism onto domain-specific languages, specialist registers, or language differentiation as studied in linguistics or the sociology of knowledge. The connection is implicit rather than explicitly theorised.

## The "no meta-vocabulary" tension

### The problem

Rorty claims no vocabulary is privileged. He also claims all vocabularies are contingent, made not found, tools not mirrors. The second claim is itself a claim about all vocabularies — it functions as a meta-vocabulary. A universal statement about the nature of all vocabularies, which is exactly what Rorty says cannot be made.

### Rorty's defences

**Therapeutic.** He is not making truth-claims about all vocabularies; he is trying to help us drop a bad habit.

**Ironic.** The liberal ironist recognises the contingency of her own commitments, including the commitment to contingency. CIS's apparent contradictions are features — "designed to frustrate the expectation of a unified coherent reading."

**Redescriptive.** He is not arguing his position is true. He is offering an alternative vocabulary and inviting us to see whether it is more useful.

### The critics' response

Habermas: performative contradiction — the activity of making universal claims contradicts the content. Putnam: self-refuting relativism. The "stretching critique": we can "stretch the meaning of language to cover everything theoretically, but the stretching produces a pragmatic distortion that casts doubt on the correctness or usefulness of the vocabulary."
