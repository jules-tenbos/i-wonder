## Wittgenstein

### The shift from early to later

**Early (Tractatus, 1921).** Language works by picturing reality. The core mechanism is isomorphism: a meaningful proposition pictures a state of affairs by sharing its logical form. Names go proxy for simple objects. The structure of language mirrors the structure of reality. "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world" (5.6). "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent" (7).

**What drove the change.** Three catalysts:

1. *The Brouwer Lecture (March 1928).* After attending Brouwer's lecture in Vienna, Wittgenstein "suddenly and very volubly began talking philosophy — at great length" (Feigl's report). He returned to Cambridge in 1929.

2. *Frank Ramsey's criticism.* Ramsey pressed him on weaknesses in the Tractatus framework.

3. *The Sraffa gesture.* On a train, Wittgenstein insisted a proposition and what it describes must have the same logical form. The Italian economist Piero Sraffa made a Neapolitan gesture — brushing the underneath of his chin — and asked: "What is the logical form of *that*?" Wittgenstein said the discussions with Sraffa made him feel "like a tree from which all branches had been cut." He credited Sraffa in the preface to the *Investigations* with "the most fruitful ideas" of the book.

**The specific shifts:**

1. From logical form to use. "For a large class of cases... the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (PI 43).
2. From ideal logical language to ordinary language. "Back to the rough ground!" (PI 107).
3. From fixed reference to activity. Language is "part of an activity, or of a form of life" (PI 23).
4. From single logic to irreducible plurality. An indeterminate number of language games, each with its own grammar.
5. From contemplating subject to embedded practitioner. The "craving for generality" is diagnosed as the root of philosophical confusion.

The *Investigations* opens with Augustine's description of a child learning language by associating words with objects — precisely the view Wittgenstein dismantles.

### Language games

**The builder's game (PI 2).** Builder A and assistant B. A calls out "slab," "pillar," "block," "beam"; B brings the corresponding stone. Wittgenstein asks us to conceive this as a complete primitive language — not a fragment but a self-contained system.

**The list (PI 23).** Wittgenstein gives a deliberately incomplete list: giving orders, describing objects, reporting events, speculating, forming hypotheses, making up stories, play-acting, singing, guessing riddles, making jokes, solving arithmetic, translating, asking, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying. He comments: "the speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life."

The multiplicity is "not something fixed, given once for all" — "new types of language, new language-games, come into existence, and others become obsolete and get forgotten" (PI 23).

**Forms of life (Lebensformen).** Language games are embedded in shared backgrounds of human cultural practices, activities, and ways of living. "It is characteristic of language that its foundation consists in steady forms of life, with function determined above all by action."

**Family resemblance (PI 65-67).** Wittgenstein poses the question directly: "What is common to all these activities and what makes them into language or parts of language?" His answer: we will not find a single common feature. Instead, "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing: sometimes overall similarities, sometimes similarities of detail" (PI 66). He calls this family resemblance. A deliberate anti-essentialist move — he "harbored the revolutionary idea of an antidote for essentialism, a notion of family resemblance, with the ambition to dissolve forever the illusion of 'common properties'."

**Relation between games.** He does not develop a theory of how language games relate to each other systematically. They overlap via family resemblance. No structural account of their interconnection. Consistent with his therapeutic method — any such account would be the kind of general theory he explicitly resists.

### The therapeutic aim

"Philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity" (Tractatus 4.112 — the therapeutic impulse is present even in the early work). "The philosopher treats a question; like an illness" (PI 255). "To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (PI 309). "Philosophy leaves everything as it is" (PI 124).

Philosophical problems arise because philosophers are "in the grip of philosophical pictures" — misapplied metaphors. Therapy consists of exposing these pictures and showing the problems dissolve once we deny them.

The confusion stems from the gap between surface grammar and depth grammar: words that look grammatically similar (e.g., "I have a pain" / "I have a pin") lead us to think they function the same way, when their depth grammar is entirely different.

"If one tried to advance theses in philosophy, it would never be possible to debate them, because everyone would agree to them" (PI 128).

The "New Wittgenstein" school (Diamond, Conant, Floyd, Cavell) extends this therapeutic reading to the Tractatus itself — even there the intent was to bring the reader to see the "hopelessness of traditional philosophy."

**Does this foreclose building?** Wittgenstein's prohibition is on philosophical theory-building (constructing metaphysical explanations for why language works the way it does). He is not obviously prohibiting the observation that language games share structural features — he simply chose not to look. Whether the seed's structural observation constitutes "craving for generality" or is a legitimate observation is the question.

Note: "Essence is expressed in grammar" (PI 371). "Grammar tells what kind of object anything is" (PI 373). These commit Wittgenstein to things having essences — just not the kind traditional philosophy looked for. Scholars have noted the tension between these passages and the family resemblance arguments at PI 65ff.

### Rule-following and private language

**The rule-following paradox (PI 185-202).** A student taught "add 2" goes correctly to 1000 but then writes 1004, 1008... He insists he is following the same rule. "No course of action could be determined by a rule, because any course of action can be made out to accord with the rule" (PI 201a). Rules for interpreting rules require their own interpretation — infinite regress.

**Resolution.** There must be a way of grasping a rule that is not a matter of adding an interpretation — it is achieved through training that institutes one into an enduring practice. "When I obey a rule, I do not choose. I obey the rule blindly" (PI 219). Rule-following is grounded in shared practice.

**Kripke's sceptical reading (1982).** The "quaddition" problem — any finite set of applications is compatible with bizarre rules. His "sceptical solution": a language-user's correct rule-following is justified by the community's expectations. Meaning is grounded in communal practice. (Controversial — many scholars argue Kripke misrepresents Wittgenstein.)

**The private language argument (PI 243-315).** A language whose expressions are in principle understandable by only a single individual is unintelligible. The diary example: writing "S" for a recurring sensation. If S just means "whatever I now feel," then "whatever seems right to me is right," and the distinction between correct and incorrect use collapses.

**The beetle in a box (PI 293).** Everyone has a box with a "beetle" no one else can see. "The thing in the box has no place in the language-game at all; not even as a *something*: for the box might even be empty."

Both arguments converge: meaning requires a public, shared framework. Language is constitutively social and relational — it cannot exist as a purely private phenomenon.

### Wittgenstein and Saussure

Roy Harris, *Language, Saussure and Wittgenstein: How to Play Games with Words* (Routledge, 1988). Chess is both Saussure's and Wittgenstein's favourite metaphor for language, developed independently. Both use it to show meaning is positional/relational, not intrinsic.

Shared positions: rejection of naive referentialism; meaning as differential/relational; anti-essentialism; the game metaphor. The convergence is genuinely independent — different intellectual traditions (Genevan linguistics vs Frege-Russell logical analysis), no evidence of mutual influence.

Key differences: Saussure focuses on langue (system) vs parole (speech acts); Wittgenstein does not make this dichotomy. Saussure wants to found a science of language; Wittgenstein wants to dissolve philosophical problems. Saussure focuses on the synchronic system; Wittgenstein on the dynamic activity of playing.

### Wittgenstein and the analytical/continental divide

He sits in the analytical tradition but his later work resonates strongly with continental concerns. Heidegger has been called "the Wittgenstein of the Continental tradition." Both share interest in everyday engagement, focusing on ordinary matters.

Lyotard's *The Postmodern Condition* (1979) appropriated language games directly. Comparative studies exist between Wittgenstein and Derrida — both reject fixed meanings, but Wittgenstein privileges speech, Derrida writing.

A research seminar series (University of Bucharest, 2019-2020) was devoted to "Wittgenstein's Phenomenology: Bridging the Analytic-Continental Gap."

### What Wittgenstein does NOT do

1. **He never addresses what is common across all language games** — beyond family resemblance. He rejects the search for a single essential feature but does not demonstrate there are no shared structural features. He simply does not look. Family resemblance describes the surface of the relationship but does not investigate structural conditions that make language games possible as such.

2. **He does not build a theory of what he uncovers.** He has demonstrated that meaning is use, language is embedded in forms of life, rule-following requires community, private language is impossible — all positive observations. He chooses not to systematise them.

3. **He does not extend "language" beyond human linguistic activity.** "If a lion could talk, we could not understand him" (PI p. 223). But he also asserts "human language is an extension of animal language" and draws parallels between "how cocks call hens and how humans command." Suggestive remarks, not a developed position. He never considers whether music, mathematics, dance, architecture, or biological signalling might be "languages."

4. **He does not ask what makes language games possible.** What structural features must a system have to function as a language game at all? He never asks.

5. **Late work pointers.** *On Certainty* (1950-51): "hinge propositions" that function as the framework within which doubt and inquiry are possible — "the inherited background against which I distinguish between true and false" (OC 94). Comes close to acknowledging structural preconditions for language games, but treats it as epistemic practice, not structural.

## Rorty

### Sources — Wittgenstein, Heidegger, Dewey

**From Wittgenstein.** Three core moves: (1) Language as tool, not mirror — "If we have a Wittgensteinian notion of language as a tool rather than a mirror, we will not look for necessary conditions of the possibility of linguistic representation." Foundational to the entire *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (PMN). (2) Therapeutic philosophy — philosophical problems are illnesses caused when "language goes on holiday." Rorty extends this to a wholesale rejection of the epistemological tradition. (3) Anti-essentialism — "family resemblances" gives Rorty ammunition to reject the idea that "language" or "knowledge" or "truth" have fixed natures.

He radicalises Wittgenstein. Wittgenstein dissolved particular puzzles; Rorty rejects the entire project of epistemology. He also historicises Wittgenstein's insights, giving them a genealogical narrative that Wittgenstein himself would likely have resisted.

**From Heidegger.** (1) The historicity of philosophy — problems are not eternal but historically contingent. Heidegger's Destruktion becomes Rorty's critical dismantling of the metaphysical tradition. (2) Overcoming metaphysics — a single assumption (the dominance relation of "the stronger overcoming the weaker") persists from Plato through to the positivists. (3) Edification — "new, better, more interesting, more fruitful ways of speaking."

What Rorty rejects from Heidegger: the "Thought of Being" as "an unnecessary vestige of Platonism." He keeps the early Heidegger's analytic of Dasein but dismisses the later Heidegger's suggestion that some language-users' understanding of Being can be "more open to Being" than others. Rorty: "it is impossible to rank understandings." He pragmatises Heidegger — keeps historicism and critique, strips out ontological quest, redirects toward Deweyan social practice.

**From Dewey.** Anti-dualism, anti-foundationalism, social practice as ground. But the crucial move: Rorty argues Dewey made a fundamental error retaining "experience." "Dewey should have dropped the term 'experience' rather than redefining it... He should have agreed with Peirce that a great gulf divides sensation and cognition, and decided that cognition was possible only for language users."

Where Dewey said "experience," Rorty says "discourse." This shifts pragmatism from something biological/ecological (organisms coping through experience) to something linguistic (language-users coping through vocabularies). It makes pragmatism more amenable to the analytic tradition but loses "the depth of Dewey's position — the continuity between organism and environment." Colin Koopman: the shift from experience to language in neopragmatism is "a real loss, not merely a translation."

### The linguisticization project

In 1995, Rorty stated: "I linguisticize as many pre-linguistic-turn philosophers as I can, in order to read them as prophets of the utopia in which all metaphysical problems have been dissolved, and religion and science have yielded their place to poetry."

The project was retrospective recruitment — reading historical philosophers as trying to say what Rorty thinks, lacking the vocabulary.

**Which philosophers:** Dewey (experience becomes discourse), James (truth-as-useful becomes vocabulary-as-tool), Heidegger (Being becomes historical vocabularies), Nietzsche (will to power becomes redescription), Hegel (Spirit becomes history of vocabularies), Freud (unconscious becomes alternative vocabulary for self-description), Proust (memory becomes private redescription), Plato (diagnosed as originator of mirror metaphor). Also Derrida ("a funny writer who attempted to circumvent the Western philosophical tradition") and Foucault (useful provider of new descriptions of power).

**Reception — hostile from all directions.** Analytic philosophers: PMN "read as an attack on analytic philosophy rather than benevolent reform, with most reviews being hostile." Searle and Williams "lamented his embrace of continental thinkers and deplored his declared indifference about the philosophical value of 'truth'." He was "called a traitor to the Analytic tradition."

Pragmatist scholars: "Rorty's heterodox reading of Dewey and his generally dismissive attitude toward Peirce meant that he found few friends among the keepers of the pragmatist tradition."

Susan Haack's "Vulgar Pragmatism" accused him of abandoning the attempt to learn about inquiry. "Symptoms of vulgar cynicism."

**Strongest objections:** (1) Interpretive violence — reading historical figures as "prophets" of his position. (2) The experience gap — replacing experience with language loses pre-linguistic bodily interaction. (3) Self-refutation — if all vocabularies are contingent tools, why prefer his? (4) Haack's moral objection — abandoning truth standards is ethically irresponsible.

### Anti-foundationalism

Built on two borrowed arguments: Sellars' attack on the Myth of the Given (no pre-conceptual given can serve as foundation) and Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction ("Two Dogmas"). Rorty's synthesis: "they are really attacking the same distinction." If you accept both, "we have to accept that no knowledge is given to us and everything is a matter of social justification."

Rejects: self-justifying beliefs, "basic" beliefs, a "way the world is" independent of description, any vocabulary capturing reality more accurately, philosophy having a special method for grounding knowledge.

**How it differs from others.** Unlike Derrida (who reveals instabilities within texts — Rorty wants to stop asking foundational questions). Unlike Lyotard (who rejects grand narratives — Rorty criticised postmodernists for "offering critiques of society, but no alternatives" and wants to preserve liberal democratic commitments). Unlike Kuhn (who showed paradigm incommensurability but never intended it as a general position — Rorty generalises).

**What replaces foundations.** Social justification: "We understand knowledge when we understand the social justification of belief." No belief is justified finally — only relative to a contextually determined set of additional statements. Conversation: replaces truth. "Conversation of mankind" borrowed from Oakeshott, restructured using Kuhn's normal/revolutionary distinction. Normal discourse = within agreed rules. Abnormal discourse = disrupts existing vocabulary. Hermeneutics replaces epistemology. Solidarity: "dropping out of our vocabulary such words as 'truth' and 'objectivity' and taking in instead 'justification' and 'solidarity'."

### The relativism question

Rorty calls himself an "ethnocentrist" rather than a relativist. Using your inherited vocabulary means taking up a position within your culture. There is no neutral standpoint. "We have no notion of rational warrant that exceeds or transcends the norms that liberal intellectuals take to define thorough, open-minded, reflective discussion."

Are some vocabularies "better"? Yes, but "better" means capacity for "coping" — not getting closer to reality. The Darwinian analogy: organisms evolve better tools for coping without "getting closer" to the environment's essence. Progress is "a history of increasingly useful metaphors."

Social progress = "thinking of more and diverse people as 'one of us'" — expanding the circle of solidarity. A moral claim, not an epistemological one.

**Critiques:** Putnam: by making justification sociological, Rorty is committed to majority sentiment, but allowing the majority can be wrong introduces a standard independent of context — incoherent. Huw Price (described as strongest): without a distinct norm of truth, Rorty cannot make sense of how someone could improve their commitments by reference to a better-informed community.

### Equal standing and "conversation"

Conversation between vocabularies does NOT require a shared foundation. Does NOT presuppose commensurability. DOES require willingness to listen, redescribe, be changed. DOES require "thorough, open-minded, reflective discussion."

Rorty explicitly opposes "an attempt at commensuration, at a common vocabulary which isolates the common human essence of Achilles and the Buddha." Seeking a common vocabulary is itself foundationalism in his view.

Instead: "passing theories" (Davidson) — ad hoc interpretive hypotheses about what the other person means.

**Does conversation imply something common?** Norman Geras, *Solidarity in the Conversation of Humankind* (1995): Rorty's liberal commitments (cruelty is the worst thing we do) presuppose something shared — the capacity to suffer and recognise suffering — that his philosophy cannot ground. Bjorn Ramberg: if devoted to community of values, Rorty must also be devoted to a distinction of truth. The implicit presupposition: conversation requires participants who can recognise each other as language-users, attempt redescription, be persuaded — all presupposing shared capacities.

Rorty's "equal standing" is a negative claim (no vocabulary is privileged). The seed's "equal standing in potential" is a positive structural claim (all languages work through relation). Rorty would resist the positive formulation as smuggling in a meta-vocabulary.

### Professional price

Left Princeton in 1982, "disillusioned with professional philosophy." Moved to Professor of Humanities at Virginia — a significant demotion in analytic eyes. Later to Stanford's Comparative Literature department (1998). "Despite Rorty's international success, his philosophy was regarded as a betrayal by most of his colleagues."

Continental reception was warmer. Habermas, Vattimo, Derrida, Wellmer, Joas, Mouffe, Critchley, Sandbothe "influenced in different ways by Rorty's thinking." Habermas engaged extensively, insisting on "objective, context-independent truth" while respecting Rorty's project.

### Rorty and the seed

**Closest approaches:** Language at the centre. No vocabulary captures reality (aligns with seed's refusal to rank by content). Vocabularies as adaptive tools (maps onto "web of growing complexity"). Crossing traditions (inter-relational in practice). Conversation as highest practice.

**Clearest divergences:** The structural claim — Rorty would be suspicious of any universal claim about how all vocabularies work. The common layer — Rorty explicitly opposes "commensuration" and "common human essence." The status of the claim — Rorty has no room for "how things actually work." Experience — the seed retains "experience" (closer to Dewey than Rorty).

**The irony:** Rorty's practice of conversation presupposes exactly the kind of common structural layer the seed describes. He cannot explain why conversation is possible across vocabularies without implicitly relying on something shared. Geras, Ramberg, and Putnam all press on this. The seed makes explicit what Rorty leaves implicit — and what his framework forbids him from making explicit.

## Merleau-Ponty

### Embodied perception — the core

**The critique.** Empiricism treats perception as passive reception of sense-data. Intellectualism (including Kant) treats it as mental construction. Both share "an unquestioned belief in the world" as ready-made objective reality. "Empiricism cannot see that we need to know what we are looking for... and intellectualism fails to see that we need to be ignorant of what we are looking for." They are "two sides of a coin."

**The third way.** Perception is neither passive reception nor intellectual construction but active bodily engagement. "The world is not what I think, but what I live." Sensation and intellect are both abstractions from perception, not its building blocks.

**Pre-reflective experience.** The "tacit cogito" — a silent, pre-reflective, inarticulate grasp on the world and ourselves. Through perception, "the body is always called upon to engage, to choose, to focus the world before any verbal reflection comes into play." Through habit, a person "establishes appropriate relations with the world... without needing any prior reasoning, but rather in a spontaneous or immediate way."

**Motor intentionality.** The body orients toward action without explicit mental commands. The fundamental locus of intentionality is not "I think" but "I can." A skilled driver or pianist knows the world "not through maps or syllogisms, but through their limbs."

**The intentional arc.** Creates inter-sensory unity between body and mind, motricity and intelligence. Integrates past experience, present situation, and projected action.

**Body schema.** A pre-conscious system of bodily movements and spatial equivalences — the body's sense of its ability to act.

**The Schneider case.** A brain-injured soldier who could perform habitual movements but could not project intentions in fictive scenarios. His intentional arc "goes limp" — demonstrating how pre-reflective organisation underlies all higher activity.

**Phantom limb.** The body schema persists beyond the physical body — the body-subject's relation to its world cannot be reduced to current physical configuration.

"The body is our general medium for having a world."

### The body-subject

The subject is not a mind in a body. "The intrinsic intentionality of the body makes the Cartesian separation untenable: the body is already conscious, and consciousness is always corporeal." The genuine cogito is a cogito "in action."

**Relation to Kant.** Kant's transcendental subject operates through categories from outside embodied life. Merleau-Ponty preserves the transcendental insight — the subject is a condition of experience — but relocates the transcendental function from intellectual categories to the body itself. The body is "a constitutive or transcendental principle, precisely because it is involved in the very possibility of experience." The subject is "not a transcendental subject, but a subject that emerges from nature."

**Relation to Husserl.** Husserl distinguished act intentionality (judgments, decisions) from operative intentionality (pre-predicative unity of world and life). But for Husserl, "pure consciousness and the transcendental ego stand separated from reality by an 'abyss.'" For Merleau-Ponty, "intentional awareness primitively belongs to the body... in a way that it does not for Husserl."

Scholars identify a progression: Kant's "transcendentalism of faculties" → Husserl's "transcendentalism of pure consciousness" → Merleau-Ponty's "transcendentalism of the flesh."

### Expression and meaning

**Speaking speech and spoken speech.** *Parole parlante* (speaking speech) — truly expressive, giving birth to new meaning. *Parole parlée* (spoken speech) — relies on already-constituted, sedimented meaning. Not two kinds of speech but two dimensions present in all speech. Examples of speaking speech: "the child uttering her first word, the lover discovering his feeling by articulating it."

**Meaning arises in expression.** Meaning is not pre-formed thought translated into words. Expression is constitutive. "Bodily gestures can convey meaning, and an already available structure of gesture is part of our embodied existence."

**Engagement with Saussure.** Encountered Saussure's structural linguistics in the late 1940s. What he took: the diacritical principle — "signs function diacritically, through their lateral relations and differentiations." "Taken singly, signs do not signify anything."

How he transformed Saussure: rejected the "rigid distinction" between sign and signification, challenged the "stark contrast between diachronic and synchronic" — la langue is not "a Gestalt of the moment, but a Gestalt in movement," a "moving equilibrium." Re-emphasised speech (*parole*) as constitutive, where Saussure privileged the system.

**Bridge between phenomenology and structuralism.** He adopts Saussure's framework (differential meaning, system of signs) but reintegrates the speaking subject, lived expression, and creative meaning-constitution. Remained "critical of the very idea of an objective science of language that would deny or ignore the spontaneity and instability of linguistic form."

### The Royaumont Colloquium (1958)

April 1958 at Royaumont Abbey outside Paris. British delegation: Austin, Ryle, Strawson, Urmson, Ayer, Quine. Continental: Merleau-Ponty, van Breda, Berger, Wahl (presiding).

Widely described as "a dialogue de sourds" — but recent scholarship (Overgaard, "Royaumont Revisited," 2010) challenges this. "When read carefully, the papers and discussions do not lend support to the view that communication was impossible."

Merleau-Ponty's response to Ryle: "I have also had the impression, while listening to Mr. Ryle, that what he was saying was not so strange to us... I do not see much that separates us... In the last part of his exposition, Mr. Ryle gave us some glimpses of his own research which, for me, is not absolutely a surprise."

Ryle's reply was notably cooler — "one of the most notorious examples of the communication failure at the conference."

Note: The remark was addressed to Ryle, not Wittgenstein directly, though Ryle's work was deeply conversant with the later Wittgenstein.

### Extension beyond the human

**The Structure of Behavior (1942).** Three orders: physical, vital, human. Each higher order reorganises rather than replaces the lower. The vital order already has organised, surpassing activity. The framework does not require being human.

**Nature Lectures (College de France, 1956-60).** Three courses on the concept of Nature, animality, and the human body. Extended discussions of Uexkull's *Umwelt* — holistic dialectic between perceiving organism and perceived environment. Analysis of Lorenz demonstrated "even what we call instinct is not an innate fixed response... but rather part of a meaningful set of symbolic relations."

**Inter-animality.** Coined this term. Animals and human beings "share a corporeity that constitutes their openness to a world-milieu." He posited "biological continuism, where consciousness has evolved through shared evolutionary paths among species" — "an overcoming that does not abolish the kinship."

**Limits.** His primary descriptions remain centered on human experience. He "suggests that other animals can have their own 'culture and history'" but "doesn't specify what he means by those words." Never extended to non-biological systems. Phenomenology is "an avowedly anthropocentric enterprise." Posthumanist extensions are later scholars' work.

### Historicity

Arrived at embodied perception, sensorimotor coupling, and the body's role in structuring experience through phenomenological method alone — decades before neuroscientific confirmation.

**Mirror neurons.** Discovery provided "striking empirical support." Gallese, Keysers, and Rizzolatti (2004): "the description by Merleau-Ponty of what it means to understand an action... expresses nicely the direct experiential understanding mediated by the mirror mechanism."

**Enactivism.** Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (1991) "explicitly sought to promote and develop the phenomenological approach in cognition studies, especially the formulations found in Maurice Merleau-Ponty's philosophy." The enactive approach was "depicted as a continuation of a research program founded by Merleau-Ponty."

### The Visible and the Invisible — late work

Unfinished at his sudden death in May 1961. Move from phenomenology toward "indirect ontology" — the ontology of "the flesh of the world."

**Flesh** is not biological tissue. It is "the mysterious tissue or matrix that underlies and gives rise to both the perceiver and the perceived as interdependent aspects of its spontaneous activity." Should be understood "as an element, as the concrete emblem of a general manner of being" — not substance. It is "neither subject nor object" — present in both parts of the relation. David Abram "identifies this elemental matrix with the interdependent web of earthly life."

**Reversibility and the chiasm.** The hand that touches can be felt as touched, "though never both at the same time." This reversibility is "the essence of flesh." Subject and object cannot be unequivocally discerned. The chiasm connects visible with invisible, perceptual with mental phenomena.

**Relation to the relational.** "Self, other and world are inherently relational, interdependent at the level of ontology." "Apparent binaries of the reversible relation are not absolute disjunctive binaries... but rather interdependent poles within the relation, distinguishable in reflection but inseparable in reality."

"That primordial being which is not yet the subject-being nor the object-being and which in every respect baffles reflection. From this primordial being to us, there is no derivation, nor any break..."

**Self-critique.** In this late work, Merleau-Ponty argued that *Phenomenology of Perception* "has still not completely shed the dualism to which it was opposed." The tacit cogito was abandoned as depending on "the illusion of non-linguistic signification."

### What Merleau-Ponty does NOT do

1. **Does not systematically extend beyond human.** Despite Nature lectures and inter-animality, no systematic account of perception and embodiment covering non-human cases.

2. **Does not address what is common across forms of embodied engagement.** Describes human embodiment in detail but does not abstract to ask: what is the common structure that any embodied engagement must share? Does not isolate minimal conditions for a body-subject relation.

3. **Does not extend to non-biological systems.** Never considers whether a non-biological system could be a body-subject, have motor intentionality, form habits.

4. **Stops at description.** Does not build formal structures. Does not specify necessary and sufficient conditions. Does not identify what is shared across different types of body-subject relations.

## The Axis — Connections

### The three-way crossing

The three-way configuration (Wittgenstein-Rorty-Merleau-Ponty) is not an established scholarly grouping. The closest is Charles Taylor treating Heidegger-Wittgenstein-Merleau-Ponty in "Overcoming Epistemology" (1987). Our axis with Rorty instead of Heidegger appears to be novel territory.

Scholarly literature tends to work in pairs: Wittgenstein-Merleau-Ponty (substantial body of work including Romdenh-Romluc's Routledge volume), Rorty-Wittgenstein (well documented), Rorty-Dewey-Heidegger (Rorty's own framing).

### Direct connections

**Rorty on Wittgenstein.** The strongest connection. Rorty's "Intellectual Autobiography" names Wittgenstein (alongside Sellars, Davidson, Brandom) as key. In PMN, Wittgenstein is central. "Epistemological behaviorism" — "an attitude common to Dewey and Wittgenstein." Wittgenstein classified as "edifying" rather than "systematic" philosopher.

**Rorty on Merleau-Ponty.** The weakest connection. No substantial direct engagement found. PMN "carries out a critique of the metaphors of 'mirroring' and 'reflection' in western philosophy, but gives no indication that he is aware of the similar critique Merleau-Ponty mounted." They were working on the same problem (representationalism) apparently without significant mutual engagement.

Rorty's insistence that "all awareness is a linguistic affair" and humans are "nothing more than sentential attitudes" leaves no theoretical space for the pre-reflective, embodied, non-linguistic dimension central to Merleau-Ponty.

**Merleau-Ponty on Wittgenstein.** The Royaumont statement (1958). Merleau-Ponty died in 1961, before the later Wittgenstein's full impact on the continent. The convergence was largely worked out posthumously by scholars.

### Common ground across the three

**Anti-representationalism.** All three reject the idea that mind or language mirrors reality. Wittgenstein replaces picturing with language games. Rorty attacks "the Mirror of Nature." Merleau-Ponty replaces the spectator model with embodied engagement.

**Rejection of the Cartesian picture.** Both Wittgenstein and Merleau-Ponty "reject a Cartesian picture of the mind and sought to offer an alternative that does justice to the role played by bodily action, language, and our membership within a community that shares a way of life" (Romdenh-Romluc). Rorty's entire PMN project dismantles the Cartesian-Lockean-Kantian tradition.

**Meaning as practice/use/engagement.** Wittgenstein: use in language games. Merleau-Ponty: embodied, pre-reflective engagement. Rorty: conversation, pragmatic consequences of vocabularies. All locate meaning in practice rather than correspondence.

**Rejection of the Given.** All three reject a privileged foundational layer. Wittgenstein dissolves private language. Rorty draws on Sellars's Myth of the Given. Merleau-Ponty rejects both sense-data and categories as foundational.

**The social/relational nature of meaning.** Wittgenstein: private language is impossible, meaning requires shared form of life. Merleau-Ponty: inter-subjectivity and inter-corporeality are constitutive. Rorty: knowledge and justification are social-linguistic practices.

**Charles Taylor's identification (1987).** In "Overcoming Epistemology," Taylor identifies a continuity through Heidegger, Wittgenstein, and Merleau-Ponty: "They all start from the intuition that the central phenomenon of experience is not made intelligible on the epistemological construal." He develops "engaged agency" as their common alternative to the Cartesian spectator model.

### Where they diverge from each other

**Philosophical stance.** Wittgenstein: therapeutic (dissolve problems). Rorty: conversational (keep talking). Merleau-Ponty: descriptive (describe experience).

**The body.** The sharpest divergence. Merleau-Ponty: the body at the centre of everything. Wittgenstein: acknowledges the body but does not systematically thematize it. Rorty: almost no place for the body at all. His "all awareness is a linguistic affair" was subject to sustained critique from Richard Shusterman.

**Experience.** Merleau-Ponty: pre-reflective lived experience is philosophically fundamental. Wittgenstein: ambivalent — explores experience but suspicious of philosophical claims about it. Rorty: explicitly wants to jettison "experience" as the last refuge of the Myth of the Given.

**What language can do.** Poppe's dissertation (Notre Dame, 1995): Merleau-Ponty holds that pre-linguistic meaning in embodied experience transforms into linguistic meaning, and the life-world provides "an external source of and constraint on linguistic meaning." Wittgenstein "rejects accounts that attempt to connect linguistic meaning with a reality outside of language." Rorty goes further — meaning is entirely internal to vocabularies.

### Key scholars who connect them

**Richard Bernstein** — *The Pragmatic Turn* (2010): "many of the themes developed by the pragmatic thinkers were also central to the work of major twentieth century philosophers like Wittgenstein and Heidegger, but the so-called analytic-continental split obscures this underlying continuity." Earlier: *The Restructuring of Social and Political Theory* (1976) examines three strands of revolt against positivism: from Wittgenstein, from phenomenology, and from critical theory.

**Hubert Dreyfus** — "Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, and Wittgenstein suggest that this inherited background of practices cannot be spelled out in a theory because (1) the background is so pervasive that we cannot make it an object of analysis, and (2) the practices involve skill."

**Richard Shusterman** — Critique of Rorty from Dewey and Merleau-Ponty. "Rejects Rorty's textualism: the idea that the world we experience is thoroughly linguistic." Argues for "the somatic and non-propositional dimension of experience." Effectively occupies the gap between Rorty and Merleau-Ponty.

**John McDowell** — *Mind and World* (1994): Wittgensteinian methodology engaging with Merleau-Ponty themes. "Second nature" connects forms-of-life thinking with embodied habituation.

**Michele F. Epstein** — "The Common Ground of Merleau-Ponty's and Wittgenstein's Philosophy of Man" (*Journal of the History of Philosophy*): they independently proposed "a third possible approach to the mind-body problem."

**Komarine Romdenh-Romluc** — *Wittgenstein and Merleau-Ponty* (Routledge, 2017): first collection dedicated to comparing these two.

### What the three together make available

**The full circuit.** Body (Merleau-Ponty) gives rise to practice and form of life (Wittgenstein), which becomes ongoing conversation of culture (Rorty). No single thinker describes the full path.

**The body gap in Rorty.** Shusterman's critique makes visible: Rorty's insistence that "all awareness is a linguistic affair" creates a gap where the body should be. Merleau-Ponty fills exactly that gap. But the combination raises a question none of them individually asks: what is the relationship between pre-linguistic embodied ground and entirely linguistified conversation? Wittgenstein sits at the hinge — "forms of life" is ambiguous between bodily/practical and linguistic readings.

**Anti-representationalism at every level.** Wittgenstein in the logical-linguistic register, Merleau-Ponty in the perceptual register, Rorty in the cultural-conversational register. Together they close off representationalism at body, language, and culture.

**The emergent question.** If meaning arises in the body, is practiced in shared forms of life, and circulates in ongoing conversation — then there is no level at which meaning is fixed or representational. But also no level at which it is merely arbitrary, because at every level it is constrained: by the body, the practice, the conversation. The three together suggest meaning as relationally constrained at every level without being foundationally grounded at any level. No single one of them quite says this.
