# Linguistic Nativism — Research Dossier

Working document. Assembled to feed downstream positioning work. It documents nativism **on its own account** — the field's positions stated as their holders state them, the internal disagreements reported from each side, the criticisms in their critics' voice and the replies in the nativists' own. No SPLectrum reading and no conclusions: routing notes are fenced at the end (§9), and they are notes about *where the material might go*, not verdicts on it. Sourced July 2026.

**Scope note.** Nativism is a theory of **human utterance-language** — speech, words, syntax; the externalised shared code of a community. That is the object throughout this document. Where the field generalises from that object to claims about mind or thought in general, the generalisation is documented as *their* move (§5, §6), not adopted as the frame.

**Standing.** This is a live, defended research program, founded by and largely identified with a figure who has an honourable page on-site (`persons/c/chomsky.md` — poverty of the stimulus presented straight, the usage-based and Everett challenges recorded as contested). Nothing here overturns that page. The field's internal disputes are sharp and are reported as sharp; that is documentation, not a verdict.

## 1. The claim, stated precisely

The vernacular version ("language is innate") is not what anyone defends, and nativists are explicit that it isn't. Nobody claims a language is present at birth, or that grammatical knowledge is propositional knowledge the infant possesses.

The careful formulation (SEP's): a developed linguistic trait is innate **iff** it "cannot be explained by appeal to the developing organism's sensitivity to the language learner's environment, but must instead be explained with reference to the influence of a **language-specific learning system**."

Two things carry the weight:

- **Domain-specificity is the whole dispute.** Everyone in the debate — including Tomasello, including the connectionists — grants that *something* innate is doing work; a rock doesn't learn Dutch. The nativist claim is that what does the work is **dedicated to language**, not general-purpose. This is the exact line, and it is worth keeping in view because arguments that establish "some innate structure is needed" do **not** establish nativism. Nativists themselves note this: a poverty-of-the-stimulus argument gets you *some* innate factor; a further argument is needed to make it language-specific.
- **What's innate is a constraint, not a content.** Modern nativists posit "computational constraints on the possible development of a language-specific cognitive system" — biases, a structured hypothesis space, an initial state — rather than rules the child knows. The endowment shapes *what grammars are reachable*, and experience selects among them.

**The initial state.** The organising idea across all versions: the faculty of language has an initial state (UG), common to the species, which experience moves to a steady state (the particular grammar of the speaker's community). UG is the initial state, not the final one.

## 2. Poverty of the stimulus — the argument on its own terms

The central argument, and the one everything else is fitted to.

**Structure** (SEP's three-premise schema):

1. Children acquire linguistic knowledge **T**.
2. Children encounter only limited primary linguistic data **PLD**.
3. Domain-general learning mechanisms **LM** cannot derive T from PLD alone.
4. **∴** Innate factors **F**, specific to language, explain the acquisition.

Supporting premises as the tradition states them: the child gets only *positive* evidence (no systematic correction, no ungrammaticality lessons); the input is degenerate in scope and quality (errors, false starts, fragments); different children get different data yet converge on the same grammar; and no other organism in the same environment acquires it.

**The evidential burden is threefold and non-trivial** — the argument requires showing (a) what adults actually know, (b) what children actually encounter, and (c) why general learning can't bridge the two. Nativists note the corresponding burden on the other side: rejecting the argument means answering it *for every phenomenon*, not one.

**The canonical cases** (each a place where the child's knowledge appears not to be in the input):

- **Structure-dependence** — the flagship. Yes-no question formation moves the *structurally* right auxiliary, never the linearly-first one; the input is consistent with both the structural and the linear rule, and children reportedly never try the linear one.
- **Binding, Principle C** — children know "he" can't refer to the Ninja Turtle in *He ate pizza while the Ninja Turtle was dancing*. Impossible interpretations are precisely what speech to children never marks.
- **Anaphoric "one"** — 18-month-olds take "one" to mean *red ball*, not *ball*, though the input is systematically ambiguous between the two.
- **Island effects** — extraction constraints on wh-questions; violations are absent from the input, but so are the grammatical long-distance cases that would teach the contrast.
- **Phonology** — stress systems, English plural allomorphy: speakers generalise over *natural classes* not present as such in what they heard.
- **Semantics** — the "dog"-category problem; attitude verbs (*think*, *wonder*) whose meanings have no observable correlate in the situation of use.

**Supporting empirical work cited by nativists:** Bergelson & Idsardi (2009) — eight-month-olds show preferences tracking natural-language stress regularities in the absence of relevant experience.

## 3. The internal history — what has been claimed to be innate, and how it shrank

The most important fact about nativism for documentation purposes: **it is not one position, and its own answer to "what's innate" has changed radically across sixty years, in one direction — downward.**

| Phase | What is innate | Acquisition is… |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformational Grammar** (Standard Theory, *Aspects*, 1965) | Extensive innate rule schemas + an evaluation metric for ranking candidate grammars | The child searching a space of grammars and evaluating |
| **Principles & Parameters** (1981–) | Universal principles (no learning needed) + a finite set of binary parameters | Not rule-learning at all — *setting switches*. Radically simplifies acquisition |
| **Minimalist Program** (1993–) | As close to nothing as possible; in the limit, **Merge** | Merge + externalisation; the rest offloaded to interfaces and general principles |

**The P&P move and its price.** Principles & Parameters solved the acquisition problem elegantly — the child doesn't induce a grammar, it fixes a small number of switches from exposure. But it did so by *shifting the burden to evolution*: everything not learned must be endowed, so the richer the principles, the heavier the phylogenetic debt. The field states this openly.

**Minimalism as a response.** The Minimalist Program strips the language-specific component toward a minimum: syntax reduces to **Merge**, a single recursive operation combining two syntactic objects into an unordered hierarchical set, the output re-enterable as input. Everything possible is derived instead from (i) the interfaces — the conceptual-intentional system (meaning) and the sensory-motor system (sound) — and (iii) general principles of computational efficiency. The **Strong Minimalist Thesis**: language is an *optimal* solution to the problem of linking meaning and sound. Older systems (semantics, phonology-motor) are held to be non-linguistic, which reduces what has to be language-specific in the first place.

**Status:** P&P is no longer the basis of Chomskyan theory; Minimalism is current mainstream generativism. Note that P&P remains widely used in descriptive/comparative syntactic work — the shift is in the theoretical core, not universally in practice.

**The dissenting nativist architecture — Jackendoff's Parallel Architecture.** Not syntax-first: multiple independent generative systems (syntax, semantics, morphology, phonology), each with its own combinatorial machinery, linked by interfaces. Distributes innateness across systems rather than concentrating it in one operation; requires a *longer* evolutionary history rather than a single recent event. A nativist alternative to Minimalism from inside the broad program.

## 4. The atoms / the lexicon — where the field itself reports a hole

Merge combines objects; it doesn't supply them. What the objects are is the most contested and least settled part of the program, and nativists' answers diverge sharply.

**The obvious environmental part.** Which words a child ends up with is plainly from the environment — Leeds gives you *cat*, Ljubljana *mačka*. No one disputes this.

**The mainstream nativist account of word-learning** is not that words are innate but that *constraints on learning them* are: whole-object bias, taxonomic bias, mutual exclusivity, and **syntactic bootstrapping** (using the grammatical frame a word appears in to infer its meaning). Innate heuristics guiding acquisition, not innate vocabulary.

**Chomsky's own position is much stronger, and is an outlier even among nativists.** He argues from "conceptual necessity" that lexical concepts have properties too complex to have been extracted from experience — so they "basically have to be there and then they get kind of triggered and you find out what sounds are associated with them." The sound is learned; the concept is triggered. He extends this explicitly to culturally-specific artefact words: there is "good reason to suppose that the argument is at least in substantial measure correct even for such words as *carburetor* and *bureaucrat*, which pose the familiar problem of poverty of stimulus." This is poverty-of-the-stimulus applied to the lexicon.

**Fodor's radical concept nativism** is the position taken to the limit: virtually all lexical concepts are innate and unstructured (word meanings don't decompose), so *car* is innate. Fodor accepted the consequence explicitly. The field's own summary: "just about everyone thinks Fodor has gone too far" — this is the standard characterisation, from broadly sympathetic quarters. **His argument for it is in §4b.1** (the circularity of hypothesis-testing), and it is inseparable from his account of non-linguistic thought — the two are one position.

**The reception.** Putnam "could hardly conceal his astonishment"; Dennett's reply — that on this view Aristotle had the concept of an airplane in his brain — is the standard reductio. Both are on-site figures.

**And the phylogenetic side reports the hole directly.** Berwick & Chomsky, *Why Only Us*: "the origin of human specific concepts and 'the atoms of computation' that Merge uses **remains for us a mystery**." Reviewers press exactly here (§5).

**Note the tension inside the program, documented rather than adjudicated:** Minimalism's whole motive is to shrink the language-specific endowment for evolvability's sake; concept nativism enlarges what must be endowed enormously. The two strands pull opposite ways, and Chomsky holds both.

## 4b. Concepts outside spoken language

Nativism is a theory of utterance-language, but every version needs an account of the concepts that never get uttered — what prelinguistic infants and non-human animals have, what thought without words is made of, and what the words attach *to*. **There are three incompatible answers, and the disagreement is as sharp as anything in §5.** Note that all three grant that non-linguistic thought exists; they differ on what it is and what language does to it.

### 4b.1 Fodor — Mentalese: the concepts are a language already

The **Language of Thought Hypothesis**. Thinking occurs in an inner symbolic system — *Mentalese* — that is "innate and independent, not derived from or reliant on external linguistic experience," and whose "ontological status is prior to the ontological status of any natural language." It has compositional semantics: primitive symbols combining into mental sentences, with the meaning of a complex representation fixed by its constituents and their structure. A Mentalese word ≈ a concept: a thinker "has the concept *cat* only if she has in her repertoire a Mentalese word that denotes cats."

**The arguments are explicitly from non-linguistic thought** — this is LOT's home ground, not a patch:
- **Prelinguistic infants and animals.** If thought ran in natural language, sophisticated problem-solving in creatures without one would be inexplicable. Rats' cognitive maps, perceptual "unconscious inference" — mental representation with no natural language anywhere.
- **Productivity.** Infinitely many possible thoughts from finite resources; explained by iterative combination of finite primitives.
- **Systematicity of thought.** Anyone who can think *John loves Mary* can think *Mary loves John* — an interdependence that suggests compositional structure in thought itself, independent of language learning.
- **Systematicity of thinking.** Inference patterns generalise uniformly across symbols — one mechanical operation applying regardless of content.

**What natural language is, on this view:** an **encoding/externalisation of Mentalese, not the vehicle of thought.** Word-learning is hypothesis formation and testing about which Mentalese concept an English word denotes — which requires *already having* the concept. The Mentalese *cat* must pre-exist for the child to hypothesise that "cat" denotes cats.

**Why this forces concept nativism** (the §4 position, now with its argument): Fodor holds hypothesis-testing is the only viable learning mechanism, and that it's **circular** — testing a hypothesis about property P requires already possessing the concept P. So concept *learning* is impossible; concepts are **unlearned acquisitions**, innate resources that experience activates but does not create. He is careful that this doesn't deny experience is required — only that concepts are learned by rational hypothesis-testing. And since Mentalese words *are* concepts, an innate conceptual stock means the mental language is innate: children don't acquire Mentalese, they already have it and use it to learn the mappings.

Fodor's relation to Chomsky, in his own framing: **UG is merely the surface manifestation of Mentalese.**

### 4b.2 Chomsky — the conceptual-intentional system, and the atoms as mystery

Chomsky's architecture *has* a place for non-linguistic concepts: the **conceptual-intentional (C-I) system**, one of the two interfaces Merge's output is legible to (the other being sensory-motor). Syntax mediates between thought (C-I) and sound (S-M); on the Minimalist picture the C-I system is **not itself linguistic** — it's one of the older, independent systems, which is exactly what lets Minimalism shrink the language-specific component (§3).

But the C-I system's *contents* are where §4's mystery sits. He holds lexical concepts are triggered rather than learned (*carburetor*), which places him near Fodor — yet unlike Fodor he does not offer a worked theory of what the atoms are or where they came from, and *Why Only Us* concedes their origin "remains for us a mystery." So the honest statement of his position on concepts outside spoken language: **there is a system, it is prior to and independent of language, it is not built by experience — and its inventory is unexplained.**

### 4b.3 Spelke & Carey — core knowledge: the concepts are not a language, and language combines them

The main developmental alternative, and a **nativist** one — but nativist about *cognition*, not about language.

**The claim** (Spelke & Kinzler): human cognition starts from a small set of evolutionarily old **core systems** — **objects** (solidity, continuity), **agents/actions** (goal-directedness), **number**, **space** (geometric navigation), and **social partners**. Their properties: domain-specific, task-specific, encapsulated, early-emerging in preverbal infants, and **shared with other animals** (primates and other mammals).

**What language does, on this account — and this is the load-bearing difference:** language **"combines core systems into uniquely human concepts."** The core systems are ancient, separate, and encapsulated; natural language is the mechanism that *integrates* them across their boundaries, yielding abstract, culturally-transmissible thought no other species reaches. "Human conceptual sophistication emerges not from entirely novel cognitive systems, but from linguistic integration of evolutionarily ancient foundations."

This is a direct inversion of Fodor and of Chomsky's late position. For Fodor, language externalises a conceptual system already complete and compositional. For Spelke, the prelinguistic conceptual stock is **real but fragmented**, and language is what does the combining — so language is *constitutive* of the uniquely human concepts, not an encoding of them.

Note the family resemblance to Chomsky's *architecture* — Carey and Spelke's Core Cognition programme was inspired by Chomsky's modularity/organology (independent domain-specific systems) — with the opposite conclusion about what language is *for*.

**Cited as bearing on the debate:** the missing-link question between core knowledge and language is live (review of Spelke's *What Babies Know*, 2022/2023); prelinguistic cognition research invokes many of the same notions linguistic theory does — objects, animate beings, causation, events, states, and thematic roles like agent, goal, source.

### 4b.4 The critics of the whole framing

Those who deny thought is language-like at all, and so reject the question's premise:

- **Imagistic / non-propositional:** the British empiricists' *ideas* combining associatively; contemporary image-like representations (**Prinz**, **Barsalou** — Barsalou already in the field map at Joint 2) and mental maps (**Armstrong**). LOT's standard reply: propositional attitudes are often logically complex, which imagistic theories struggle with. The entry records these as live alternatives, not defeated ones.
- **Connectionism:** neural networks model cognition without discrete Mentalese symbols. **Fodor & Pylyshyn** argued only compositional symbol systems explain productivity and systematicity; connectionists reply that one can reject Turing-style models while still having representations with compositionally relevant structure — distributed, non-concatenative.
- **Eliminativism (P.S. Churchland):** mature cognitive science may need no representational mental states at all — rejecting LOT's first premise outright.
- **Internal problems reported in the literature:** the regress worry (understanding Mentalese threatens regress if understanding requires representing meanings — proponents reply that thinkers don't *interpret* Mentalese, their activity simply coheres with its meanings); and the individuation problem — no consensus on how Mentalese expressions are individuated (neural properties fail via multiple realizability; functional vs semantic individuation unsettled), which leaves LOT "highly schematic."

## 5. How the endowment got there — the phylogenetic question, and the split it produces

The field's own vice, stated from inside the program: **UG must be rich enough to overcome poverty of the stimulus, but the condition of evolvability demands that UG be very restricted.** Every increment of innate richness is structure evolution must have built. This is an acknowledged design constraint, not an outsider's objection — and it is what the two camps below answer differently.

### 5.1 Chomsky & Berwick — saltation

*Why Only Us* (2016). The faculty is minimal, so what must have evolved is minimal: **Merge**, arriving via "a chance discontinuity" — a minor genetic rewiring in a small population, between anatomically modern humans (~200kya) and the African diaspora (~60kya), with ~80kya as focal. Explicitly **saltationist**, and explicitly anti-gradualist: "no room in this picture for any precursors to language — say a language-like system with only short sentences."

**Why no selection for communication:** because on this account language did not evolve *for* communication. It is "primarily an instrument of thought, only secondarily a means of communication"; externalisation is a secondary, ancillary problem. Merge could be selected internally — "by means of better planning, inference, and the like." (Chomsky's long-standing anti-adaptationism, shared with Gould: language as by-product rather than adaptation.)

**Criticisms of this account, in the critics' voice** (Studdert-Kennedy & Terrace, *Journal of Language Evolution*):
- It neglects how *words* evolved, though it grants words preceded grammar.
- Cutting syntax off from behaviour and speech undermines selection's role, since "changes in behavior generate new selection forces which modify the structures involved."
- "Better planning, inference, and the like" is too vague to be a selection story — they argue language solved *displaced reference* (Bickerton).
- The account of vocalisation leans on "Darwin's Caruso theory" (sexual selection through song), which they hold lacks evidence and ignores speech's neuroanatomical demands.
- The atoms are conceded as a mystery, yet the framework can't proceed without them.
- Formal critique: the single-mutation step doesn't follow from Merge's atomicity — external-Merge and internal-Merge could be separate events, needing ≥2 mutations ("the no half-Merge fallacy").

### 5.2 Pinker & Bloom — gradual adaptation

"Natural Language and Natural Selection" (1990, *BBS*). **Still nativist** — grammar is innate, specialised, domain-specific — but the endowment evolved the ordinary way: incrementally, by neo-Darwinian natural selection, **for communication**. Grammar is "a complex mechanism tailored to the transmission of propositional structures through a serial interface" — exactly the kind of complex adaptive design for which selection is the only known explanation. They reject both the language-is-incompatible-with-Darwin framing and the exaptation/by-product story. Their target was explicitly Chomsky's and Gould's view that UG confers no advantage and so can't be selected for.

### 5.3 The negotiated middle and the fight over it

**Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch (2002, *Science*)** — "The Faculty of Language: What Is It, Who Has It, and How Did It Evolve?" splits the object:
- **FLB** (broad) — everything language recruits; largely shared with other animals, evolutionarily older.
- **FLN** (narrow) — the uniquely human residue; hypothesised to be **recursion alone**, recently evolved, possibly not an adaptation for communication.

**Pinker & Jackendoff (2005, *Cognition*)** attack the recursion-only hypothesis: recursion isn't unique to language; phonology, morphology, and the human conceptual system are also special and are overlooked by FLN-as-recursion. **Fitch, Hauser & Chomsky (2005)** reply that the critics conflate FLB and FLN. This exchange is the live internal fault line of nativism and should be cited as *nativists disagreeing with nativists*, not as nativism-vs-critics.

## 6. Thought vs communication — the late position

Documented separately because it is a distinct claim, easily conflated with the evolution story.

Chomsky's mature view: language is **an instrument of thought**; communication is a secondary, derivative use. Minimalism's architecture reflects this — syntax links to the conceptual-intentional system, and externalisation to the sensory-motor system is an ancillary process, "an afterthought" in the design. Evidence adduced: the ubiquity of structures inefficient for communication but natural for computation; the alleged suboptimality of language *as* a communication system.

This inverts the direction most of the field assumes (that language is for, and shaped by, communication) and is one of the sharpest disagreements between Chomsky and the adaptationists — Pinker & Bloom's whole case depends on communication being the selective pressure.

## 7. The criticisms, in their critics' voice

Reported as positions, with nativist replies where the sources give them.

**Against premise 2 — the stimulus isn't that poor.**
- **Pullum & Scholz** — question whether the input is impoverished as claimed; the empirical basis of specific POS arguments has been overstated.
- **Corpus evidence** — systematic searches of actual child-directed speech reportedly reveal more of the relevant patterns than nativists allow.
- **Reali & Christiansen** — the structure-dependence case specifically: relevant distributional information is present.
- *Nativist reply:* Berwick, Chomsky & Piattelli-Palmarini, "Poverty of the stimulus stands: critics reconsidered."

**Against premise 3 — general learning can do it.**
- **Statistical learning** — children extract far more from frequency and distribution than the argument assumes.
- **Connectionist / neural network results** — hierarchical structure learned "without an explicit constraint," which if right removes the need for a structure-dependence endowment.
- **Bayesian inference, construction grammar, analogy** — domain-general mechanisms proposed as sufficient.
- **Tomasello / usage-based** (on-site) — the sharpest *domain-specificity* denial: substantial innate structure is granted, but it's intention-reading, joint attention, pattern-finding, coordination — **not language-dedicated**. This attacks precisely the line §1 identifies as the whole dispute.

**Against premise 1 — the target is mis-described.** Whether the knowledge attributed to adults is real, or whether apparently sophisticated competence reflects simpler learned patterns.

**Evolutionary.** Positing rich innate structure "incurs debts to evolutionary theories" (§5). Noted as the driver of Minimalism's retreat, i.e. the field's own response.

**Circularity** (Lasnik, quoted in SEP): identifying the units the theory ranges over already relies on notions like *utterance* and *word* — "The big step is going from 'noise' to 'word.'" If picking out linguistic units presupposes linguistic categories, the theory may assume what it must explain.

**Explanatory emptiness** (Churchland): calling a trait innate without explaining its origin is not an explanation — "merely pronouncing them innate."

**Developmental.** Parameter-setting predicts switch-flips; children's competence emerges piecemeal and gradually. Generativists appeal to maturational or performance factors, which critics say are underspecified.

**Morphology and semantics.** Acquisition tracks input frequency closely — the pattern empiricist learning predicts. Nativists here fall back on constraints (whole-object bias, syntactic bootstrapping) rather than innate content.

**LLMs — the current front (2023–).** **Piantadosi (2023):** large language models undermine "virtually every strong claim for the innateness of language" — grammatical competence from exposure alone, no UG. **The standard rebuttal, noted in the same literature:** LLMs receive orders of magnitude more data than a child, so they don't touch the developmental claim (which is precisely about the poverty of *the child's* input). Generativist replies exist ("Why Linguistics Will Thrive in the 21st Century: A Reply to Piantadosi"). This is fast-moving and dates quickly.

**"Third-way" proposals** — statistical sensitivity and innate grammar interacting; the faculty as complementary storage and computation components; categories both innate and learned. Presented by their authors as dissolving the dichotomy rather than winning it.

## 8. Cast and neighbours

**Nativists:** Chomsky (on-site); Berwick, Piattelli-Palmarini, Fitch, Hauser; Pinker, Bloom, Jackendoff (adaptationist wing / parallel architecture); Fodor (radical concept nativism + LOT); Lasnik. **Nativist about cognition rather than language:** Spelke, Carey (core knowledge).

**Named critics:** Tomasello (on-site), Pullum, Scholz, Christiansen, Chater, Reali, Piantadosi, Churchland (Paul), P.S. Churchland (eliminativism, vs LOT), Deacon; Everett (on-site as external, recursion-not-universal); Goldberg, Langacker, Lakoff (construction/cognitive — already named on the Chomsky page); Prinz, Armstrong, Barsalou (imagistic/non-propositional, vs LOT — **Barsalou is already in the field map at Joint 2**); Pylyshyn appears *with* Fodor, not against.

**Bearing on the debate but outside nativism** — the accounts that make the endowment unnecessary by relocating the structure:
- **Deacon**, *The Symbolic Species* — co-evolution of language and brain via **niche construction**; the **reverse Baldwin effect** (genetic specification *decreasing* as the linguistic environment becomes reliable — offloaded onto the environment, masked rather than assimilated).
- **Christiansen & Chater** — **language evolved to be learnable**, rather than brains evolving to learn language; cultural evolution outruns biological, and can prevent biological adaptation to arbitrary linguistic features. The literature's own finding: the Baldwin effect works for *functional* but not *arbitrary* features, so genomic assimilation isn't freely available as a move.

**On-site figures already in the argument:** Skinner (the 1959 review), Piaget (the 1975 Royaumont debate on innateness vs developmental construction), Putnam, Dennett, Quine, Descartes (the rationalist lineage via *Cartesian Linguistics*), Saussure/Harris (the structuralist and distributional background Chomsky broke from).
