# Research: the standing of aesthetics in philosophy

Research document for the subject page "History of aesthetics in metaphysics." Two threads: the subordination arc (how aesthetics ended up at the bottom) and the counter-current (who kept pushing it back up). Not a survey of aesthetic theories but the story of aesthetics' *rank*.

---

## 1. The subordination arc

### Plato — the original demotion

Plato set the template. In *Republic* X, the arts are mimesis — imitation of imitation. The carpenter's bed imitates the Form of the Bed; the painter's bed imitates the carpenter's. Art is "thrice removed from truth." The poet and painter deal in appearances, not knowledge; they stir the irrational parts of the soul and weaken reason. Plato banishes the poets from the ideal city — not because art is trivial but because it is dangerously seductive: it has power but no access to truth.

This is not indifference. It is active distrust. The hierarchy is explicit: dialectic (philosophy) grasps the Forms; practical life (ethics, politics) applies them; art misleads. Sensory experience — the domain that will later be called "aesthetic" — sits at the bottom because it belongs to the realm of becoming, not being.

*Ion* and *Phaedrus* complicate the picture — the poet as divinely inspired, not merely imitating. But the net effect in the tradition is the demotion: philosophy deals in truth; art deals in shadows.

### Aristotle — partial rehabilitation, still subordinate

Aristotle rehabilitates art in the *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE). Poetry is "more philosophical and more serious than history" because it deals with universals, not particulars. Catharsis — the purging of pity and fear through tragic representation — gives art a cognitive and therapeutic function. Mimesis itself is rehabilitated: imitation is natural to humans and a source of learning.

But Aristotle's own classification keeps the hierarchy in place. The sciences divide into theoretical (metaphysics, mathematics, physics), practical (ethics, politics), and productive (poetics, rhetoric). The productive sciences — making things — rank beneath both knowing and doing. Art has value, but its value is instrumental: it produces something for an end beyond itself. The theoretical life remains the highest.

### The medieval period — absorption into theology

Aesthetics did not vanish during the medieval period, but it lost its identity as a philosophical domain. Beauty became a theological category — one of the transcendentals (alongside being, truth, goodness, and unity). Thomas Aquinas defines the beautiful as "that which pleases on being seen" (*id quod visum placet*) and identifies three conditions: integrity (*integritas*), proportion (*consonantia*), and clarity (*claritas*). But these are properties of being as such, studied under metaphysics and theology, not under a separate discipline.

The effect is paradoxical. Beauty ranks high — it is a transcendental, coextensive with being itself. But precisely because beauty is absorbed into the theory of being, there is no separate *aesthetic* inquiry. The question "what is beauty?" is a question about God and creation, not about art or sensory experience. Art itself is craft (*ars*), classified with the mechanical arts, beneath the liberal arts, beneath theology. The artisan makes; the philosopher knows; God creates.

Augustine's suspicion of sensory pleasure reinforces the subordination. Beautiful things can distract from God — they are valuable only insofar as they lead upward. The senses are a site of temptation, not a path to truth.

### The Renaissance and early modern gap — aesthetics before its name

The Renaissance elevates the visual arts and the artist (Leonardo, Michelangelo as geniuses, not craftsmen), but this happens in artistic culture and art theory, not in philosophy. Philosophical aesthetics does not yet exist as a discipline.

The early modern rationalists (Descartes, Leibniz, Spinoza) organise philosophy around epistemology and metaphysics. The senses are unreliable — clear and distinct ideas come from reason, not perception. Empiricists (Locke, Hume) take sensation seriously but as epistemology, not aesthetics. When Hume writes "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757), he is a philosopher writing about taste, not an aesthetician working within a recognised field.

The gap matters: by the time aesthetics gets a name in 1750, epistemology, logic, ethics, and metaphysics are centuries old as named disciplines. Aesthetics arrives last and has to justify its existence from the start.

### Baumgarten — founded late, seated low (1735/1750)

Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten coined the term "aesthetics" in his 1735 master's thesis (*Meditationes philosophicae de nonnullis ad poema pertinentibus*), defining it as *episteme aisthetike* — the science of what is sensed and imagined. His 1750 *Aesthetica* defined beauty as "the perfection of sensible cognition as such" (section 14) and explicitly designated aesthetics as *gnoseologia inferior* — lower gnoseology, the science of the lower cognitive faculties, structurally seated beneath logic.

The founding gesture is ambivalent. Baumgarten is elevating sensory cognition — giving it philosophical dignity, arguing that "the philosopher is a man among men" who should not exclude "such a major part of human cognition." He defended aesthetics against the charge that "sensorial input, imaginings, stories are unworthy of philosophers." But the very name he gives it — *lower* gnoseology — concedes the hierarchy. The discipline is born subordinate.

Baumgarten died in 1762 with his *Aesthetica* unfinished. As the SEP notes, he "remained more a Moses who glimpsed the new theory from the shores of Wolffianism than a Joshua who conquered the new aesthetic territory."

**Sources:** SEP, "18th Century German Aesthetics"; Baumgarten, *Aesthetica* (trans. Acevedo), sections 1, 6, 14.

### Kant — dignity without foundation (1790)

Kant's *Critique of Judgment* (1790) is the pivotal moment. It gives aesthetics full philosophical standing — an entire Critique, the third pillar of the critical system. But it positions aesthetics as a bridge, not a foundation.

The architecture is explicit: the first Critique grounds theoretical knowledge (nature, necessity); the second grounds practical reason (freedom, morality); the third bridges the "great gulf" between nature and freedom through the faculty of judgment. Aesthetic judgment — reflective, not determinative — finds "the universal for the given particular," operating through "free lawfulness" or "lawfulness without a law." It reveals the "subjective conditions of cognition in general."

What Kant gives aesthetics: autonomy (the judgment of taste is disinterested, purposive without purpose), universality (the claim to universal assent without concepts), and a systematic role (bridging nature and freedom). What he withholds: constitutive power. Aesthetic judgment does not determine objects; it does not produce knowledge; it does not ground morality. It is regulative — it shows that nature *as if* has purpose, preparing us for moral life without substituting for moral reasoning.

The result: aesthetics is philosophically dignified but structurally mediational. It sits between the serious business of science and ethics — never beneath them (as in Baumgarten), but never on the same footing either. As the SEP notes matter-of-factly, "the areas of aesthetics and natural teleology have traditionally been considered less philosophically central than those of ethics, metaphysics and epistemology" — a ranking partly produced by Kant's own architecture.

**Sources:** SEP, "Kant's Aesthetics and Teleology."

### Hegel — art's highest vocation belongs to the past

Hegel's *Lectures on Aesthetics* (delivered 1820s, published posthumously 1835) give art enormous philosophical weight — art is one of the three forms of Absolute Spirit, alongside religion and philosophy. But the three are ranked developmentally: art presents the Absolute in sensuous form; religion grasps it through representation and feeling; philosophy comprehends it in pure thought. Art comes first historically and logically, but it is the least adequate form.

The famous verdict: "Art, considered in its highest vocation, is and remains for us a thing of the past." This does not mean art stops being produced. It means art can no longer carry the highest spiritual content of an age — that task has passed to religion and then to philosophy. The sensuous medium is too limited for the complexity of modern spiritual life.

The hierarchy is explicit: sensuous presentation (art) < representational thinking (religion) < conceptual thinking (philosophy). Aesthetics is the philosophy of a medium that has already been surpassed.

### The analytic tradition — the soft corner (20th century)

In the analytic tradition, aesthetics became philosophy's least serious department. The marginalisation has several roots:

- **Logical positivism** treated aesthetic judgments as expressions of feeling, not meaningful propositions. If "this is beautiful" is neither analytically true nor empirically verifiable, it falls outside philosophy proper.
- **Institutional narrowing.** After 1945, aesthetics increasingly defined itself as "the philosophy of art" — a narrow definitional focus that, as Wiltsher (2017) argues, "estranged curious students and devalued expertise in aesthetics."
- **Departmental reality.** Only about 2% of faculty in top-10 U.S. philosophy departments specialise in aesthetics. Only 14 of the top 50 departments have aesthetics specialists at all (Ribeiro 2014). Aesthetics is not required in most graduate programmes.

Arthur Danto captured the standing: aesthetics ranks "about as low on the scale of philosophical undertakings as bugs are in the chain of being." Mary Devereaux confirmed: "Philosophers widely regard aesthetics as a marginal field."

The result is a discipline that even its own mainstream reference work (the SEP) describes as "traditionally considered less philosophically central" — a subordination so normalised it requires no argument.

**Sources:** Wiltsher, "Lonely Arts," *Metaphilosophy* 48:5 (2017); Ribeiro, *Aesthetics for Birds* (2014); Devereaux, ASA presidential address; SEP, "Kant's Aesthetics and Teleology."

## 2. The counter-current

### Schiller — aesthetic education as precondition for freedom (1795)

Friedrich Schiller's *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man* (1795) is the first explicit argument that aesthetic experience is not subordinate to ethics but its precondition. The French Revolution's collapse into Terror showed that moral ideals imposed on fragmented citizens produce barbarism, not freedom.

Schiller's argument: the human being is split between the sense drive (*Sinntrieb*) and the form drive (*Formtrieb*). Only the play drive (*Spieltrieb*), activated through beauty, reconciles the two. "It is only through beauty that man makes his way to freedom." And: "If man is ever to solve the problem of politics in practice he will have to approach it through the problem of the aesthetic."

This radically departs from Kant, who treats aesthetic experience as "propaedeutic" — preparing us for morality without substituting for it (Guyer 2014, p. 456). Schiller argues aesthetic experience can actually *constitute* moral transformation. Aesthetics is not a bridge to ethics; it is the ground ethics stands on.

**Sources:** Schiller, *Letters*, Letter 2 (Wilkinson/Willoughby trans., 1967, p. 90); Leontiev, *British Journal of Aesthetics* 63:4 (2023); Guyer, *A History of Modern Aesthetics*, Vol. 1, Cambridge UP (2014).

### Schelling and the Romantics — art as organ of philosophy (1800)

Friedrich Schelling's *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800) goes further than Schiller: art is "the only true and eternal organ and document of philosophy." Where philosophy can only represent the identity of the conscious and unconscious in concepts, art presents it directly in the artwork. Art is not an illustration of philosophical truth — it is the highest form of access to it.

The early German Romantics (Friedrich Schlegel, Novalis) similarly elevated poetry to the status of philosophy's equal or superior. Schlegel: "Poetry and philosophy should be made one." Novalis: "The world must be romanticised" — the ordinary raised to the dignity of the unknown, the finite made to appear infinite. This is not aestheticism (art for art's sake) but a metaphysical claim: the aesthetic mode of experience discloses what rational analysis cannot.

The Romantic elevation was short-lived as a philosophical programme — Hegel's system absorbed and subordinated it. But it planted the seed: if art can access what concepts cannot, aesthetics cannot simply be the junior partner.

### Croce — aesthetics as first form of knowledge (1902)

Benedetto Croce's *Estetica come scienza dell'espressione e linguistica generale* (1902) places aesthetics at the foundation of the Philosophy of Spirit. The four domains — aesthetic (intuition/beauty), logic (concepts/truth), economic (utility), ethical (the good) — form a hierarchy in which each presupposes the ones below. Aesthetics is the first.

"The intellect presupposes the intuitive mode — which just is the aesthetic — but the intuitive mode does not presuppose the intellect."

"All mental activity, which means the whole of reality, is founded on the aesthetic, which has no end or purpose of its own, and of course no concepts or judgements."

This is a radical reordering. Aesthetics is not a department of philosophy but its ground floor — the mode of cognition on which logic, practical activity, and ethics all depend. Croce further identifies aesthetics with general linguistics: "Philosophy of language and philosophy of art are the same thing." Expression — linguistic, artistic — is where knowledge begins.

**Sources:** SEP, "Croce's Aesthetics"; Encyclopedia.com entry on Croce.

### Dewey — aesthetic experience as complete experience (1934)

John Dewey's *Art as Experience* (1934) argues that aesthetic experience is not a special category set apart from ordinary life but ordinary experience at its most complete and integrated. When experience achieves unity — when means and ends, doing and undergoing, are fused — it becomes aesthetic. A well-conducted scientific inquiry, a satisfying conversation, or a craftsman's work can be aesthetic; a badly hung gallery visit is not.

The implication for standing: if aesthetic experience is the fullest form of experience, then it is not a subordinate branch but the measure against which all experience is judged. Moral deliberation depends on attentive, imaginative, fully engaged perception — capacities that are aesthetic in nature. Later scholars (Pappas, Johnson, Fesmire) have drawn the implication out: aesthetic capacities — the ability to perceive fully, to hold complexity, to feel the qualitative unity of a situation — are what moral reasoning actually requires.

### Collingwood — expression as foundation of conscious life (1938)

R.G. Collingwood's *The Principles of Art* (1938) develops Croce's expression theory with a crucial shift. For Collingwood, the artist begins not with a fully formed intuition but with "a vague perturbation" that clarifies through the act of expression. Expression is not communication (which assumes you already know what you want to say) but the process by which confused feeling becomes articulate awareness.

"Language and art become interchangeable" because both are the process of moving from inarticulate to articulate consciousness. When expression fails, consciousness itself becomes "corrupt" — not just aesthetically impoverished but fundamentally compromised. This makes the quality of expression a condition of conscious life, not an optional refinement.

### Heidegger — poetry and the truth of Being

Martin Heidegger's engagement with art and aesthetics runs through several works. In "The Origin of the Work of Art" (1935/36), art is not representation or expression but a "setting-to-work of truth" — truth (*aletheia*, unconcealment) happens in the artwork through the strife between "world" and "earth." Art is not subordinate to philosophy; it is an independent site where truth occurs.

"Language is the house of Being. In its home man dwells" (*Letter on Humanism*, 1947). "Poetically man dwells" (*"...Poetically Man Dwells..."*, 1951). Poetry is the paradigmatic form of language because it preserves language's capacity to disclose Being rather than merely representing entities.

Heidegger explicitly rejects "aesthetics" as a discipline — he sees it as a product of the subjectivisation of art that began with Descartes and was completed by Kant (reducing art to subjective experience). But his own position elevates what aesthetics is *about* — the sensuous, the linguistic, the disclosive — to the highest philosophical significance.

### Gadamer — art as truth against Kant (1960)

Hans-Georg Gadamer's *Truth and Method* (1960) opens with Part I: "The question of truth as it emerges in the experience of art." Art is the paradigmatic case for his hermeneutic philosophy — not an illustration but the entry point.

Gadamer directly opposes Kant's separation of aesthetic experience from truth and knowledge. "Gadamer maintains precisely what Kant denies — namely, that art is not separated from truth and knowledge but rather our experience of art as event discloses truth." The artwork is an *Ereignis* (event) with ontological significance: "A work of art belongs so closely to what it is related to that it enriches the being of that as if through a new event of being."

Gadamer's project is "simultaneously deconstructive and constructive" and "in many respects, strictly anti-Kantian." He dismantles elements of the Platonic (art as secondary to reality), Kantian (aesthetic experience divorced from knowledge), and Hegelian (art reduced to a vehicle for philosophical ideas) traditions that subordinated aesthetics.

**Sources:** SEP, "Gadamer's Aesthetics" (Nicholas Davey).

### Merleau-Ponty — the aesthetic dimension of perception

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) does not separate aesthetics from perception. Perception is already aesthetic — it involves the body's active engagement with the world, a "motor intentionality" that is neither purely sensory nor purely intellectual. The perceived world is not raw data processed by the mind but a meaningful field disclosed through bodily inhabitation.

His later work on painting (*"Eye and Mind"*, 1961; *"Cézanne's Doubt"*, 1945) develops the point: the painter does not represent what they see but makes visible what ordinary perception passes over. Art reveals the perceptual structures that underlie all experience. If perception is foundational to knowledge, and perception is inherently aesthetic, then aesthetics is not a branch of philosophy but a dimension of all philosophical inquiry.

### Murdoch — attention as moral foundation (1970)

Iris Murdoch argued in "The Idea of Perfection" (1962 lecture / 1964 publication, collected in *The Sovereignty of Good*, 1970) that moral agency consists in "a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality" — a concept of attention borrowed from Simone Weil. Moral failure is fundamentally a failure of seeing, not of choosing.

Her concept of "unselfing" (drawn from Weil's "decreation") holds that the ego must be silenced to perceive reality justly. Art trains this attention — it is one of the sites where "unselfing" can occur (the famous kestrel passage). Her Platonic framework holds that moral understanding is achieved "through knowing and loving the good in good particular things (including persons but also art, nature and ideas), then ascending to an understanding of Good itself."

Murdoch ultimately subordinates aesthetics to "the Good" — she is not anti-Platonic in the way Gadamer is anti-Kantian. But her argument that seeing precedes doing, and that art trains the capacity to see, places aesthetic experience at the foundation of ethical life.

**Sources:** SEP, "Iris Murdoch."

### Rancière — the distribution of the sensible (2000)

Jacques Rancière's *Le partage du sensible* (2000; English: *The Politics of Aesthetics*, 2004) redefines aesthetics as "the system of a priori forms determining what presents itself to sense experience" — not a branch of philosophy dealing with art but the foundational framework governing what is visible, sayable, and thinkable in a given political order. The "distribution of the sensible" defines "the mode of articulation between forms of action, production, perception, and art."

Aesthetics is "at the core of politics" because the distribution of the sensible determines who can speak, what counts as speech (versus noise), and what is perceptible at all — before any ethical or political claim is made. Politics disrupts the prevailing aesthetic order by making visible what was invisible, audible what was noise.

This is the most structural version of the counter-current: aesthetics is not a department that should rank higher but the condition that precedes all departments.

**Sources:** Rancière, *The Politics of Aesthetics*, pp. 13, 82; Vihalem, *Journal of Aesthetics and Culture* (2018).

## 3. The pattern

The subordination arc and the counter-current are not two separate stories — they are the same story seen from two angles. The subordination is never merely institutional; it is built into how philosophy classifies its own work. And the counter-current is never merely a claim that aesthetics deserves more respect — each figure makes a structural argument that the hierarchy itself is misconceived.

**What the subordination arc shows:**
- Plato establishes the template: truth above appearance, reason above sensation.
- The medieval period absorbs beauty into theology — high in rank but dissolved as a distinct inquiry.
- Baumgarten founds aesthetics as a named discipline but seats it explicitly beneath logic.
- Kant gives it dignity but keeps it regulative — a bridge, not a pillar.
- Hegel ranks it first historically but last philosophically — art's highest vocation belongs to the past.
- The analytic tradition inherits the hierarchy and hardens it into institutional fact.

**What the counter-current shows:**
- Schiller: aesthetic experience is the precondition for ethical and political freedom.
- Schelling/Romantics: art accesses what philosophy can only represent.
- Croce: aesthetics is the first form of knowledge; everything else presupposes it.
- Dewey: aesthetic experience is experience at its most complete.
- Collingwood: expression is the foundation of conscious life.
- Heidegger: poetry is where truth (unconcealment) happens.
- Gadamer: art discloses truth — directly opposing Kant.
- Merleau-Ponty: perception is already aesthetic; aesthetics is a dimension of all inquiry.
- Murdoch: attention (an aesthetic capacity) is the foundation of moral life.
- Rancière: the distribution of the sensible is prior to all politics and ethics.

Each counter-voice arrives from a different angle. But they converge on a single structural claim: **aesthetics is not a secondary department but a condition that the other branches of philosophy presuppose.** What varies is what exactly that condition is — perception (Merleau-Ponty), expression (Croce, Collingwood), attention (Murdoch), the sensible field (Rancière), disclosure (Heidegger, Gadamer), wholeness (Schiller, Dewey).

## 4. Gaps and notes

- **Adorno** (*Aesthetic Theory*, published posthumously 1970) could be added — art as the last refuge of non-identity thinking in a totally administered world. A counter-voice from critical theory.
- **Nietzsche** — *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) argues that art is not a luxury but the highest task and the truly metaphysical activity of life. "Only as an aesthetic phenomenon is existence and the world justified." A major counter-voice but complicated by Nietzsche's own later self-critique.
- **Non-Western traditions** are covered on the existing close-affinity page (Wen, Rasa, Japanese Ways, I'jaz) and do not need to be repeated here. They could be referenced: traditions that never made the split in the first place.
- **Hegel's "end of art" thesis** is widely cited but its precise meaning remains debated. Some scholars argue Hegel means art's romantic form is exhausted, not art as such. The research notes this as an open question.
