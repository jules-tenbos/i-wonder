# Notes: overlap, substrate, and the shared-base argument

Captured from conversation. Companion to the Whitehead plan. Useful for post exploration — feeds into the Stengers post, possibly a separate post on the shared-base argument itself, and the interrelational pluralism revisions.

## The starting point: Stengers' overlap

How does Stengers handle overlap between practices? She has several concepts, all part of one apparatus.

Reciprocal capture is the load-bearing one. When two practices meet and find a way to work together — each becoming intelligible to the other without dissolving into it — Stengers calls that reciprocal capture. The classic case is experimental science and the phenomena it constructs in the lab: scientist and setup capture each other, each makes the other what it is, neither precedes the encounter.

The ecology metaphor names the wider frame. Practices share environments, modify what's available to their neighbours, compete for resources, sometimes form mutualistic relations, sometimes drive each other extinct.

Cosmopolitics is the political-scale version of the same question. When many practices need to coexist in a shared world, what does it take to hold them together? Not a meta-framework that adjudicates, but the slow construction of practices that can each take the others seriously enough to be changed by them.

Where her apparatus reaches less directly: reciprocal capture is more a name for an outcome than a theory of how the outcome comes about. Once two practices have captured each other, here is what their relation looks like; the mechanism is left mostly to the empirical case. This is characteristic — pragmatist in temper rather than systematic.

## The contrasting picture: substrate-based, not encounter-based

Stengers' ecology is half-borrowed. She uses the word but mostly to describe encounter. The genealogical and substrate dimensions of real ecology — shared ancestry, co-evolution, cross-pollination through a common medium — don't get developed. The practice stays the unit; what practices share by virtue of being practices, or what medium lets them connect at all, isn't her question.

The alternative picture flips the priority. Practices aren't autonomous units that meet and might capture each other; they're differentiations within a shared field, descending from common ancestry, connected through the medium that lets them be practices at all. Overlap isn't an edge phenomenon — it's the structural condition. Cohesion isn't a result of successful encounter; it's the substrate from which encounter is possible.

The biology metaphor done properly carries this. Species don't meet as strangers; they meet as relatives with co-evolved environments and shared genetic toolkits. When they cross-pollinate, the productive part of the meeting is enabled by what they already share. Stengers' reciprocal capture describes the meeting of strangers who might end up productively related — the shared substrate is missing.

## Where this picks up SPLectrum

P0: differentiation produces being and language together. The differentiation is *from* something, not from nothing. The shared substrate is built in at the level of the foundational move.

P3: shared reality through shared language. The channel is named explicitly. "Shared reality channels" is what P3 articulates.

P5: complexity grows from history. Practices carry their ancestry forward; what is shared is what has been built across history.

The "shared reality channels" idea points at language as medium — the substrate Stengers doesn't theorize because her unit-of-analysis question never gets pressed below the practice.

## The claim: diversity requires sufficient common base to be fruitful

Diversity that doesn't share enough substrate to be intelligible isn't diversity in any meaningful sense — it's isolation. Two practices that share absolutely nothing don't experience each other as different; they don't experience each other at all.

Davidson made a related argument against the very idea of a fully incommensurable conceptual scheme: to recognize another scheme *as* a scheme, you have to share enough to interpret it. Schemes that fail this test aren't alternative schemes — they're not schemes at all. Wittgenstein's "if a lion could speak, we could not understand him" gets at the same thing from the other side: shared form-of-life is the substrate, not an addition.

The shared-base argument pushes further than recognition: substrate is what makes diversity *fruitful*, not just recognizable. Without common base, there's nothing to cross-pollinate through.

This clarifies what P4 is doing. Equal standing among languages is sometimes read as "languages exist in isolation, none privileged" — a pluralism of mutual indifference. The claim makes the relational part load-bearing: equal standing is between languages that share enough substrate to stand in relation at all. The "interrelational" in interrelational pluralism isn't a modifier on pluralism — it's the condition for the pluralism to do any work.

## Cooperation as mutualism — fungi and plants

The model for "cooperation as a source of common base" is biological mutualism, specifically the mycorrhizal relationship between fungi and plants. Most plants live in association with fungal networks: the fungi extract minerals and water from the soil and exchange them for sugars the plants produce through photosynthesis. The relationship is around 400 million years old — old enough that most plants cannot live without it.

The mycorrhizal network — sometimes called the "wood-wide web" — connects plants to each other through the fungal substrate. Different plants, even different species, share nutrients and signals through this network. Plants are not autonomous units in the soil; they are nodes in a fungal exchange network.

What this models for the practice case: mutualism is cooperation that becomes ancestry. Fungi and plants have been cooperating for so long that their cooperation is part of their evolutionary history. The two sources of common base — shared ancestry and cooperation — aren't separate categories; they're a continuum. Mutualism is the mechanism by which cooperation builds substrate, and given enough time, substrate becomes ancestry.

Practical implication: shared reality channels are not only what's inherited but what's grown through sustained mutualistic relating. Two practices with no shared lineage can become fruitfully diverse to each other if they cooperate long enough to build substrate. The picture isn't fatalistic — the common base isn't only given, it's also built. And the kind of relating that builds substrate is structurally different from the kind that exploits or extracts.

The fungi-plants example anchors the connection to the substrate argument and may strengthen the mutualism material on the site.
