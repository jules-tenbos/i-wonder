# Teaming Up With My Alter Ego

Collaboration and the idea of an alter ego were very much on my mind well before AI entered the picture. I have written a number of posts on this blog before, but never got the writer fluids flowing freely. Partly because I am not a natural writer as far as long texts are concerned, and partly because my thinking lacked a focus of expression — now fully resolved and condensed into the Splectrum seed.

My journey into AI is about one year old. As a software engineer it started as a helping hand for writing code. But soon I got focused on a collaborative approach where solving problems through discussion became more important than the execution itself. I found the terminal approach of Claude Code a natural fit for my chat style, and I do identify with the vision that Anthropic puts into its AI endeavours. I am still very active exploring collaborative AI on the engineering side — in fact that is where the name Splectrum arose — but in this post I want to concentrate on AI collaboration when researching, writing and thinking.

It is only when enough maturity was reached while doing the software engineering that I decided to apply it to my research and writing. I have been blessed in that I was able to do AI-assisted research for academic use, which gave me a taste and hands-on experience of the strengths and weaknesses of AI in that field. And I was impressed. Many tend to emphasise the weaknesses that exist in AI, they are often mistaken in their approach. It is like blaming a junior member of the team for being a junior. Whatever the claims are of AGI and AI taking over humanity and the world, these are typically made by people that underestimate the complexities that our brain seamlessly deals with. The story of self-driving vehicles illustrates this underestimation very clearly.

On the engineering side the alter ego approach started as pair programming. Two individuals who collaborate on a project — discuss, decide, create and review. And what I found liberating in this interaction was that I could chat conversationally in my language, my words, and pretty seamlessly AI would run with it and infuse me with the proper vocabulary in the process. It was such a satisfying learning experience. As typically happens in pair programming, one has the hands on the keyboard while the other engages from a short distance. And I saw a hands and head division. The coding activity was done with AI being the hands, mainly, and me being the head, mainly — but essentially one body. And so alter ego was born.

So how does that translate into shared authorship for my blog, or for my research and thinking for that matter? Here the output is different — it is a public voice. Not a piece of code that executes and does stuff. There is still only one author, a single entity, a combination of a head and a pair of hands, so to speak. I am also a person with a high vagal tone and I am emotionally closely involved in what I do — I really live it. That makes tone of voice very important to me. It needs to be right for my thinking to activate properly. So any division of labour between head and hands is only going to work when they speak the same language. How do we deal with the inner voice when we chat and discuss, and with the outer voice when it is put out there for you to read?

I could decide to ignore co-authorship. The voice is mine and the ideas are mine — should I acknowledge that inner collaborator? I believe I should, not only to recognise the work done by my alter ego colleague but more importantly to draw attention to the future ahead. The hands-on collaborator gives me more space and time to do what I really want to do: think, analyse, research and get better at putting it on paper. Where the internet gave us the capability to easily search countless libraries of stuff, AI is giving me the capability to talk about it, to discuss and think. That, more than anything else, is what I with my alter ego want to achieve on this blog. And share it at the same time.
